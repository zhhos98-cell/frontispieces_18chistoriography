# Colour as method — first controlled sweep, 2026-08-27

## 1. Reframing

The colour branch should not be opened as a general history of colour in eighteenth-century historiography. The current corpus already contains a more precise problem:

> **How is difference made operational through colour, and under what conditions can chromatic information be encoded, decoded, standardized, reproduced, compared, abstracted, or rejected as unreliable?**

The word `colour` currently covers several epistemically different operations. The extension ontology in `schema/COLOUR_ONTOLOGY.md` therefore separates object colour, semantic categories, monochrome encoding, decoder keys, hand-applied pigment, relational colour syntax and classificatory colour.

## 2. The existing corpus already contains a technical genealogy of colour transmission

### A. Siebmacher 1605: description can instruct later colouring

The extended title/declaration of the 1605 armorial states colour information so that a purchaser can have the arms illuminated. The important relation is therefore not simply image versus text but:

`printed structural image + colour information`
→ `later material colouring of an impression`.

Colour is both semantic information and a possible post-print production operation.

### B. Siebmacher 1609 / German heraldic practice: colour is compressed into letters

Later letter notation embeds colour categories within the monochrome engraved image. This increases portability and compresses information but requires code knowledge and sufficient graphic space.

### C. Spener 1680: a primary statement of channel limits

Spener explicitly contrasts German colour initials with a French dot/line hatching system. More important than the chronology of techniques is his stated fallback: where the figure is too small for the hatch code to discriminate colours adequately, the reader must return to the printed explanation.

This is already a method statement:

`graphic encoding`
→ constrained by `scale/resolution`
→ backed by `verbal redundancy`.

### D. Köhler / Weigel: explanatory loss itself becomes historically visible

The 1747 *Bibliotheca Rinckiana* synopsis of Köhler's 1734 preface says he regretted the disappearance of a German explanation of shield colours from the post-Siebmacher lineage, while the 1734 Weigel edition still failed to restore it.

The project should therefore distinguish the history of the matrix/image from the history of its explanatory channels. A stable picture can become methodologically poorer if its decoder disappears.

### E. Wappenkalender: the `Erklärung der Farben` as a centralized monochrome decoder

The directly controlled 1744 Wappenkalender presents a full-page engraved `Erklärung der Farben` immediately before armorial plates using the same hatch vocabulary; a later alphabetical section verbalizes the colours again.

The page links:

`hatch sign ↔ heraldic tincture ↔ French/German name`.

The significant feature is that it is a **colour instrument without applied colour**. It makes chromatic categories portable in monochrome print. It is therefore analytically distinct from the painted/printed sample-based colour charts that dominate current colour-chart historiography.

The annual series also exposes copy-level dependency: a 1729 copy is described as missing the colour-key leaf while retaining other armorial material. Thus:

`armorial corpus materially present`
≠ `internal decoding apparatus complete`.

### F. 1772 retrospective: code failure becomes an explicit publication problem

The Weigel/Raspe retrospective reports that older letter-based tincture notation generated disorder and misunderstanding, in a publication regime already under pressure from small images, huge numbers of arms, limited descriptive space and historical change in the arms themselves.

This gives an actor-level diagnosis:

`compression`
→ `ambiguity / misunderstanding`
→ demand for a different information architecture.

The history of colour coding is therefore not a teleology from primitive letters to superior hatching. It is a history of trade-offs among space, clarity, reproducibility, explanation and copy usability.

## 3. Gatterer's `Synopsis`: colour changes epistemic role

The `Synopsis historiae universalis` (1766; second edition 1769) creates a different colour problem. Its title already states that two tables are copper-engraved and `coloribusque illustratae`, but surviving copies diverge: some relevant tables are coloured, some uncoloured, and the Tübingen witness reportedly colours one table but not its partner.

André de Melo Araújo's 2015 analysis is direct prior art here. He argues that the tables answer the problem of representing historical simultaneity through a new scientific visual semantics. He further shows that pigment can highlight boundaries and major historical `revolutions`, while line geometry carries the underlying durations and relations.

Two consequences follow.

First, **the cognitive role of colour in Gatterer's tables is already historiographical prior art**. The project cannot claim to discover that coloured tables matter epistemically.

Second, the material copy divergence is methodologically productive. Colour may sharpen overview while remaining non-invariant across impressions. The line system is therefore more edition-stable than pigment, while pigment may reduce the reader's work of visual discrimination.

This creates a tension:

`copy invariance / reproducibility`
versus
`immediate visual discrimination`.

That tension is more useful than a generic statement about colourful historical tables.

## 4. Colour as relational syntax

The `Synopsis` also marks a categorical break with heraldic tincture representation. In a coat of arms, red or blue refers to a semantic colour category belonging to the represented object. In a synchronistic historical table, pigment can instead stand for a political or temporal relation whose referent is not literally coloured.

Thus:

`pigment colour`
→ `assigned historical relation`
→ `visual overview`.

This is `colour-as-relational-syntax`: colour becomes a notation for historical structure.

The useful question is no longer whether colour faithfully reproduces an original object, but whether an assigned chromatic distinction makes a historical relation more quickly inspectable.

## 5. Colour as classifier in Gatterer's geography / Völkerkunde

A third Gattererian regime appears in the 1775 *Abriß der Geographie*. Modern scholarship reconstructs Gatterer's fourth main geographical division, Menschen- und Völkerkunde, as beginning with a `Geography of human bodies, by shape and colour`, followed by languages, religions, products, culture and other classificatory dimensions.

This must remain separate from both heraldic coding and the `Synopsis`.

Here `Farbe` is neither a hatch-code referent nor an arbitrary diagram colour. It is treated as a feature of human bodies available for geographical/ethnographic differentiation.

This gives a potentially important cross-domain problem inside Göttingen historical sciences:

- heraldry: colour as rule-governed semantic category that can be **translated into signs**;
- synchronistic history: colour as **assigned graphical relation**;
- Menschen-/Völkerkunde: colour as **observed/classified difference**.

At present, no source establishes that Gatterer himself theorized these as one transferable colour method. The commonality is an analytical hypothesis to test, not a demonstrated actor category.

## 6. Historiography boundary I: colour charts and colour order are a newly consolidated field

Giulia Simonini's *Color Charts in 18th-century Europe: Natural, Pigmentary, Trichromatic* (2025), DOI `10.11588/arthistoricum.1483`, is the most important new boundary. The book presents the first integrated overview of eighteenth-century colour charts across natural history, artisanal practice and trichromatic systems, building on the DFG project `Die Ordnung der Farben`.

The literature establishes that eighteenth-century colour reference systems addressed problems of:

- terminology;
- standardisation;
- comparison;
- recipe/material practice;
- communication among scholars and practitioners;
- numerical/systematic ordering.

Simonini's natural and pigmentary charts rely centrally on actual colour samples and their relation to names, recipes or specimens. Her examples show why a physical sample beside a name can stabilize comparison across time and users.

### Historiographical opening

The Wappenkalender `Erklärung der Farben` is different enough to require its own category. It is a **monochrome chromatic decoder**: it supplies no sample of red, blue or green, but encodes those categories through reproducible black-line patterns and multilingual labels.

It should not simply be relabelled a `color chart`. The stronger move is to compare two solutions to the same general problem of chromatic reference:

`actual colour sample ↔ name`
versus
`monochrome sign ↔ colour category ↔ name`.

The second solves a print-economic and reproducibility problem by removing pigment from the carrier.

## 7. Historiography boundary II: scientific hand-colouring already has a method history

Kärin Nickelsen, “The Challenge of Colour: Eighteenth-Century Botanists and the Hand-Colouring of Illustrations,” *Annals of Science* 63.1 (2006): 3–23, DOI `10.1080/00033790500151177`, reconstructs hand-colouring as a supervised scientific practice and identifies standardization, recipes, reproducibility, evaluation and correction as methodological problems.

This removes several easy novelty claims. The project should not claim that:

- hand-colouring introduced scientific error problems;
- colour recipes/names lacked standardization;
- coloured copies could diverge;
- scholars developed reference systems to control colour reproduction.

Those are established in the history of natural history illustration.

The project-specific question is how similar problems appear in **historical and auxiliary-historical media**, especially when colour can be eliminated into hatch codes or redeployed as abstract historical syntax.

## 8. Historiography boundary III: Göttingen itself already has a colour-science history

Barry B. Lee's “Colour science in Göttingen in the 18th Century” (2001) places Tobias Mayer, Erxleben and Lichtenberg inside an active Göttingen history of colour science and early trichromatic thinking. Simonini likewise treats Tobias Mayer as a major eighteenth-century colour-order figure.

This is important context rather than a direct influence chain. It establishes that Gatterer's Göttingen was not institutionally colour-neutral. Mathematical/natural-philosophical projects of ordering colour coexisted with historical, geographical and auxiliary-science practices.

No direct transfer from Mayer/Erxleben/Lichtenberg into Gatterer's historical graphics has yet been established. A future influence claim requires citation, institutional interaction, teaching evidence, shared instruments or explicit vocabulary.

## 9. The stronger gap after the first sweep

The project should avoid three broad claims:

1. `colour was cognitively important in Gatterer's Synopsis` — Araújo already demonstrates this;
2. `eighteenth-century scholars sought to standardize colour` — a substantial colour-order literature already exists;
3. `hand-colouring caused methodological problems` — Nickelsen and related work already establish this.

A stronger gap is:

> **the history of colour translation regimes inside historical method: how chromatic difference moved between word, letter, hatch, legend, pigment, table syntax and classificatory description, and how each translation redistributed reproducibility, inspectability, fidelity and error.**

The Nürnberg–Göttingen archive is unusually strong because it contains several regimes in a connected material and institutional environment rather than one isolated colour system.

An even tighter formulation is:

> **Colour is not the content of this project. Colour is a family of techniques for making difference operational.**

## 10. Connection to the Gatterer–Schlözer dispute

The existing 1772–1775 dispute map remains valid. Gatterer and Schlözer competed over teaching, priority and the architecture of Universalgeschichte. Both confronted selection, synchronism and the problem of keeping a historical whole available to students/readers.

Colour now offers a discriminator within the third, medial economy of that dispute:

- what operations can prose perform?
- what operations require table, map or diagram?
- what does pigment add to a line-based table?
- is colour load-bearing information or a redundant aid to overview?
- does a method depend on an element that is not invariant across copies?

The present evidence does **not** yet show that the 1773 polemic explicitly debated colour. The correct formulation is therefore:

> colour is a stress test for competing architectures of historical knowledge, not yet a demonstrated topic of the polemic itself.

## 11. Immediate source programme

The next pass should privilege discriminating primary checks rather than broad discovery.

1. **Köhler 1734 preface** — recover the literal passage behind the 1747 `explicatio germanica colorum` synopsis; test whether colour explanation is framed as clarity, usability, fidelity, pedagogy or something else.
2. **Wappenkalender colour-key matrix** — compare 1728/1732/1737/1738/1744/1751/1755/1757 impressions for same physical matrix/state; this can produce a long-duration decoder history separate from the frontispiece.
3. **Legend-to-arm test** — verify a small set of stable tinctures against the same hatch grammar in multiple annuals.
4. **1772 retrospective** — isolate the exact wording linking letter-coded tinctures to `Unordnung und Mißverstand` and the stated remedy.
5. **Gatterer `Synopsis`** — encode the known copy census and distinguish line-borne information from pigment-borne information; compare coloured and uncoloured impressions from the same matrix.
6. **Gatterer `Abriß der Heraldik`** — directly control his discussion of Tinkturen/Schraffierungen and any historical justification from old armorial practice.
7. **Gatterer `Abriß der Geographie` 1775** — inspect the primary contents/method passage for `Gestalt und Farbe`; establish exactly how colour enters Menschen- und Völkerkunde.
8. **Gatterer–Schlözer 1772/73** — search both programme and polemical texts for `Farbe`, `color`, `Tafel`, `Gemälde`, `Plan`, `Karte`, `Übersicht`, `Zeichnung`, `illumin*`; only primary hits can promote a direct colour-method controversy claim.
9. **Göttingen cross-domain control** — test explicit contact between Gatterer's historical instruments and contemporary colour-order projects associated with Tobias Mayer and the Göttingen scientific milieu.

## 12. Stop rule for this branch

Do not run a general bibliography of colour symbolism, aesthetics, pigments or Goethean colour theory unless a source bears directly on one of the operations in `schema/COLOUR_ONTOLOGY.md`.

Prioritize materials that answer one of four method questions:

- How is colour information translated?
- What relation does colour make easier to inspect?
- What does the chosen colour regime make reproducible or non-reproducible?
- Where does colour fail as a stable discriminator?
