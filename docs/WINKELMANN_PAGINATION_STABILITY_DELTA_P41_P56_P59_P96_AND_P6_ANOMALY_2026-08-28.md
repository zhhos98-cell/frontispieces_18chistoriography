# Winkelmann pre-print pagination delta: stable p.41 / p.56 / p.59 / p.96 and the anomalous p.6

Date: 2026-08-28
Status: HIGH-VALUE DELTA — PARTIAL PAGINATION CONTINUITY WITH THE 1697 PRINT TRADITION IS NOW STRONGLY INDICATED; OPENING-PAGE DIVERGENCE REMAINS TO BE DIRECTLY COLLATION-CONTROLLED

## Result

The previous checkpoint established that the page locators in Leibniz's `Ex W. Hassicae Chron.` note are autograph source references rather than modern editorial additions.

A closer reading of the Leibniz-Katalog's long source note now sharpens the result.

Leibniz's autograph note contains, among others, the explicit locators:

- `p. 6`
- `p. 41`
- `p. 56`
- `p. 59`
- `p. 96`

The catalogue editors, when describing the later Winkelmann print tradition, independently list pages:

`S. 10, 11, 12, 40, 41, 54, 55, 56, 59, 69, 96 ...`

as places containing passages that correspond to or help control Leibniz's notes.

Therefore at least four autograph locators have exact-number counterparts in the later printed source environment:

`41 → 41`

`56 → 56`

`59 → 59`

`96 → 96`.

This is too structured to treat the early page references as arbitrary note numbering.

The strongest current model is **substantial pagination continuity between the pre-print textual state and the 1697 publication**.

However, the opening locator `p. 6` is anomalous: the editors' comparison note highlights printed pp. 10–12 near the corresponding early material and does not list p. 6 among the matched/helpful pages.

This creates a discriminating hypothesis:

> the pre-print state may already have carried a pagination scheme largely preserved in print, while the opening gathering or introductory section was reorganized before publication.

This remains an inference until the 1697 first-edition pages are directly inspected.

---

## 1. Why the matching numbers matter

The source note is not one isolated `p.` reference. It moves through a long historical extraction and repeatedly records source coordinates.

Four later numbers — 41, 56, 59 and 96 — recur in the editors' print-collation note exactly.

This strongly favors:

`stable source pagination carried across textual states`

over

`later editors silently supplied print locators`.

The latter is already ruled against by field separation in the catalogue: the locators occur in `Wortlaut`, whereas the printed-source comparison belongs to `Längere Bemerkungen`.

---

## 2. The p.6 problem is now analytically useful

The first locator is:

`Ex W. Hassicae Chron. p. 6.`

Yet the editors' later-print comparison points to pages 10, 11, 12 and then 40ff., not to p. 6.

Possible explanations, ranked provisionally:

### Model A — opening-gathering revision before print

The pre-print text had a page 6 passage that moved to ca. pp. 10–12 when preliminary or introductory matter was expanded/reordered.

This would allow later pagination to converge again by p. 40/41.

### Model B — different pagination layer

`p. 6` may refer to an internal part/chapter pagination or an excerpt-copy pagination, while later locators reflect another layer.

This is less economical if all references belong to one source packet, but it remains possible.

### Model C — editors did not exhaustively collate p. 6

The catalogue wording says the listed print pages `passen bzw. helfen` and `u. viell. öfter`; it is explicitly non-exhaustive. The omission of printed p. 6 may therefore reflect incomplete collation rather than divergence.

This guardrail is essential. The p.6 anomaly is suggestive, not yet proof of rearrangement.

---

## 3. Continuous pagination in the 1697 first edition matters

Independent HAB bibliographical description identifies the Bremen 1697 first edition as five published parts with **continuous pagination**; the sixth part was announced but appeared only posthumously in 1754.

Thus printed page 41, 56, 59 and 96 belong to one continuous first-edition pagination system rather than separate part-local page systems.

This makes exact numerical continuity with Leibniz's pre-print locators more significant.

Source control:
HAB digital scholarly edition bibliographical apparatus, entry `Winkelmann` for *Gründliche und Warhaffte Beschreibung ...*, Bremen 1697.

## 4. Revised source-state model

The best current reconstruction is:

`Winkelmann written Hessian Chronicle / evolving manuscript`
→ `paginated pre-print state or paginated derivative excerpt source`
→ `Leibniz excerpt reservoir with source locators`
→ `routing across Geographica and problem files`
→ `1697 print, preserving substantial portions of the earlier pagination`

with a possible local revision near the opening pages.

This is stronger than the generic statement `Leibniz had excerpts before publication`.

It indicates that the pre-publication material had enough internal stabilization to support **addressable retrieval**.

## 5. Broader conceptual consequence: addressability precedes publication

The important operation is not simply manuscript circulation.

The evidence now suggests:

`pre-print text`
→ `stable coordinates`
→ `extractable propositions`
→ `cross-file references`
→ `later printed stabilization`.

Historical material became retrievable and transportable before final bibliographical publication.

This gives the repo a concrete early-modern instance of **addressability before print finality**.

That concept may be more useful than describing the source merely as an unpublished manuscript.
