# `Verlagskupfer` as reusable plate stock — *Die Grösse und Mannigfaltigkeit* (1766)

## Result

The 1766 preface to *Die Grösse und Mannigfaltigkeit in den Reichen der Natur und Sitten* supplies unusually explicit primary evidence for an operational assumption used throughout this repository: engraved copperplates could remain in a publisher's shop as reusable capital, outlive an earlier textual host, and be assigned a new textual programme because re-engraving comparable plates would be commercially irrational.

This is stronger than inferring reuse from similar images. The publisher's own paratext defends the practice.

## Stable primary carrier

- Internet Archive / Getty Research Institute: `diegrosseundmann00will`
- item: https://archive.org/details/diegrosseundmann00will
- OCR/full text: https://archive.org/stream/diegrosseundmann00will/diegrosseundmann00will_djvu.txt
- publication date: 1766
- IA notes: 100 half-page emblematic copper engravings; the plates had previously been used for Weigel's *Ethica naturalis* and Abraham a Sancta Clara's *Huy und Pfuy der Welt* and were here issued with new text.
- important copy caveat: the Getty copy appears to lack the 1766 letterpress title page. IA reports Landwehr's attribution of the edition to Johann David Tyroff; that publisher attribution is therefore catalogue-level for this copy until a complete title-page witness is inspected.

## Primary wording and operations

The preface first makes the criticism explicit: detractors of the publisher had alleged that the coppers had long since been engraved, had been present **`in der Officin des Verlegers`**, and had already been used in another way. The answer is not to deny the allegation. Instead, the preface argues that it is no deception to supply those valuable old coppers with a completely newly worked text rather than leave them unused.

It then states the economic logic still more plainly. The low selling price and good paper should itself show that newly engraved plates could not have been paid for. The decisive sentence is:

> `Die Kupfer waren also schon da;`

The same paragraph links the plates to Christoph Weigel and names two earlier textual hosts: Abraham a Sancta Clara's *Huy und Pfuy der Welt* and an *Ethica Naturalis* edition with five Latin distichs beneath each copper.

The publisher's action is then described as giving the matter a completely different form and extracting better use from **`diesen feinen Verlagskupfern`**.

Because the current control is machine OCR of a primary scan, these phrases are secure enough for analytical use but any diplomatic quotation in publication should be checked against the scan image.

## What this establishes

### 1. Plate stock is explicitly separated from textual host

The sequence is actor-supported rather than inferred:

`engraved plate stock already exists in the publishing office`
→ `earlier textual programme(s)`
→ `commercial/textual obsolescence of those programmes`
→ `same plate stock assigned newly composed explanations`
→ `new audience and use claims`.

The unit that persists is therefore not the book or the text but the copperplate stock.

### 2. Reuse is defended as publishing economy

The preface makes production cost part of the explanation. Comparable large, fine copperplates could not have been newly engraved at the work's selling price. Reuse is presented as rational exploitation of sunk material capital.

This gives the repository a period actor's category for what elsewhere must be reconstructed from plate states, imprints and host changes: **publisher-held reusable copper stock**.

### 3. Retextualisation changes function without requiring a new image programme

The old German prose of Abraham a Sancta Clara is described as no longer matching contemporary taste; the Latin-distich version is described as too inaccessible and too brief. The plates are consequently retained while explanation, language, audience and pedagogical claims are changed.

The 1766 preface then says that painters and other artists can copy the coppers, that the descriptions can support new designs, and that the material can be used in schools or private instruction. The same visual stock is therefore not merely reprinted; it is **re-authored as a new didactic and interpretive apparatus**.

## Why it matters for the Clio / Wappenkalender problem

This source does **not** prove that the Clio plate followed the same custody path. It does establish, inside the Weigel/Tyroff publishing ecology, that long-lived copper stock could:

- remain physically available in a publisher's office;
- migrate between distinct textual hosts;
- be commercially reactivated after earlier editions lost appeal;
- acquire new explanatory and pedagogical functions;
- be described by the publisher as `Verlagskupfer`.

That materially strengthens the plausibility of the repository's host-regime model. Plate history, text history and publication history can diverge not only because surviving copies happen to do so, but because eighteenth-century publishers deliberately operated in that way.

## Attribution problem to keep open

There is a useful tension between the preface and modern metadata. The preface says `Christoph Weigels vortrefliche Hand lieferte sie`, while IA/Getty metadata associates the plates with Jan and Caspar Luiken. Until the earlier hosts and signatures are compared directly, do not collapse `liefern`, ownership/publishing, design and engraving into a single production role.

The right next visual control is therefore small and bounded: choose several numbered emblems, compare their line work, dimensions, captions/numbering and plate wear across *Huy und Pfuy*, *Ethica Naturalis* and the 1766 host, and inspect one complete 1766 title-page witness for the Tyroff imprint.
