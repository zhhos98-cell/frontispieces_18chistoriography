# `Verlagskupfer` as reusable plate stock — Weigel → Tyroff, ca. 1700–1766

## Result

The 1766 preface to *Die Grösse und Mannigfaltigkeit in den Reichen der Natur und Sitten* supplies unusually explicit primary evidence for an operational assumption used throughout this repository: engraved copperplates could remain in a publisher's shop as reusable capital, outlive earlier textual hosts, and be assigned a new textual programme because re-engraving comparable plates would be commercially irrational.

A second control now makes this substantially stronger. The reuse can be placed inside the same Weigel/Tyroff corporate succession that matters for the Wappenkalender:

`Christoph Weigel the Elder (1654–1725), publisher of Ethica Naturalis, ca. 1700`
→ `same image corpus reused in Huy und Pfuy der Welt, 1707`
→ `plates survive in the publishing office`
→ `Johann David Tyroff, explicitly styled heir of the late elder Christoph Weigel, rehosts the corpus in 1766`.

This is therefore not just a generic eighteenth-century analogy. It is actor-level evidence that long-lived copper stock could cross generations inside the Nuremberg Weigel/Tyroff publishing ecology.

## Stable controls

### 1766 primary carrier

- Internet Archive / Getty Research Institute: `diegrosseundmann00will`
- item: https://archive.org/details/diegrosseundmann00will
- OCR/full text: https://archive.org/stream/diegrosseundmann00will/diegrosseundmann00will_djvu.txt
- publication date: 1766
- IA notes: 100 half-page emblematic copper engravings; the plates had previously been used for Weigel's *Ethica naturalis* and Abraham a Sancta Clara's *Huy und Pfuy der Welt* and were here issued with new text.
- copy caveat: the Getty copy appears to lack the 1766 letterpress title page.

### Complete-copy / catalogue control for the 1766 imprint

A surviving-copy description tied to `VD18 14823888-001` gives the title/imprint as:

`Die Grösse und Mannigfaltigkeit ... herausgegben von J. D. Tyroff der ältern Christoph Weigels seel. Erb. Nürnberg 1766.`

It reports a copper title, 100 text coppers and 103 leaves. The Rijksmuseum library independently catalogues the work as `Nürnberg: J.D. Tyroff, 1766` and notes that the Jan and Caspar Luyken engravings first appeared in *Ethica Naturalis* around 1700.

The literal phrase `der ältern Christoph Weigels seel. Erb.` is particularly important: the 1766 publisher identifies himself through inheritance from **the elder Christoph Weigel**, not merely through a generic Weigel name.

## Primary wording and operations

The 1766 preface first makes the criticism explicit: detractors of the publisher had alleged that the coppers had long since been engraved, had been present **`in der Officin des Verlegers`**, and had already been used in another way. The answer is not to deny the allegation. Instead, the preface argues that it is no deception to supply those valuable old coppers with a completely newly worked text rather than leave them unused.

It then states the economic logic still more plainly. The low selling price and good paper should itself show that newly engraved plates could not have been paid for. The decisive sentence is:

> `Die Kupfer waren also schon da;`

The same paragraph links the plates to Christoph Weigel and names two earlier textual hosts: Abraham a Sancta Clara's *Huy und Pfuy der Welt* and an *Ethica Naturalis* edition with five Latin distichs beneath each copper.

The publisher's action is then described as giving the matter a completely different form and extracting better use from **`diesen feinen Verlagskupfern`**.

Because the current control of the preface is machine OCR of a primary scan, these phrases are secure enough for analytical use; any diplomatic quotation in publication should still be checked against the scan image.

## Earlier host 1: *Ethica Naturalis*, ca. 1700

Authority control matters because the two Weigel brothers are routinely confused in catalogue metadata.

The strongest current controls identify the publisher as **Christoph Weigel the Elder (1654–1725)**:

- Metropolitan Museum: *Ethica Naturalis seu Documenta Moralia*, ca. 1700; publisher Christoph Weigel, Redwitz 1654–1725 Nuremberg; designers Jan Luyken and Caspar Luyken.
- Deutsche Nationalbibliothek authority record for Christoph Weigel (1654–1725) explicitly lists him as brother of Johann Christoph Weigel (1661–1726), notes that his widow Magdalena continued the business to 1748 and the heirs to 1790, and connects his authority to the later *Die Grösse und Mannigfaltigkeit* record.
- Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek / Deutsches Museum authority material explicitly lists *Ethica naturalis seu documenta moralia*, ca. 1700, under Christoph Weigel (1654–1725).
- Folger catalog likewise assigns the undated *Ethica naturalis* to Weigel, Christoph, 1654–1725.

Some digitised-library metadata elsewhere maps the title to Johann Christoph Weigel (1661–1726). Given the institutional object records and authority controls above, the current working identity is **elder Christoph Weigel with high confidence**. Preserve the conflicting metadata as an authority-control warning rather than merging the brothers.

### Production roles

Do not translate the 1766 preface's `Christoph Weigels vortrefliche Hand lieferte sie` into a literal engraving attribution. Modern object/catalogue evidence separates roles more carefully:

- Christoph Weigel the Elder: publisher / supplier / holder of the series;
- Jan Luyken and Caspar Luyken: designers and, in object-level records, printmakers/engravers for individual plates.

The relevant custody claim concerns publisher-held copper stock, not sole artistic authorship by Weigel.

## Earlier host 2: *Huy und Pfuy der Welt*, 1707

The LEO-BW/HAB record gives the 1707 title explicitly as:

`Mit Kupfern geziert und verlegt Durch Christoph Weigel/ Kupferstechern und Kunsthändlern in Nürnberg`.

The record reports 100 copperplate leaves. The 1766 preface itself states that Abraham a Sancta Clara supplied German prose for these already-existing images. Later scholarship identifies Bauer, cols. 924–928, as the detailed reconstruction of the contribution structure and the relation between *Ethica Naturalis* and *Huy und Pfuy*.

Thus the plate corpus already demonstrates retextualisation within elder Weigel's lifetime before its later inheritance by Tyroff.

## What this establishes

### 1. Plate stock is explicitly separated from textual host

The sequence is actor-supported rather than inferred:

`engraved plate stock in elder Weigel's publishing programme`
→ `Ethica Naturalis: Latin moral distichs`
→ `Huy und Pfuy: new German prose programme, 1707`
→ `plates remain available in publisher stock across succession`
→ `J. D. Tyroff, heir of elder Weigel, 1766`
→ `new physical/moral explanations, new audience and pedagogical claims`.

The unit that persists is therefore neither author nor text nor book title. It is the copperplate corpus under changing textual and corporate hosts.

### 2. Reuse is defended as publishing economy

The preface makes production cost part of the explanation. Comparable large, fine copperplates could not have been newly engraved at the work's selling price. Reuse is presented as rational exploitation of sunk material capital.

This gives the repository a period actor's category for what elsewhere must be reconstructed from plate states, imprints and host changes: **publisher-held reusable copper stock**.

### 3. Retextualisation changes function without requiring a new image programme

The old German prose of Abraham a Sancta Clara is described as no longer matching contemporary taste; the Latin-distich version is described as too inaccessible and too brief. The plates are consequently retained while explanation, language, audience and pedagogical claims are changed.

The 1766 preface then says that painters and other artists can copy the coppers, that the descriptions can support new designs, and that the material can be used in schools or private instruction. The same visual stock is therefore not merely reprinted; it is **re-authored as a new didactic and interpretive apparatus**.

### 4. Corporate succession and plate custody can coincide

The new 1766 imprint control matters because Tyroff does not appear as an unrelated later publisher. He is named as heir of the late elder Christoph Weigel. Combined with the primary statement that the old coppers were already in the publisher's office, the evidence supports a concrete mechanism for material persistence:

`publisher stock → inheritance/succession → new host publication`.

That mechanism should now be treated as a strong comparator when testing the Clio plate's survival across Weigel widow/heirs, Tyroff and later formats.

## Why it matters for the Clio / Wappenkalender problem

This source does **not** prove that the Clio plate followed the identical custody path. It establishes inside the relevant firm ecology that long-lived copper stock could:

- remain physically available in a publisher's office;
- migrate between distinct textual hosts;
- survive the death of the original publisher;
- pass into an heir's publishing programme;
- be commercially reactivated after earlier editions lost appeal;
- acquire new explanatory and pedagogical functions;
- be described by the publisher as `Verlagskupfer`.

That materially strengthens the repository's host-regime model. Plate history, text history, agent history and publication history can diverge because publishers deliberately managed old copper matrices as inheritable assets.
