## Result

The reverse sweep from `Geographica` sharpens the previous `Insignia → Geographica` finding.

The official Leibniz-Katalog currently returns **53 records** for the normalized ordering mark `Geographica`.

Within the rendered 53-result corpus, direct searches for:

- `Insignia`;
- `Genealogica`;
- `Wappen`;
- `sigill-`

do not currently produce a reciprocal hit.

This is analytically important.

The June 1690 `Insignia` record ID 7139, which says Winkelmann material `liegen bey Geograf.`, should **not** be treated as evidence that `Insignia` and `Geographica` were simply merged or symmetrically co-labelled files.

The current evidence instead supports an **asymmetric sorting relation**:

`Insignia problem`
→ redirects selected material to `Geographica`.

At the same time, the `Geographica` corpus itself is much broader than topography or map description. It includes:

- peoples and ethnonyms;
- migrations;
- linguistic/ethnic identifications;
- historical territorial names;
- pagi, counties and jurisdictional boundaries;
- documentary/diplomatic evidence for spatial extent;
- historical chronicles used to reconstruct regional identity;
- cross-labels with other research domains such as `Linguae`, `Witikindea`, and `Slavi`.

The strongest model is therefore **sorting jurisdiction**, not a fixed modern disciplinary ontology.

Working relation:

`historical source material`
→ routed according to the immediate problem
→ `Insignia`, `Geographica`, `Linguae`, etc.
→ later classificatory compression stabilizes some of these mobile relations.

This is historical-spatial adjudication rather than static cartography.

## 4. Diplomas can belong to `Geographica`

Two records make the evidentiary reach especially clear.

### ID 67533

Leibniz notes the extent of `Pagus Lagragan` and adds that Schrader possesses a diploma from which this can be seen:

`H. Schrader hat ein diploma, darauf solches zu sehen`.

Filed:

`Geographica`.

### ID 63471

A long documentary excerpt reconstructs jurisdictional/territorial boundaries through counties, pagi, rivers, roads and named places; the document ends by invoking the imperial seal.

Filed:

`Geographica`.

The implication is important for the larger repository argument.

A diploma or seal is not intrinsically assigned to one auxiliary science. Its classificatory destination depends on the **question being asked of it**.

The same media can support:

- `Insignia` when testing heraldic chronology or status;
- `Geographica` when testing territorial extent or historical place identity.

Working principle:

`media identity ≠ disciplinary jurisdiction`.

That principle closely parallels the repository's existing material rule:

`plate identity ≠ text identity ≠ host identity`.

## 7. Sorting jurisdiction rather than subject essence

The emerging archive rule is:

> A source or object does not possess one fixed disciplinary identity; Leibniz routes it according to the evidentiary operation it is performing.

Examples:

`seal`
→ `Insignia` when used to date or test an armorial claim;

`diploma`
→ `Geographica` when used to establish the extent of a pagus;

`Winkelmann`
→ `Insignia` for dynastic titles and arms;

`Winkelmann`
→ `Geographica` for Hessian regional/historical material;

`Winkelmann`
→ `Linguae | Geographica` for ethnonymic/linguistic identity.

This is better described as **jurisdiction by question** than classification by object type.

That formulation may matter later for Gatterer, where the institutional issue is likewise which domain has jurisdiction over shared evidentiary resources.

## 9. Consequence for the frontispiece argument

This reverse sweep strengthens a broader proposition already developing in the repository:

`discipline labels`
are not best understood as containers with fixed evidence types.

They are claims of **jurisdiction over operations and witnesses**.

That matters for the 1726 visual constitution:

`HISTORIA / GENEALOGIA / HERALDICA / TESTES TEMPORUM ET VERITATIS`.

The plate visually separates personified fields, but the earlier Leibniz archive shows that their evidentiary materials and research operations could be routed across boundaries.

The historical problem becomes:

> How are mobile witnesses converted into apparently stable disciplinary persons/categories?

This is a stronger bridge from the Leibniz material to the frontispiece than a conventional “rise of auxiliary sciences” genealogy.
