# Tyroff–Weigel family-firm continuity and stock custody control, 1729–1780s — 26 August 2026

## Institutional control

The British Museum authority records supply an unusually useful firm-level control for interpreting the recurrent frontispiece repertoire without collapsing firm continuity into plate identity.

For **Martin Tyroff (1705–1759)**, the British Museum records him as an engraver and publisher in Nuremberg, states that he married a daughter of Christoph Weigel in **1729**, and says that he **ran the family firm until his death**. The same authority record associates Tyroff with the Museum's 1740 *Geschichts-, Geschlechts- und Wappen-Calender* object `1910,0906.2`, made by Tyroff after Gabriel Müller and published by the Heirs of Christoph Weigel.

For **Heirs of Christoph Weigel**, the British Museum states that after Christoph Weigel's death the firm was continued by his widow together with his daughter Barbara Sibylla and her second husband Martin Tyroff; after the widow's death Tyroff continued the firm using the name `Christoph Weigels Erben`; after Tyroff's death in 1759 his sons continued under a successor form of the old Weigel name; and in the **1780s the stock was divided and sold to other dealers**.

Sources:

- British Museum, Martin Tyroff authority record, `BIOG49071`.
- British Museum, Heirs of Christoph Weigel authority record, `BIOG148663`.
- British Museum object `1910,0906.2`, 1740 Wappenkalender / frontispiece context.

## What this controls

This gives an external institutional basis for treating **family-firm continuity and custody of commercial stock as a real historical mechanism** across the Weigel→Tyroff period. It is therefore directly relevant to the frontispiece sequence now controlled at composition level:

`Type A → Type B → Type C → later Type A/C recurrence`.

The recurrence need not be explained as purely iconographic memory. A family publishing/engraving concern existed across the relevant decades, and the Museum explicitly describes a stock that remained a transferable commercial asset until its later division and sale.

The chronology is particularly suggestive because Tyroff enters the Weigel family/firm in 1729, while the first directly controlled Müller–Tyroff Type B witnesses belong to the later 1730s/1740s; Type C then appears by 1747 and remains active into the 1750s, when Tyroff was still alive and active as engraver/publisher. The 1759 editorial/publishing transition therefore occurs in the same year as Tyroff's death, although no causal claim should be made from coincidence alone.

## Evidentiary guardrail

Firm continuity is **not** same-matrix proof. The British Museum authority records support:

`family-firm continuity → plausible custody/reuse environment for plates and impressions`.

They do **not** establish:

`same firm → same copper matrix`,

nor do they establish that a specific Type A plate survived physically from the 1720s into the 1759/1765 recurrences.

Matrix-level claims still require direct comparison of dimensions, signatures, frame, line structure, micro-defects, retouching and wear. The firm-history evidence should be used to explain why long-lived or reactivated plate stock is historically plausible, not as a substitute for object comparison.

## Analytical consequence

The frontispiece repertoire can now be modelled at three distinct but interacting levels:

1. **programmatic continuity** — the recurring `HISTORIA / GENEALOGIA / HERALDICA / TESTES TEMPORUM ET VERITATIS` verbal threshold;
2. **graphic repertoire** — Types A/B/C can replace and later recur;
3. **commercial custody** — a continuous family engraving/publishing concern provides a documented institutional environment in which plates, designs and impressions could be retained, selected, remade or transferred.

The third level materially strengthens the repertoire/stock hypothesis while preserving the distinction between commercial custody and physical matrix identity.
