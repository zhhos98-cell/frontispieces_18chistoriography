# Wappenkalender colour key as redundant information architecture — 1728–1757 control

## Status

The annual Weigel/Köhler *Geschichts-, Geschlechts- und Wappen-Calender* did not merely encode tinctures inside individual heraldic images. Direct inspection of the 1744 ULB Düsseldorf witness shows a **central engraved `Erklärung der Farben`**, followed immediately by heraldic plates using the same hatching conventions, while a later alphabetical section gives a **verbal description of all arms** and repeatedly spells out colours (`Gold`, `roth`, `schwarz`, `blau`, `silbern`, etc.).

A physical-copy catalogue independently records a full-page engraved `Erklärung der Farben` in copies for **1728, 1732, 1737, 1738, 1744, 1751, 1755 and 1757**. A 1729 copy is explicitly incomplete and is described as lacking the expected colour-explanation plate. Another 1745 physical-copy description identifies the colour explanation as being represented `durch Schraffierung`.

The safest current conclusion is therefore:

> **By the late 1720s and through the end of the Köhler/Weigel annual sequence, the colour-key copper functioned as a recurrent central decoder within a multi-channel heraldic apparatus. In the directly inspected 1744 witness, engraved hatching, a centralized legend, and prose blazon coexist rather than replace one another.**

Matrix identity of the colour-key plate across years remains unproved. Recurrent module identity does not yet equal same-copper continuity.

## 1. Direct 1744 primary control: the colour key is a graphic decoder

ULB Düsseldorf's 1744 digitisation (`... zum zwey und zwanzigstenmahl ... 1744`) indexes `Erklärung der Farben` as a distinct content unit immediately before the main armorial section.

Direct image inspection shows a full-page engraved legend headed **`Erklärung der Farben`**. It presents shield-shaped samples with French and German names and distinct graphic fills:

- `d'Or / Gold` — dotted field;
- `d'Argent / Silber` — blank field;
- `de Gueules / Roth` — vertical lines;
- `d'Azur / Blau` — horizontal lines;
- `de Sable / Schwartz` — crossed/meshed line field;
- `de Sinople / Grün` — diagonal lines;
- `de Vairs / Eisenhüte` — vair pattern;
- `de Hermines / Hermelin` — ermine pattern;
- `de Pourpre / Purpur` — opposite/variant diagonal hatching;
- additional lower examples labelled `de Fer / Eisen` and `Naturel / Naturfarbe` with their own fills.

This is not a decorative colour chart. It is a **legend for converting monochrome line-patterns back into heraldically meaningful tincture categories**.

The multilingual labels are also significant. The same shield is positioned between conventional graphic code and lexical equivalence in French/German. The page therefore mediates among:

`visual hatch pattern ↔ heraldic colour category ↔ multilingual colour name`.

## 2. The next armorial plate visibly uses the code

The page immediately following the 1744 colour key begins the heraldic section with `Kayserliches Wappen`. Direct inspection shows systematic use of dots, vertical strokes, diagonal strokes and cross-hatching across the shield, supporters and heraldic fields.

This closes the basic functional relation at copy level:

`central colour legend`
→ `same graphic vocabulary deployed in actual armorial images`.

The present pass does not require assigning every tiny field of the imperial arms to a tincture. The key empirical point is that the legend's coding vocabulary is visibly instantiated in the immediately following heraldic apparatus.

## 3. The book later verbalizes the arms again

ULB's structured contents separately index printed p. 87:

`Kurtze und deutliche Beschreibung aller hierinnen vorkommenden Wappen / Nach dem Alphabet.`

Direct inspection of its opening page shows dense verbal blazons that repeatedly name tinctures and metals: `Gold`, `roth`, `schwartz`, `blau`, `silbern` and related forms. The prose therefore re-expresses information already carried by the images and their centralized legend.

This makes the 1744 system **redundant by design** at the level of information channels:

`armorial image with hatching`
+
`centralized explanation of hatching`
+
`alphabetical prose description`
→
`multiple routes to the tincture information`.

The prose is more than a caption to one adjacent shield. It forms an independently navigable alphabetical descriptive layer.

## 4. A fourth channel: genealogical tables

The same 1744 volume also contains a register of **125 Stammtafeln**, and physical-copy descriptions of the annual series repeatedly describe armorial engravings and corresponding genealogical tables as paired components.

Thus the annual reference architecture is better represented as:

`central legend`
+
`heraldic image`
+
`verbal armorial description`
+
`genealogical table / dynastic context`.

Each channel answers a different question:

- legend: **how to decode the graphic marks**;
- shield image: **what the arms look like structurally**;
- prose: **what tinctures/charges the arms contain in words**;
- Stammtafel: **where the bearer/dynasty sits genealogically**.

This is considerably more precise than calling the calendar `illustrated`.

## 5. The colour-key module recurs across the annual series

Holger Christoph & Co., auction catalogue 37 (2014), gives unusually detailed physical-copy collations for a run of Weigel Wappenkalender. It records:

| Host year | Colour-key evidence | Armorial apparatus |
|---|---|---|
| 1728 | `ganzseit. gest. “Erklärung der Farben”` | 89 full-page armorial coppers, verso text |
| 1732 | full-page engraved `Erklärung der Farben` | 89 armorial coppers, verso text |
| 1737 | full-page engraved `Erklärung der Farben` | 94 armorial coppers, verso text |
| 1738 | full-page engraved `Erklärung der Farben` | 94 armorial coppers, verso text |
| 1744 | full-page engraved `Erklärung der Farben` | 97 of 100 armorial coppers in the described copy |
| 1751 | full-page engraved `Erklärung der Farben` | 101 of ca.109 armorial coppers in an incomplete copy |
| 1755 | full-page engraved `Erklärung der Farben` | 109 armorial coppers |
| 1757 | full-page engraved `Erklärung der Farben` | 110 armorial coppers |

The same catalogue's 1728 and 1732 descriptions also explicitly say the armorial coppers have text on the verso. The later ULB 1744 object allows the broader textual architecture to be inspected directly.

This cross-year recurrence is strong evidence for **module persistence**. It is not yet evidence that one physical colour-key copperplate survived unchanged from 1728 to 1757.

## 6. 1729 copy loss exposes dependency on one central decoder

A separately described 1729 physical copy is incomplete. Its seller lists among the missing components:

`1 gest. Tafel "Erklärung der Farben"`

along with two missing armorial engravings and other leaves.

This is analytically valuable. The armorial plates can survive while the centralized decoding page is lost. That creates a copy whose heraldic images remain materially present but whose easiest internal decoding route has disappeared.

The relation is:

`many encoded images`
→ depend in part on
`one centralized legend leaf`.

The colour key is therefore a **single point of interpretive concentration**. Its loss need not make the entire book unintelligible, because prose blazon and external heraldic knowledge may compensate. But copy-level usability changes even when most armorial matrices/impressions remain present.

This gives a new material-state distinction:

`armorial corpus complete`
≠
`decoding apparatus complete`.

## 7. 1745 description confirms hatching as the legend's subject

A physical 1745 copy currently described by Booklooker states that the volume begins with an engraving explaining the colours **`durch Schraffierung dargestellt`**.

This wording independently supports what the 1744 ULB image directly shows: the `Erklärung der Farben` is specifically a key to heraldic hatching, not merely a coloured sample plate or generic list of colour names.

Use the 1744 image as the primary technical control; use the 1745 seller description only as a copy-specific corroboration.

## 8. Relation to Spener 1680

Spener's preface had already articulated the same general information problem. He says his plates use dots and differently directed lines for tinctures rather than the German habit of initial letters, but if figures are too small for those lines to distinguish colours sufficiently, the reader must return to the printed explanation.

The 1744 calendar realizes a closely comparable architecture at book scale:

`hatching on images`
+
`central legend`
+
`verbal descriptions`.

Do not infer direct influence from Spener without a Köhler/Weigel citation. The safe comparison is functional:

> **Both systems treat graphic tincture code and verbal explanation as complementary channels, with textual redundancy protecting against the limits of a compressed visual code.**

## 9. Relation to Köhler's 1734 complaint about lost colour explanation

The 1747 *Bibliotheca Rinckiana* synopsis reports that Köhler's 1734 preface regretted the disappearance of the German colour-explanation layer from post-Siebmacher editions and noted that the 1734 Weigel armorial still lacked it.

The recurrent annual colour-key plate therefore becomes especially interesting. The Wappenkalender appears to possess precisely what Köhler judged missing in the large armorial lineage: an explicit explanatory apparatus for tincture representation.

A stronger hypothesis can now be formulated:

> **The Wappenkalender centralized tincture decoding into one reusable graphic legend while retaining prose descriptions elsewhere, thereby reducing the need to repeat colour explanations beside every engraved shield without abandoning textual redundancy.**

This is well supported functionally in 1744. The historical claim that Köhler consciously designed the annual legend *as a remedy* for the large armorial's lost German colour explanation still requires his own programme text.

## 10. Why this matters for seriality

The colour-key plate is a small object with a large serial function. The number and identity of armorials can change from year to year while the decoding grammar can remain stable.

That permits an efficient publication architecture:

`stable code / legend`
+
`variable annual armorial corpus`
→
`new content can be inserted without re-explaining the graphic language each time`.

This is one way serial publication can separate **stable infrastructure** from **changing data**.

The same distinction appears elsewhere in the project:

- recurring frontispiece programme vs changing composition/matrix;
- stable genealogical table format vs changing dynastic content;
- recurring publication rhythm vs annual additions/corrections;
- stable legend vs changing armorial inventory.

The colour key is therefore not peripheral to the project's argument. It is a compact instance of serial information architecture.

## 11. Data-model consequence

Add or preserve separate copy/witness properties for:

- `tincture_encoding_system` — hatching / letters / pigment / mixed;
- `colour_key_present`;
- `colour_key_missing_copy_loss`;
- `colour_key_state_or_matrix` — only after image-level comparison;
- `verbal_armorial_description_present`;
- `armorial_plate_text_relation` — verso / separate section / cross-reference;
- `copy_colour_finish` — monochrome / partial / contemporary colour / later colour suspected.

Do not encode all of this as one generic `colour` field.

A useful relation is:

`decoded_by`

linking a family of armorial images to the centralized `Erklärung der Farben` witness in the same copy.

## Evidence guards

1. **1744 ULB Düsseldorf is the strongest direct primary control**: legend image, following armorial image and alphabetical prose description have all been directly inspected in one identified digital copy.
2. The 1728–1757 recurrence list comes from detailed physical-copy catalogue descriptions. It establishes copy-level presence, not same-matrix identity.
3. A seller's phrase `durch Schraffierung dargestellt` for 1745 is corroborative, not a substitute for image inspection.
4. The 1729 missing legend is a copy-loss state. Do not infer that the 1729 edition was issued without the colour key.
5. The prose-description layer is directly verified for 1744; do not automatically assume identical page architecture in every annual without checking contents/collation.
6. The central legend reduces repeated explanation but does not replace prose in the 1744 witness. The correct model is redundancy, not substitution.
7. No conclusion about the physical copperplate's continuity across 1728–1757 is licensed until lettering, shield outlines, spacing, defects, wear and retouching are compared across actual legend impressions.

## Next discriminators

1. **Same-legend test:** retrieve `Erklärung der Farben` images from 1728/1732/1737/1738/1744/1751/1755/1757 and compare lettering, shield contour, spacing, plate dimensions, scratches and wear. Classify `same_matrix_as`, `same_design_new_matrix`, or unresolved.
2. **Legend-to-arm test across years:** choose Gold, Roth, Blau, Schwarz and Grün fields in one named recurring arm and verify whether the actual plate hatching consistently matches the legend in at least three annuals.
3. **Text redundancy chronology:** check whether the alphabetical `Beschreibung aller ... Wappen` is already present in 1728/1732 or enters later. This will show whether centralized graphic legend and prose description were co-original or layered over time.
4. **1725 boundary:** if the 1725 colour-key copper can be routed, inspect it before the still-unresolved frontispiece. Its coding system may provide an independent continuity marker even while the Clio title copper remains unknown.
5. **Post-1757 succession:** test whether Gatterer's 1759 Handbuch retains the same legend design/copper and whether later Seyfart/Raspe cumulative Wappenbücher keep, replace or textualize the decoder.
