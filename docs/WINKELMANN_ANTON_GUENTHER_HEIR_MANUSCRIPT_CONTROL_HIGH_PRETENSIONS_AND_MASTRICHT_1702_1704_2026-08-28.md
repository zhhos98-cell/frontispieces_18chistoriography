# Anton Günther Winkelmann, 1702–1704: high claims, inherited manuscripts, and controlled access to the father's papers

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: DECISIVE HEIR / MANUSCRIPT-CUSTODY CHECKPOINT — LEIBNIZ DIRECTLY REPORTS THE SON'S `HAUTES PRÉTENSIONS` IN JANUARY 1702; GERHARD VON MASTRICHT IDENTIFIES THE SON IN 1704 AS ANTON GÜNTHER AND STATES THAT VARIOUS MANUSCRIPTS OF POTENTIAL HISTORICAL VALUE ARE IN HIS POSSESSION; DIRECT IDENTITY BETWEEN THOSE MANUSCRIPTS AND THE MISSING HESSIAN SIXTH BOOK REMAINS UNPROVED

## Result

The previously generic posthumous category `Heretiers` can now be partially resolved into a named manuscript-custody actor: **Anton Günther Winkelmann**, son of Johann Just Winkelmann.

Three contemporary evidence nodes now align.

### 24 November 1701 — Staff

Nathanael von Staff reports that after Johann Just Winkelmann's death:

`les Heretiers ont declaré que ce livre etoit achevé et ont demandé une somme assez grande pour le remettre`.

The missing sixth book / second tome was therefore claimed complete by the heirs, but its transfer/delivery was conditioned on a substantial payment.

### 14 January 1702 — Leibniz

Leibniz tells Staff that he possesses only:

`quelque commencement de la chronique de feu Mr Winkelmann`

which Winkelmann himself had once brought to Hannover. The critical editors date this event to **summer 1697**.

Leibniz then adds:

`Son fils est venu un jour à Hannover, il parloit de Mr son pere, mais avec de si hautes prétensions que je jugeois bien qu’on le negligeroit de cette maniere.`

The critical edition identifies the son as `A. G. Winkelmann`.

### 9 November 1704 — Gerhard von Mastricht

Mastricht writes from Bremen:

`Innotuit mihi interea apud D. Winkelmannum filium esse varia Manuscripta quae credo historiam Brunsvicensem et Luneburgicam illustrare possunt.`

He suggests Leibniz contact the son and ask whether anything remains in the father's papers or now in his own possession that could assist Leibniz's historical work:

`peteresque si quid in forulis Dni Parentis esset aut nunc in suis, quod conatus Tuos juvare posset, ut hoc communicaret.`

Mastricht then promises:

`Studebo tunc, ut ei extorqueam quae nunc apud eum latent.`

The Akademie-Ausgabe editors explicitly identify `Winkelmannum filium` as **Anton Günther**, son of historiographer Johann Justus Winkelmann.

Source:

- Leibniz, *Sämtliche Schriften und Briefe*, Reihe I, Bd.20, no.420, Leibniz to Nathanael von Staff, Berlin, 14 Jan.1702, pp.729–732; partial concept `LBr.888 Bl.3–4` plus first print Kortholt 1738;
- Gerhard von Mastricht to Leibniz, Bremen, 9 Nov.1704, *Sämtliche Schriften und Briefe*, Reihe I, Bd.24, no.58, manuscript `LBr.614 Bl.3–4`.

## 1. Leibniz did not possess a complete Chronicle in January 1702

Staff's 21 December 1701 letter again offers to send Leibniz a copy of the printed Chronicle, saying:

`J’ay veu par votre derniere que vous n’avez pas encor veu la Cronique de Winkelman, si vous en avez envie je ne manqueray pas de vous en envoyer un exemplaire`.

In his 14 January 1702 answer, Leibniz clarifies his own holdings:

`J’ai quelque commencement de la chronique de feu Mr Winkelmann, qu’il avoit porté lui même à Hannover autrefois.`

The editorial note dates `autrefois` to **summer 1697**, with cross-reference to I,14 no.473.

Leibniz plans:

`Quand j’y serois de retour ... je verrai jusqu’où il va, pour apprendre si la votre va plus loin.`

Thus the object in Leibniz's possession was sufficiently partial that he needed to compare how far it extended against Staff's copy.

Safe status:

`LEIBNIZ HOLDING = SOME BEGINNING / PARTIAL CHRONICLE WITNESS`.

The exact material state remains open:

- manuscript;
- corrected manuscript copy;
- partial print/proof sheets;
- printed beginning of the 1697 volume;
- presentation material combining print/manuscript elements.

Do not translate `quelque commencement` into any one of those states without direct inspection or the 1697 correspondence.

## 2. Winkelmann himself brought the partial witness to Hannover in 1697

The critical editors specifically align Leibniz's `autrefois` with **summer 1697**.

This is close to the first-edition printing year and therefore potentially important for reconstructing pre-publication circulation.

Directly controlled:

`WINKELMANN HIMSELF`
→ `BRINGS A CHRONICLE BEGINNING / PARTIAL WITNESS TO HANNOVER`
→ `LEIBNIZ RETAINS IT INTO 1702`.

Open:

- date in summer 1697;
- whether the object preceded or followed completion of the first printed tome;
- whether Leibniz annotated it;
- whether it corresponds to any surviving Leibniz excerpts or printed sheets;
- whether the December 1697 Winkelmann–Leibniz correspondence discusses the visit/object.

This is now a priority object-history question.

## 3. `Son fils ... de si hautes prétensions`

Leibniz reports that Winkelmann's son came to Hannover and spoke about his father:

`avec de si hautes prétensions`

that Leibniz judged:

`qu’on le negligeroit de cette maniere`.

The noun `prétensions` is deliberately not narrowed to money alone. It can denote claims, demands, expectations, or conditions.

However, Staff's independent November 1701 report says the heirs demanded:

`une somme assez grande`

for transfer/delivery of the completed sixth book.

Therefore the two sources support a strong but carefully bounded convergence:

`STAFF: HEIRS + LARGE SUM`

alongside

`LEIBNIZ: SON + VERY HIGH CLAIMS / DEMANDS`.

Safe proposition:

> High posthumous demands by the Winkelmann family were independently visible to both a Kassel court insider and Leibniz and were understood as obstructing continuation or uptake.

Still unresolved:

- whether Anton Günther personally set the precise price Staff heard;
- whether other heirs participated;
- whether the high `prétensions` concerned only the sixth book or the father's papers/estate more broadly;
- whether they included office/patronage expectations in addition to money.

## 4. Anton Günther is directly identified as manuscript custodian in 1704

Gerhard von Mastricht's 9 November 1704 letter is the decisive prosopographical bridge.

He knows that:

`apud D. Winkelmannum filium esse varia Manuscripta`

and believes those manuscripts may illuminate Brunswick-Lüneburg history.

The critical editors identify the son as:

**Anton Günther Winkelmann**.

Secondary biographical control gives him as **1673–1718**, later a ducal Brunswick councillor and Amtmann. Mastricht's own letter places him in the service of either Leibniz's Elector or the Duke of Celle:

`Est ille in Seren. Tui Electoris aut Ducis Zellensis Ministerio`.

Therefore the manuscript custodian was himself embedded in the Brunswick-Lüneburg governmental environment rather than remaining solely inside a Bremen family household.

Safe model:

`FAMILY ESTATE CUSTODY`
+
`BRUNSWICK-LÜNEBURG OFFICE NETWORK`
→ `POTENTIAL ACCESS NODE FOR LEIBNIZ`.

The exact office in 1704 remains to be independently controlled.

## 5. Mastricht's language reveals active access negotiation

Mastricht does not merely report manuscript existence.

He proposes a two-stage extraction strategy:

1. Leibniz should write politely to Anton Günther, asking whether anything in the father's papers (`forulis Dni Parentis`) or his own current holdings could aid Leibniz and requesting communication;
2. Mastricht would then try:

`ut ei extorqueam quae nunc apud eum latent`.

Literally, he would try to **wrest/extract from him what is now hidden with him**.

The rhetorical force of `extorqueam` and `latent` shows that access was not assumed to be frictionless.

This is important for the repository's larger history of source mobility:

`MANUSCRIPT EXISTS`
≠ `MANUSCRIPT IS CATALOGUED`
≠ `MANUSCRIPT IS COMMUNICATED`
≠ `MANUSCRIPT ENTERS AN EDITORIAL PROJECT`.

Indeed, the I/24 editors conclude that Mastricht's suggestion of potentially useful manuscripts in Winkelmann's estate appears to have found **no resonance** in Leibniz's *Scriptores* project.

Thus existence and scholarly relevance did not guarantee transfer.

## 6. Relation to the missing sixth book remains open but is now sharply testable

Mastricht speaks of `varia Manuscripta` useful for Brunswick-Lüneburg history, not explicitly of the Hessian sixth book.

Therefore:

`ANTON GÜNTHER POSSESSED FATHER'S MANUSCRIPTS = CONTROLLED`

but

`ANTON GÜNTHER POSSESSED THE SPECIFIC HESSIAN SIXTH BOOK IN NOV.1704 = NOT YET DIRECTLY CONTROLLED`.

Nevertheless the chronological/prosopographical conjunction is highly significant:

- 1701 heirs claim completed sixth book and demand substantial sum;
- 1702 the son comes to Hannover with very high claims;
- 1704 Anton Günther is directly known to possess multiple manuscripts from his father's papers.

The next archival question is no longer generic:

`where did Winkelmann's manuscripts go?`

It is:

`which manuscripts passed into Anton Günther's custody, and does the 1701 claimed-complete sixth book appear among them?`

## 8. Relation to the 1754 sixth-part publication

The major unresolved transmission problem is now:

`ANTON GÜNTHER / FAMILY PAPERS c.1701–1704`
→ `?`
→ `KASSEL 1754 SIXTH-PART PRINT`.

The 1754 edition is not simply a late publication date. It represents a successful recovery/re-entry of sixth-part material after a period in which:

- Kassel had not seen the second tome;
- the heirs demanded a substantial sum;
- court interest was low;
- the son controlled manuscript materials;
- scholarly attempts to access family manuscripts could fail to generate transfer.

The editorial/provenance history of 1754 is now therefore a direct manuscript-transmission problem.

## 10. Repository-level payoff

The archive/source model now needs an explicit custody layer:

`authorial manuscript identity`
≠ `estate inventory identity`
≠ `heir custody identity`
≠ `access permission identity`
≠ `scholarly communication identity`
≠ `editorial uptake identity`
≠ `later publication identity`.

The Winkelmann case shows that source survival and source use are different historical processes. A manuscript could survive in a learned official's hands and still fail to enter a major historical edition because access, price, family claims, court interest, and scholarly coordination did not align.
