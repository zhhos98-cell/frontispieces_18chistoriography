# Winckelmann 1684: Oldenburg Horn object access, engraved-surrogate error, and reuse chain

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: HIGH-VALUE OBJECT/SURROGATE CHECKPOINT — DIRECT OBJECT ACCESS, SOURCE-CRITICAL JUDGEMENT FROM MATERIAL FEATURES, ENGRAVED REPRESENTATION, REPORTED REPRESENTATIONAL ERROR, AND LATER IMAGE REUSE ARE ALL CONTROLLED AT DIFFERENT EVIDENCE LEVELS; DIRECT INSPECTION OF THE 1684 PLATE AND 1696 DERIVATIVE REMAINS PRIORITY

## Result

Johann Just Winckelmann's 1684 *Des Oldenburgischen Wunder-Horns Ursprung / Herkunft / Materie / Form / Gestalt / Figuren und Hieroglyphische Auslegung* gives the repository an unusually compact case in which:

`material object access`
→ `source criticism based on object-specific features`
→ `drawing / engraved surrogate`
→ `representational alteration/error`
→ `reproduction in later collection literature`
→ `long-range reuse as a visual model`.

The key point is that **direct access and representational fidelity do not coincide automatically**.

Winckelmann could use material details to recognize that another writer had not seen the object, while his own privileged access still produced an image that later scholarship regards as inaccurate in a consequential heraldic detail.

Working proposition:

> `OBJECT-BASED EPISTEMIC AUTHORITY ≠ SURROGATE FIDELITY`.

This is directly relevant to the repository's wider distinction between historical witness, visual surrogate, matrix/image state, and later evidentiary use.

## 1. The publication explicitly promises an object-centred visual apparatus

The full 1684 title describes the subject through materially differentiated categories:

`Ursprung / Herkunft / Materie / Form / Gestalt / Figuren und Hieroglyphische Auslegung`

and promises:

`Nebst Dessen eigentlichen künstlichen Abrisses / Landcharten und andern Figuren`.

Thus visual representation is built into the publication's epistemic programme rather than added as generic ornament.

The official Landesbibliothek Oldenburg digital index controls the edition:

Johann Just Winckelmann, *Des Oldenburgischen Wunder-Horns Ursprung/ Herkunft/ Materie/ Form/ Gestalt/ Figuren und Hieroglyphische Auslegung ... Nebst Dessen eigentlichen künstlichen Abrisses/ Landcharten und andern Figuren*, Bremen: Wessel, 1684.

Source:

- Landesbibliothek Oldenburg person/work index: https://digital.lb-oldenburg.de/name/view/463964

The exact plate/map census still requires direct collation of the digital object.

## 2. Winckelmann explicitly used material details to diagnose non-eyewitness knowledge

A modern study of Happel's *Relationes Curiosae* transcribes Winckelmann's own 1684 argument.

Reading Happel's earlier description of the horn, Winckelmann says that from the circumstances of the description, especially the treatment of the inscription and a known break, he concluded:

`der Author dieses Horn selbst nicht müsse gesehen haben`.

In other words, material/visual discrepancies become a test for source proximity.

Operational form:

`known object feature`
+
`reported inscription/break state`
→ `comparison with textual description`
→ `inference about whether reporter inspected object`.

This is a highly relevant early control for **material source criticism**.

Source / transcription:

- Flemming Schock, *Die Text-Kunstkammer: Populäre Wissenssammlungen des Barock am Beispiel der Relationes Curiosae*, discussion of Winckelmann/Happel, quoting Winckelmann 1684 pp. 4–5.

Public text version: https://dokumen.pub/die-text-kunstkammer-populre-wissenssammlungen-des-barock-am-beispiel-der-relationes-curiosae-von-ew-happel-9783412214166-9783412206154.html

Evidence status:

`PRIMARY WORDING THROUGH SCHOLARLY TRANSCRIPTION / PAGE LOCATOR CONTROLLED`.

Direct scan inspection of Winckelmann pp.4–5 remains desirable.

## 3. Happel publicly recognizes Winckelmann's object-side authority

Happel responded to Winckelmann within the same year 1684.

In his article `Das wiederholte Horn zu Oldenburg`, Happel describes Winckelmann as an excellent antiquary and historian and explicitly grounds his superior authority in prior physical/institutional access:

`diese Rarität unter seinen Händen gehabt`

and

`in ipso loco die beste Wissenschafft davon hat und geben kann`.

Happel then republishes Winckelmann's corrections to his own earlier account.

Thus the contemporary reception sequence is directly visible:

`Happel mediated/compiled description`
→ `Winckelmann detects non-eyewitness weakness`
→ `Winckelmann object-based correction`
→ `Happel accepts superior local/material authority`
→ `corrections re-enter periodical circulation`.

Source:

- Schock transcription of Happel, *Relationes Curiosae*, vol. 2.2, no. 65, pp.518ff.

Evidence status:

`PRIMARY CONTEMPORARY RECEPTION THROUGH SCHOLARLY TRANSCRIPTION`.

## 4. The object was reportedly available to Winckelmann for weeks during image production

Gitte Kjær's 1995 object-history study states that Winckelmann had the Oldenburg Horn in his home for several weeks in order to have it drawn for an engraving.

Kjær's wording:

- Winckelmann had the horn at home `i flere uger`;
- purpose: `for at lade det aftegne til et kobberstik`.

Her article cites Winckelmann 1684 pp.4f. and the illustration on an unnumbered/early plate page as its immediate reference environment.

Source:

- Gitte Kjær, `En heraldisk gåde på Rosenborg Slot`, *Heraldisk Tidsskrift* 72 (1995), esp. pp.73 and 77–78 of the article.
- Public PDF: https://slaegtsbibliotek.dk/2024/941239.pdf

Evidence status:

`VERIFIED SCHOLARLY OBJECT-HISTORY REPORT WITH PRIMARY LOCATOR`.

Until Winckelmann's exact sentence is directly inspected, do not sharpen this into a more precise custody arrangement than `object reportedly remained in/at his home for several weeks for drawing/engraving preparation`.

---

## 5. Privileged object access nevertheless produced a reported heraldic error

Kjær compares the surviving physical horn with Winckelmann's representation and reports that he introduced transverse bars on the horn's tail as an Oldenburg coat of arms even though **no Oldenburg coat of arms exists on the object itself**.

She states that this `Fejl` / error was repeated in later representations and even entered the Rosenborg catalogue for a period.

Her later discussion specifies that Winckelmann inserted a series of arms on the tail, including an Oldenburg form associated with Count Gerhard's arms after his 1474–75 Burgundian alliance context.

This gives the repository a controlled contradiction:

`LONG / PRIVILEGED OBJECT ACCESS`

but

`ENGRAVED SURROGATE CONTAINS A HERALDIC FEATURE NOT PRESENT ON THE SURVIVING OBJECT`.

The mechanism of the error remains open.

Possible explanations to distinguish:

- Winckelmann or draughtsman interpreted decorative bars as heraldic evidence;
- an intermediate drawing amplified ambiguous features;
- historical reconstruction was intentionally inserted into the visual surrogate;
- the physical object itself changed after 1684;
- Kjær's modern reconstruction requires further object-history checking.

Kjær herself raises the last possibility as a question: whether such an armorial element might once have existed and later been removed.

Therefore the safest status is:

`1684 REPRESENTATION vs PRESENT OBJECT HERALDIC DISCREPANCY = CONTROLLED`

`CAUSE = UNRESOLVED`.

Do not call it simple careless drawing until the plate, present object, and earlier/later object images are directly compared.

## 6. The error apparently propagated into later visual/catalogue traditions

Kjær reports that the erroneous armorial element recurred in later images and at one point in the Rosenborg catalogue.

She further states that Holger Jacobaeus's 1696 *Museum Regium* contains a copperplate apparently based on the `calque`/visual model published by Winckelmann in 1684.

This yields a strong provisional transmission model:

`physical Oldenburg Horn`
→ `Winckelmann observation/drawing environment`
→ `1684 engraved surrogate with disputed heraldic addition`
→ `1696 royal Kunstkammer catalogue image apparently derived from 1684 model`
→ `later images / catalogue description`.

Jacobaeus's 1696 object is independently controlled:

- *Museum Regium*, Copenhagen, 1696;
- engraved frontispiece + 37 engraved plates;
- Oldenburg Horn appears as Tab. XVI;
- text at p.59 describes `Cornu Oldenburgicum`.

Digital controls:

- Biodiversity Heritage Library: https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/item/133595
- Alvin record: https://www.alvin-portal.org/alvin/view.jsf?pid=alvin-record%3A103981
- Wikimedia/IA digital PDF indexed as `museumregiumseuc00jaco`.

A 1732 Dutch antiquarian discussion independently identifies Jacobaeus Tab. XVI as a large image of the Oldenburg drinking horn.

Current status:

`1696 JACOBAEUS OLDENBURG-HORN PLATE = VERIFIED`

`1696 PLATE DERIVED FROM 1684 WINCKELMANN SURROGATE = REPORTED SCHOLARLY INFERENCE, NOT YET DIRECTLY PROVED BY IMAGE COMPARISON`.

## 7. The 1696 catalogue transforms an image-derived heraldic feature into historical reasoning

The searchable 1696 *Museum Regium* text is particularly revealing.

Jacobaeus reasons from the shields/arms affixed to the horn and specifically discusses Oldenburg/Delmenhorst heraldic beams/bars as chronological evidence for dating and rejecting an early-Otto attribution.

Thus the potential image error is not merely decorative noise.

If Jacobaeus's plate/reading ultimately depends on Winckelmann's surrogate, the transmission sequence may be:

`representational interpolation`
→ `catalogue image`
→ `heraldic interpretation`
→ `chronological historical inference`.

This possibility is high-value but must be tested carefully against the **actual object Jacobaeus had access to in Copenhagen after 1690**. Because Jacobaeus could inspect the transferred object itself, one cannot infer from image similarity alone that his heraldic argument derives only from Winckelmann's image.

Guardrail:

`IMAGE ERROR TRANSMISSION ≠ AUTOMATIC TEXTUAL/INTERPRETIVE DEPENDENCE`.

## 8. Long-range visual reuse: 1807–08

The 1684 image acquired a separate life more than a century later.

A study of the title copper for Arnim/Brentano's *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* records that on 25 December 1807 Göttingen librarian Beneke answered Jacob Grimm's request for a good image of the Oldenburg Horn by sending him `den Winkelmann`, adding:

`worin die beste Abbildung sich befindet`.

Within days Brentano could transmit a sketch/concept for the title image.

A separate modern object-history study confirms that the 1684 engraving served as a model for the copperplate title of the second volume of *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* in 1808.

Sources:

- study on the *Wunderhorn* title copper, ULB Münster/NOAH dissertation PDF: https://noah.nrw/ulbmshsnoah/content/titleinfo/4290897/full.pdf
- modern object-history discussion: University of Bremen PDF on medieval treasury art / Kunstkammer transmission.

The relation is therefore:

`1684 antiquarian object surrogate`
→ `1807 library retrieval as best available image`
→ `1808 literary title-copper model`.

This is a genuine **function shift**:

`historical/material evidentiary illustration`
→ `authoritative visual reference`
→ `literary threshold apparatus`.

---

## 9. A paper-museum control predates and parallels Winckelmann

A Berlin Kunstkammer object biography identifies a sixteenth-century drawing of the Oldenburg Horn, recorded in the Berlin Kunstkammer's 1694 inventory and preserved as an image-object in a collection of paper representations.

This means the Horn's evidentiary/collecting life already included transportable images independent of the physical object.

Source:

- *The Berlin Kunstkammer. Collection History in Object Biographies*, object entry `The Oldenburg Horn`, drawing c.1550(?), Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, Art Library.

The broader ecology is therefore not simply:

`object → Winckelmann print`.

It includes:

`physical court object`
↔ `drawings / painted copies / paper museum`
↔ `printed engravings`
↔ `collection catalogues`
↔ `later literary/artistic reuse`.

## 10. Analytical payoff

The case now yields a particularly strong repository proposition:

> Early-modern historical authority could be strengthened by direct inspection of an object while the visual surrogate produced from that privileged access simultaneously introduced features that acquired independent evidentiary lives.

Therefore:

`object access`
≠ `representation accuracy`
≠ `later catalogue accuracy`
≠ `later interpretive use`.

And:

`surrogate portability`
can preserve **both evidence and error**.

Working sequence:

`OBJECT`
→ `INSPECTION / MATERIAL SOURCE CRITICISM`
→ `DRAWING / ENGRAVING`
→ `SURROGATE STATE`
→ `REMOTE REPRODUCTION`
→ `CATALOGUE / HISTORICAL INFERENCE`
→ `LATER VISUAL REUSE`.

This directly parallels the Sagittarius–Schlegel control already in the repository:

`object / received drawing → printed surrogate → remote circulation → reproduction error → expert correction`.

The Winckelmann Horn case adds a different possibility:

`object access → surrogate error → error propagation`.
