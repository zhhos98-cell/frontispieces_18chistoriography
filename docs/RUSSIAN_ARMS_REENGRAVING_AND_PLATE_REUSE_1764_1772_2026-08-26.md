# Russian arms: design inheritance, new plate, and later reuse, 1764–1772 — 26 August 2026

## Result

A published plate-history control identified by P. A. Druzhinin sharply supports the repository's distinction between design identity and matrix identity. Discussing the recurring representation of the Russian arms in eighteenth-century German genealogical/heraldic calendars, Druzhinin notes that the same erroneous broad image type already circulated in earlier calendars, but that the version appearing in the Gatterer/Seyfart milieu in the 1760s was made from a **new plate**. He further states that this same new plate was used again by Johann Friedrich Seyfart in 1772.

The controlled relation is therefore:

`older recurring Russian-arms design`
→ `new copperplate by/for the 1764 Gatterer calendar context`
→ `same new plate reused in Seyfart 1772`.

This is particularly valuable because it demonstrates both halves of the repository's plate ontology in one concrete case:

1. **same design does not entail same matrix**: an inherited armorial image could be re-engraved onto a new plate;
2. **a new matrix can then outlive one annual host**: once produced, the new plate could be reused in a later Seyfart publication.

## Source

P. A. Druzhinin, study of palace/ex-libris and heraldic material (2014), accessible through the Russian heraldic portal `gerboved.ru`, PDF `Druzhinin-2014-Palace-ExLibris.pdf`, discussion around figure 4 and notes 52–53.

Druzhinin's text states that a recurring erroneous form of the Russian arms had appeared in German historical calendars from the 1720s. When the calendars in the 1760s were issued with the participation of prominent heraldists Johann Christoph Gatterer and Johann Friedrich Seyfart, the same image appeared again **but on a new plate**. His note 53 identifies the 1764 *Fortgesetzter Wappen-Calender* as the control, and the text says that **the same plate was used in 1772 by J. Seyfart**.

The cited 1764 carrier is:

Johann Christoph Gatterer, *Fortgesetzter Wappen-Calender auf das Jahr 1764 oder jährliches Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik* (Nürnberg, 1764), plate [2] according to Druzhinin's note.

## Analytical use

### A. Design history and matrix history must be recorded separately

The Russian-arms example supplies an externally argued case where an image can look historically continuous while the production substrate has changed. Accordingly:

`iconographic recurrence`
≠ `continuous copper matrix`.

This validates the caution already applied to the Historia/Genealogia/Heraldica frontispieces: even a highly stable composition cannot be called the same plate without line/state evidence.

### B. Re-engraving does not end reuse

The 1764 new plate itself becomes reusable stock and survives into the 1772 Seyfart context. Thus re-engraving and stock reuse are not alternative models but sequential operations:

`old design copied/re-engraved`
→ `new matrix enters publisher/editorial stock`
→ `new matrix reused across later hosts`.

This is the most economical general model for several unresolved frontispiece cases as well: an older Type A/B/C design could recur either through survival of an old copperplate or through a copied/re-engraved matrix that then acquires its own reuse history.

### C. The Gatterer→Seyfart transition includes physical continuity below title/editor level

The 1764→1772 plate reuse crosses an editorial and bibliographic transition. Gatterer's 1764 *Fortgesetzter Wappen-Calender* and Seyfart's later cumulative armorial context therefore share not merely subject matter but, in this case according to Druzhinin, one physical printing matrix.

This gives a concrete comparator for the repository's broader question about publisher-held `Verlagskupfer` and the ability of graphic stock to outlive authors, editors and annual titles.

## Evidentiary guardrails

1. Druzhinin's published statement is currently the control for the `new plate` and `same plate reused in 1772` claims. A direct 1764/1772 image comparison remains desirable before adding defect/state-level detail.
2. The claim concerns the Russian-arms plate, not the Historia/Genealogia/Heraldica frontispiece.
3. Do not generalize one confirmed re-engraving/reuse sequence into an assumption that all recurring arms or frontispieces behaved identically.
4. `Same plate` here follows Druzhinin's specialist judgment; repository-level matrix attribution should be upgraded further if direct high-resolution images expose identical micro-defects, lettering or wear.

## Consequence for current frontispiece work

Use this case as a methodological comparator when testing recurrent Type A and Type C frontispieces:

- composition recurrence may represent an old matrix;
- or a copied/re-engraved replacement;
- once a replacement exists, that newer matrix may itself be reused for years.

The frontispiece question should therefore remain a **matrix genealogy**, not a binary old-plate/new-plate choice.
