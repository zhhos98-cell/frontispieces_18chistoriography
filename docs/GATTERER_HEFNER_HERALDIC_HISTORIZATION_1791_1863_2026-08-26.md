# From Gatterer to Hefner: heraldic historization as an object-history operation, 1791–1863

## Result

The 1767 control that separates blazon from historical elucidation can now be followed into a later, much more explicit methodological reception.

Johann Christoph Gatterer's *Praktische Heraldik* (Nürnberg, 1791) is a 150-page practical manual with six copperplates and three genealogical tables. Its importance for this repository is not merely that it supplies worked heraldic examples. Later heraldic teaching understood Gatterer's practical programme as differentiating operations that had to be performed on a given armorial object rather than collapsing all heraldic work into description.

Otto Titan von Hefner's *Praktische Heraldik* (1863) makes that operational differentiation unusually explicit. Hefner treats four inherited practical chapters—**Blasonierung, Historisierung, Kritisierung, Aufreißen**—and adds further chapters of his own. His chapter XIX, `Die Historisierung`, defines historization as the narration of the origin, gradual development, augmentation and alteration of a coat of arms up to the state in which it is presently observed.

The key methodological consequence is:

> **historization is object-centred temporal reconstruction, not a synonym for adding historical context.**

This later reception helps clarify why the repository should distinguish formal description, historical elucidation, object-history and criticism even when all four may concern the same engraved coat of arms.

This confirms an intensely worked practical environment but does **not** by itself recover the exact wording of Gatterer's four-operation taxonomy.

The fourfold formula `Blasonieren / Historisieren / Kritisieren / Aufreißen` is presently controlled through later heraldic reception/transcription and must therefore remain separate from the direct 1791 bibliographic control until the opening practical-rules pages are inspected line by line.

## II. Hefner 1863: `Historisierung` becomes an explicit object-history procedure

Hefner's chapter is unusually precise about what the operation should do.

### Core object

The object is a **particular armorial state actually before the researcher**. Historization reconstructs how that particular Wappen came to have the form in which it is observed.

The operation is therefore temporally directional:

`earliest recoverable occurrence / grant`
→ `documented augmentations or alterations`
→ `presently observed armorial state`.

For an armorial object that has never changed after its first appearance, the history may be extremely short: date of grant or earliest known occurrence. For a composite armorial object accumulated over generations, the operation expands because each added field has a historical cause.

### Required contextual knowledge

Genealogy or the history of the relevant family, city, corporation or other body is necessary because changes in the arms cannot be dated or explained without knowing the events that produced them.

But context is instrumental rather than unlimited. The purpose is to recover **time and cause of armorial change**.

This distinction matters enormously for the project:

`context useful to explain a witness state`
≠
`general history surrounding the witness`.

### Source ecology

Hefner names archives, family chronicles, seal collections, armorials, public monuments and printed special histories among the sources required for historization.

This source ecology crosses media, but unlike Gatterer's earlier rank hierarchy it is organised here around one practical problem: reconstructing the temporal career of a given armorial object.

### Procedure

Hefner's sequence can be paraphrased conservatively as:

1. identify the name, type and geographical/institutional home of the arms;
2. establish the origin, or where origin cannot be demonstrated, the oldest known seal, monument or other occurrence;
3. for granted arms, determine the date/place/grantor and, when recoverable, the cause of the grant;
4. record later dates of alteration or augmentation.

This is very close to a controlled provenance history of the **armorial configuration**, not merely of the family or polity represented by it.

## III. Operation boundaries: historization is neither blazon nor criticism

Hefner's practical architecture keeps several jobs separate:

- **Blasonierung** — formal/verbal description of the coat of arms;
- **Historisierung** — reconstruction of its historical formation and changes;
- **Kritisierung** — judgment of correctness, admissibility or historical/heraldic validity;
- **Aufreißen** — practical graphic construction/rendering.

The significance for an image corpus is structural. One and the same image can be:

- accurately described yet historically unexplained;
- historically reconstructed yet not critically adjudicated;
- critically rejected because its formal state conflicts with older witnesses;
- graphically redrawn while preserving or altering evidentiary features.

`seeing / naming / historicizing / criticizing / rendering`
are therefore related but non-identical scholarly operations.

## IV. Hefner's explicit criticism of Gatterer

Hefner directly discusses the worked arms of Austria, Prussia, Mecklenburg, Great Britain and Quedlinburg in Gatterer's 1791 *Praktische Heraldik*.

His judgment is particularly useful because it separates two dimensions of Gatterer's work:

- the **Kritisierungen** are not especially valuable in Hefner's assessment;
- the **Historisierungen** are more significant, yet they extend too far and move farther than necessary from the particular coat of arms under investigation.

This is a methodological criticism of **scope control**.

The later heraldist is not rejecting historical knowledge. He is insisting that historical inquiry remain indexed to the armorial object whose state is being explained.

The contrast can be rendered as:

`history relevant to armorial state`
versus
`history that exceeds the explanatory needs of the armorial state`.

That distinction is unusually close to the repository's own witness-level discipline. It provides a historical reception control for asking how much context can be attached to a visual witness before the object itself disappears inside general narrative.

## V. Relation to the repository's witness-occurrence model

The repository's preferred atomic unit is a witness occurrence: one verifiable appearance of a plate or related state in a particular publication/copy context.

Hefner's historization procedure suggests a parallel armorial unit:

> **one armorial state at one historically situated occurrence, connected backward and forward by documented transformations.**

This makes several familiar repository distinctions methodologically sharper:

`same family / polity`
≠ `same armorial state`

`same armorial programme`
≠ `same historical configuration`

`same described blazon`
≠ `same graphic witness`

`later composite arms`
≠ `timeless representation of the family`.

A heraldic image can therefore be treated as a historically situated configuration whose additions, substitutions and omissions need explanation rather than normalization.

## VI. A two-axis model now becomes a three-axis model

The 1766–1863 controls suggest three independent analytical axes.

### Axis 1: source status

What kind of witness supplies the knowledge?

Examples: armorial/ennoblement charter, seal, coin, monument, historical writer, family book, armorial collection.

### Axis 2: scholarly operation

What is done with that witness or armorial object?

Examples: blazon, historical elucidation, historization, criticism, graphic reconstruction.

### Axis 3: object-state temporality

Which historical state of the object is under discussion?

Examples:

- first documented occurrence;
- granted state;
- augmented state;
- quartered/composite state;
- later altered state;
- copied or corrupted graphic state;
- current observed witness.

This third axis prevents a major flattening. A source may be authoritative, and the operation may genuinely be historical, while the researcher still conflates several chronologically distinct armorial states.

## VII. Relation back to 1767

The newly recovered 1767 Rudolphi control had already implied:

`Blasonist`
≠ `historical elucidator`
≠ historian of the arms.

Hefner 1863 shows one later way that the last category could be formalized as a bounded operation: reconstruct the origin and transformations of the **given Wappen**.

The safe chronology is therefore:

`1766: sources of heraldry are an elementary methodological topic`
→ `1767: blazon is distinguished from historical elucidation/history of arms`
→ `1791: Gatterer publishes a dedicated Praktische Heraldik with worked examples`
→ `1863: Hefner codifies Historisierung as object-centred temporal reconstruction and explicitly evaluates Gatterer's 1791 examples`.

This is a history of published distinctions and reception. It is **not** evidence that Hefner's 1863 definition reproduces Gatterer's 1767 or 1791 wording unchanged.

## VIII. Why this matters for frontispieces

The methodological gain reaches beyond heraldry.

A frontispiece or plate can also be studied through object-state temporality:

`design origin`
→ `plate/matrix state`
→ `publication occurrence`
→ `copy-specific alteration`
→ `later reuse or recontextualization`.

The analogy should not be turned into a claim that Gatterer possessed the repository's modern plate ontology. The value is more modest and more useful: historical heraldry supplies an eighteenth-/nineteenth-century control showing that **visual configurations themselves could be made into temporally changing historical objects** rather than treated as transparent illustrations of an enduring identity.

This reinforces the project's central separation:

`programme identity ≠ composition identity ≠ matrix identity ≠ impression/copy identity`.

## Evidence guards

1. Direct 1791 control currently establishes the book, pagination, visual apparatus and practical/example architecture. The exact opening prose of Gatterer's operation taxonomy still requires direct page inspection.
2. The fourfold practical vocabulary is firmly attested in later heraldic reception but should not be quoted as Gatterer's exact 1791 sentence until the primary page is read.
3. Hefner 1863 is a **reception and reformulation**, not a substitute witness for Gatterer's own definitions.
4. Hefner's criticism that Gatterer ranges too far is evidence for a later norm of object-centred scope control; do not project that norm backward as Gatterer's intention.
5. The analogy between armorial-state history and plate-state history is analytical. It does not establish direct conceptual borrowing.
6. The GenWiki index entry `Methode ein gegebenes Wappen historisch zu beschreiben (Gatterer)` remains only a navigational lead until the underlying page/text is directly recovered.
