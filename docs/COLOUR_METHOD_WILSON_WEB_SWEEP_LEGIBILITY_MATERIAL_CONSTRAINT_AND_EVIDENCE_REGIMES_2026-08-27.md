# Colour method — Wilson-informed web sweep: legibility, material constraint, observer training, and evidence regimes

Date: 2026-08-27  
Status: research sweep / method revision input  
Scope: colour as a cross-domain methodological probe; early modern–long eighteenth century core, with carefully marked nineteenth-/twenty-first-century controls

## 0. Why this sweep exists

The previous colour protocol asked:

> **When may a visible difference become a historical difference?**

The Wilson reading adds an analytically prior question:

> **How does a difference become available to history in the first place?**

Georgina Wilson's *Paper and the Making of Early Modern Literature* is the methodological anchor. Her important move is not simply from textuality to materiality. It is from using a material feature only as first-order evidence for a historical fact to using materiality recursively, as a way to interrogate the coordinates from which a discipline notices, categorises and values evidence.

Applied to colour, this produces two linked chains.

### Historical chain

`material affordance / constraint`
→ `perceptual availability`
→ `discretisation / naming`
→ `externalisation / encoding`
→ `classification / comparison`
→ `inference target`

### Modern-surrogate chain

`surviving copy / material state`
→ `cataloguing decision`
→ `digitisation / surrogate transformation`
→ `analyst visibility`
→ `coding / category`
→ `historical claim`

The cross-check is now mandatory:

> **Does the modern chain reproduce, erase, compress, or invent a feature of the historical chain?**

This sweep searches the web for cases that make those two chains empirically visible.

## 1. First major control: colour can disappear historiographically without disappearing materially

### 1.1 Elizabeth Savage / Elizabeth Upper: early colour printing as a manufactured absence

The strongest Wilson-style control case found in this sweep is the reconstruction of early European colour printing by Elizabeth Savage (formerly Upper), Ad Stijnman and collaborators.

The important point is methodological rather than merely bibliographic. Early colour printing did not simply wait to be “discovered.” It was made hard to see by several layers of disciplinary and surrogate practice:

1. unusual colour-printing processes lacked stable descriptive vocabulary;
2. graphic-art scholarship inherited a strong bias toward monochrome printmaking;
3. bibliographical and art-historical traditions divided objects produced by related technologies into separate corpora;
4. many digitisation projects omitted or flattened colour;
5. copy-level colour could be treated as embellishment rather than as evidence of production.

The result is a historiographical absence that is not identical to an archival absence.

Savage and Stijnman's *Printing Colour 1400–1700* (2015) explicitly describes a corpus of **thousands** of early colour prints and argues that they were not rare outliers but significant components of early modern book, print and visual cultures. A contemporary review notes that the volume deliberately joins the previously separated worlds of bibliography and art history and bases interpretation on production techniques rather than privileging authors and artists.

Sources:
- Ad Stijnman and Elizabeth Savage, eds., *Printing Colour 1400–1700: History, Techniques, Functions and Receptions* (Brill, 2015), DOI 10.1163/9789004290112.
- Ad Stijnman and Elizabeth Savage, “Introduction: A Historical Overview of Printed Colour before 1700,” pp. 1–7, DOI 10.1163/9789004290112_002.
- Review, *The Library* 17.4 (2016), pp. 453ff., Oxford Academic.
- Centre / University material associated with Elizabeth Savage's work on rediscovering early printed colour.

### Methodological consequence

Add a field:

`conditions_of_legibility`

with at least:

- `disciplinary_vocabulary_available`
- `catalogue_visibility`
- `surrogate_colour_fidelity`
- `disciplinary_boundary_effect`

A feature can be physically present yet methodologically unavailable.

Thus:

`archival presence` ≠ `catalogue presence` ≠ `digital presence` ≠ `historiographical presence`.

## 2. Reuse is not only afterlife: it can create the future evidence channel

### 2.1 Red frisket sheets

Savage's 2014 article, “Red Frisket Sheets, c. 1490–1700: The Earliest Artefacts of Colour Printing in the West,” is especially important after Wilson.

Before the discovery of surviving frisket sheets, the earliest methods for printing selected text in red had largely to be reconstructed from the printed result because written technical instructions appeared much later. The identified frisket sheets have a striking tripartite material biography:

`manuscript / printers' waste`
→ `frisket in a printing press for colour printing`
→ `binding material in another book`.

The third use obscured the second, but it also preserved the object that later made the second use reconstructible. The growing corpus revises assumptions about workshop practice and indicates a wider system of material exchange within the early modern book trade.

Sources:
- Elizabeth Savage (published as Elizabeth Upper), “Red Frisket Sheets, c. 1490–1700: The Earliest Artefacts of Colour Printing in the West,” *Papers of the Bibliographical Society of America* 108.4 (2014): 477–522.
- Institute of English Studies, “Early Modern Frisket Sheets,” project/census page.

### Methodological consequence

The existing ontology should distinguish:

`reuse_as_information_loss`
from
`reuse_as_evidence_preservation`
from
`reuse_as_new_operation`.

The same material transition can do more than one simultaneously.

A Wilson-style stop rule follows:

> Never infer the epistemic status of a material trace from its last surviving function.

The thing that survives as binding waste may be the only surviving instrument of an earlier production operation.

## 3. Material constraint can generate an operator instead of merely degrading information

This corrects a weakness in the previous colour-method formulation. We had tended to model material limitation as a cost to fidelity or reproducibility. The web evidence shows repeatedly that limitation can generate a new syntax.

### 3.1 Heraldic tincture and monochrome encoding

Gatterer's *Abriß der Heraldik* (Göttingen/Gotha, 1773; second edition Nuremberg, 1774) has a directly relevant structure. The 1773 edition is digitised from the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek and contains eight engraved plates. Google Books indexing confirms recurrent vocabulary including `Farbe`, `Tinkturen`, `Schrafirungen`, `Gold`, `Silber`, `roth`, and line-direction terminology.

Martin Gierl's reconstruction of Gatterer's historical method explicitly identifies Gatterer's 1773 Table I as combining a **Wappentopographie** with a **black-and-white representation of the tinctures**. A modern transcription of the 1774 text preserves a passage in which Gatterer evaluates proposed additions to the conventional hatching system: he calls a separate sign for “natural colour” superfluous and rejects a distinct iron-colour sign because older German letters patent used iron colour and white/silver indifferently. This quotation still requires direct page-image closure before promotion to `primary_direct`, but the analytical structure is clear.

Sources:
- Johann Christoph Gatterer, *Abriß der Heraldik* (Göttingen/Gotha: Dieterich, 1773), VD18 10528091; Bayerische Staatsbibliothek / Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek URN `urn:nbn:de:bvb:12-bsb10703603-5`.
- Google Books scan, BSB original, 1773.
- Martin Gierl, *Fundamenta Historica* (figure list: Fig. 17, Gatterer 1773a, Table I, “Schwarz-Weiß-Darstellung der Tinkturen”).
- Gatterer 1774, p. 12, hatching passage: currently mediated by a modern heraldic transcription; direct scan verification pending.

### 3.2 What the constraint does

The operation is not:

`colour original → imperfect monochrome copy`.

It is better represented as:

`tincture category`
→ `conventional hatch code`
→ `monochrome carrier`
→ `decoder`
→ `recoverable categorical colour information`.

The absence of pigment generates a graphic syntax that can be more reproducible in monochrome print than hand-applied pigment.

This suggests a new field:

`operation_generated_by_constraint`.

Possible values:
- `encoding_system`
- `abstraction`
- `quantification`
- `selection_rule`
- `observer_training`
- `none`

Constraint is therefore neither intrinsically negative nor intrinsically generative. It must be reconstructed operationally.

## 4. Werner 1774: colour as the first sensory generic character

Abraham Gottlob Werner's *Von den äusserlichen Kennzeichen der Fossilien* (Leipzig, 1774) supplies unusually direct primary evidence for the transition from perceptible difference to disciplined classification.

### 4.1 External characters are sensory characters

Werner defines external characters as properties found on the exterior/aggregate of minerals **through the senses**. He argues that their scientific usefulness depends on making these characters as determinate as possible (`so genau als möglich bestimmt`).

The work is organized around the need to make external descriptions complete, ordered and determinate.

Primary source:
- Abraham Gottlob Werner, *Von den äusserlichen Kennzeichen der Fossilien* (Leipzig: Crusius, 1774), Heidelberg University Library digital edition, DOI `10.11588/diglit.2529`.
- §§13–14: external / sensory characters.
- chapter 3, p. 71: requirement of determinacy.

### 4.2 Colour arrives first

At p. 87 (§41), Werner states that colour is the first of the generic characters to strike the senses and calls it one of the most reliable among them, especially as a means of distinction for many metals, combustible substances and salts.

The order matters:

`object`
→ `sensory encounter`
→ `colour noticed first`
→ `colour named / differentiated`
→ `mineral distinguished`.

Colour is not simply a property subsequently recorded; it structures the temporal order of examination.

### 4.3 Werner also prunes the visual field

His method is selective. At §57 Werner refuses to include certain surface or variegated colour phenomena in the same way, because his taxonomy is designed for the colour of individual minerals rather than mixtures or surface alterations.

This is methodologically important. A classificatory system does not merely sharpen vision. It also tells an observer which visible differences **do not count** for the target operation.

Add:

`perceptual_selection_rule`

with values such as:
- `include`
- `exclude_as_surface_change`
- `exclude_as_mixture`
- `exclude_as_unstable`
- `exclude_as_redundant`

The trained eye is partly an eye trained **not** to see certain differences as classificatorily relevant.

## 5. Syme 1814/1821: the finite colour book as an instrument that trains the eye

Patrick Syme's *Werner's Nomenclature of Colours* is the strongest discovered case for adding `observer_training_function` to the ontology.

The second edition (Edinburgh, 1821; first edition 1814) begins from a problem of nomenclatural ambiguity: where colour is a character, Syme says, a regular standard is necessary. He argues that description, figure and colour together approach the evidentiary force of seeing the object itself.

But the more important passages come later.

### 5.1 The world is more continuous than the chart

Syme says Werner's series contained seventy-nine tints; he extends the practical standard to 110. He immediately acknowledges that the colours and shades of nature are far more numerous and gradual, potentially running into many thousands. A printed book cannot materially include them all without prohibitive cost.

Thus the chart is explicitly a finite partition of a much larger continuum.

### 5.2 The finite chart compensates by producing a trained observer

Syme's solution is not to claim exhaustive representation. Instead, studying the supplied colours should improve “general knowledge of colours” until the eye, by practice, becomes capable of analysing intermediate shades and assigning them relationally to components and transitions.

The chain is therefore:

`continuous visual field`
→ `finite material standard`
→ `repeated comparison`
→ `trained eye`
→ `judgment of intermediate shade`
→ `portable verbal description`.

The printed object does not simply standardise the colour. It standardises a **procedure of seeing**.

### 5.3 Materiality sets the boundary of the standard

Syme excludes metallic colours because they would tarnish, and excludes changeable/play colours because they cannot be represented adequately. He also states that the pigments used for the chart were selected and applied with concern for long-term constancy, while acknowledging that sunlight affects material colours.

So the classification itself is conditioned by the physical behaviour of pigments and paper.

Primary source:
- Patrick Syme, *Werner's Nomenclature of Colours, with Additions...*, 2nd ed. (Edinburgh: William Blackwood; London: T. Cadell, 1821), especially pp. 5–23 of the preface/introduction.

### Methodological consequence

Add:

`observer_training_function`

Possible values:
- `none`
- `terminological_calibration`
- `visual_matching`
- `intermediate_shade_judgment`
- `memory_training`
- `comparative_navigation`
- `measurement_training`

And distinguish:

`standardisation_of_objects`
from
`standardisation_of_observers`.

These often occur together but are not identical.

## 6. Gatterer's tables: display can become a measuring instrument

The Wilson revision also changes how synchronistic and diplomatic tables should be treated.

### 6.1 Linnaeismus graphicus

A detailed study of Gatterer's `Linnaeismus graphicus` shows that he transferred classificatory procedures associated with Linnaean natural history into diplomatics. He classified scripts into classes, orders, series, genera and species, but did more than produce a static taxonomy.

For the script of the *Tabula Peutingeriana*, Gatterer counted capitals, uncials and minuscules and rendered their mixture in tabular form. The resulting numerical relations are described by the modern historian as something like a script “fingerprint.” Chronometric tables then connected letter form, time span, national palaeographic context and documentary evidence.

Gatterer's explicit aim was practical and instrument-like: such tables supplied **standards for the eye**, for scholars, laypeople and students, against which undated material could be measured.

Source:
- Martin Gierl, “Das Alphabet der Natur und das Alphabet der Kultur im 18. Jahrhundert. Botanik, Diplomatik und Ethno-Linguistik bei Carl von Linné, Johann Christoph Gatterer und Christian Wilhelm Büttner,” *NTM* 18 (2010), open-access version in PMC.

### 6.2 Synchronistic tables

Gatterer's historical tables work on the same broad epistemic terrain: relations that unfold temporally are redistributed spatially on paper.

A late-eighteenth-century follower, Daniel Gotthold Joseph Hübler, explains the operation unusually clearly. Events are arranged on paper by spatial proximity corresponding to temporal simultaneity/succession so that chronology and synchronism become `sinnlich dargestellt`, graspable largely by the eye and less burdensome to memory.

Sources:
- Gatterer, *Einleitung in die synchronistische Universalhistorie zur Erläuterung seiner synchronistischen Tabellen* (Göttingen, 1771).
- Daniel Gotthold Joseph Hübler, synchronistic tables, 1796, preface; transcription/reproduction in *Die Welt in einer Nuß: Alte Geschichtslehrwerke*.
- LMU München project on eighteenth-century historical tables.

### Methodological consequence

The previous `display_jurisdiction` field is too narrow if it only asks whether colour or graphic form increases salience, overview or memory.

Add a distinction:

`display_epistemic_role`

Possible values:
- `redundant_salience`
- `navigation`
- `memory_support`
- `relation_externalisation`
- `measurement_standard`
- `comparison_instrument`
- `knowledge_generative_rearrangement`

A display may externalise a relation already independently warranted; it may also create a new operation of comparison that would be difficult or impossible in prose.

This still does **not** mean that visual obviousness automatically increases historical warrant. It means that display can alter the *available operation*.

## 7. Human colour is the stress test: description, discretisation and descent come apart

The human-science cases expose the point at which the inferential chain becomes politically and epistemically dangerous. The relevant historical problem is not that colour was simply “ideological” or “scientific.” The problem is that actors disagreed about which inference target colour could legitimately bear.

### 7.1 Blumenbach: classification despite admitted continua

The University of Göttingen's Blumenbach project summarises a recurrent claim in Blumenbach's work: bodily differences, including form and colour, run into one another through gradual shades and transitions and can be separated only by arbitrary limits. The same project notes that Blumenbach considered skin colour unsuitable as a stable criterion for distinguishing varieties, even while he retained a five-part division of humanity.

A translated primary passage from the 1795 third edition states that no variety of colour, countenance or stature is so singular that it lacks imperceptible transitions to others; nevertheless Blumenbach proceeds to reckon five principal varieties.

Sources:
- Johann Friedrich Blumenbach, *De generis humani varietate nativa*, 3rd ed. (1795), §§80–90; English translation in Thomas Bendyshe, *The Anthropological Treatises of Johann Friedrich Blumenbach* (1865).
- University of Göttingen, “Blumenbach and the concept of race.”

### Methodological structure

`continuous variation acknowledged`
→ `finite partition retained`
→ `classification treated as pragmatic/conventional`
→ **stronger genealogical or essential inference requires additional warrant**.

This is the cleanest case yet for separating:

`discretisation success`
from
`ontological discreteness`.

### 7.2 Forster versus Kant: category versus “matter of fact”

Stella Sandford's reconstruction of Georg Forster's 1786 response to Kant is nearly an eighteenth-century statement of the repository's inference-jurisdiction rule.

Forster denies that skin colour adequately distinguishes Kantian races. More importantly, he attacks the methodological move from an invented conceptual category and hypothesis about descent to treating that structure as the “matter of fact itself.” If natural history requires reconstructing generative origin and descent beyond what observations can establish, Forster calls it effectively a science for gods rather than humans.

Source:
- Georg Forster, “Noch etwas über die Menschenraßen,” *Der Teutsche Merkur* (October and November 1786).
- Stella Sandford, “Kant, race, and natural history,” *Philosophy & Social Criticism* (2018).

This gives a historically explicit stop rule:

> A visible descriptive difference does not become evidence of generative origin merely because a theory supplies a category into which the difference can be placed.

### 7.3 Meiners 1792: colour explicitly posed as an inferential operator

Christoph Meiners's “Ueber die Farben, und Schattierungen verschiedener Völker,” *Neues Göttingisches historisches Magazin* I/4 (1792), pp. 611–672, is now a priority primary source.

A contemporary/near-contemporary encyclopaedic digest of Meiners's essay (*Oekonomisch-technologische Encyclopädie*, article “Leibes-Farbe der Menschen”) preserves its research questions with striking precision. The inquiry asks:

- whether national colours depend more on climate or descent;
- whether children's colour depends more on birthplace or parental blood;
- whether colour is a secure sign of races and gradations of humans;
- how many principal colours should be assumed;
- whether one or several of these principal colours can be treated as basic/original colours from which others arose.

A modern study citing pp. 614–15 also records Meiners's awareness that sun, heat/cold, air, wind, geography, clothing and cleanliness can alter or mask visible skin colour. This means the essay should **not** be simplified into “Meiners thought skin colour transparently revealed race.” The methodological problem is more interesting: he knows the perceptual variable is environmentally labile while still exploring whether it can bear claims about descent, mixture and rank.

Sources:
- Christoph Meiners, “Ueber die Farben, und Schattierungen verschiedener Völker,” *Neues Göttingisches historisches Magazin* I/4 (1792): 611–672.
- Johann Georg Krünitz et al., *Oekonomisch-technologische Encyclopädie*, vol. LXXI, “Leibes-Farbe der Menschen,” pp. 657ff., digesting Meiners.
- Secondary colour-history discussions citing Meiners pp. 612, 614–15, 640–41.

Evidence status:

`article_identity`: primary_direct metadata  
`research_question_structure`: strong near-contemporary mediated text  
`specific 611–672 argumentative sequence`: **pending direct article transcription/page verification**

This should be closed before we make a stronger claim about how Meiners resolves the tension between environmental variability and genealogical/hierarchical inference.

## 8. Nineteenth-century downstream control: Broca shows what happens when comparison becomes numbered instrumentation

Paul Broca's 1864 colour table is outside the eighteenth-century core and must not be used as evidence of direct genealogy from Werner, Gatterer, Blumenbach or Meiners. It is useful as a downstream control for what a fully materialised colour-measurement regime can look like.

Broca presented a chromatic table of 54 numbered colour types: 1–20 for eyes and 21–54 for principal colours of skin and hair. The important methodological transformation is:

`verbal description / remembered impression`
→ `material colour chart`
→ `numbered comparison`
→ `portable data field`.

Later anthropometric instructions made the bodily encounter more procedural: comparisons used charts, controlled viewing conditions, and sometimes apertures/masks to isolate the compared patch of skin. A historical study of Dutch New Guinea field anthropology notes the paradox: scientific “distance” from subjective impression required physically bringing chart, observer and measured body into a much more intimate instrument-mediated encounter.

Sources:
- Paul Broca, “Tableau chromatique des yeux, de la peau et des cheveux,” *Bulletins de la Société d'anthropologie de Paris* 5 (1864): 767–773, DOI `10.3406/bmsap.1864.4169`.
- Broca, “Tableau chromatique de la chevelure et de la peau,” ibid., pp. 138–140, DOI `10.3406/bmsap.1864.6637`.
- “Touch in anthropometry: Enacting race in Dutch New Guinea 1903–1909,” later methodological history.

### Why this control matters

It separates four operations that can otherwise collapse into one:

`seeing`
≠ `matching`
≠ `numbering`
≠ `racial inference`.

A chart can improve reproducibility of matching without validating the ontology or politics of the categories into which its numbers are inserted.

## 9. Modern digitisation is part of the evidentiary chain, not neutral access

The Federal Agencies Digitization Guidelines Initiative (FADGI) provides an unusually explicit contemporary control for Wilson's point.

### 9.1 Colour survives only after a valuation decision

FADGI separates textual/pictorial objects into categories in which colour is or is not considered necessary. For T.5, colour reproduction is required where it is important to interpretation, needed to distinguish markings, or desired to represent the look-and-feel of the original. For T.1/T.3/T.4 and related categories, curator/end-user valuation can determine that monochrome or grayscale reproduction is acceptable.

Thus the modern chain contains a human classificatory gate **before scanning**:

`original object`
→ `curatorial valuation of colour's relevance`
→ `chosen capture mode`
→ `digital surrogate`
→ `future research visibility`.

Sources:
- Federal Agencies Digitization Guidelines Initiative, Still Image Working Group, Content Categories, especially T.1, T.3–T.5 and PR.1/PR.2.
- National Archives, scanned textual records guidance: 24-bit RGB is appropriate when colour information is important to interpretation/content.

### 9.2 Digital colour is itself difficult to stabilise

FADGI's glossary states that colour is among the hardest aspects of digital imaging to specify, encode, reproduce and verify because it is perceptual rather than an absolutely objective image quality. NARA/FADGI technical guidance further notes that scanner profiles, colour spaces, conversion procedures and software implementations can yield different results.

The point for this project is not that digital colour is unreliable in general. It is that `digital colour` is a produced surrogate state with an identifiable workflow and error surface.

Add:

`surrogate_transformation`

Possible values:
- `full_colour_capture`
- `grayscale_capture`
- `bitonal_capture`
- `unknown_capture_mode`
- `colour_profile_transform`
- `contrast_or_tone_adjustment`
- `microfilm_derived`
- `photographic_reproduction`

and:

`surrogate_claim_limit`.

A digital image may be enough to prove layout or line geometry but insufficient to prove the copy's exact pigment state.

## 10. Catalogues can erase copy-level colour even when scans preserve it

The same Wilson problem occurs one step earlier in descriptive metadata.

A Folger Shakespeare Library discussion of mid-twentieth-century rebinding points out that many physical details never entered the online catalogue because a grant-funded cataloguing project's priority was edition/variant text and broad coverage; copy-specific notes emphasized defects affecting text and often omitted other material features.

Other projects explicitly compensate for this. Durham's *Pictures in Print* data profile treats hand-colouring as copy-specific enrichment. Cambridge and Glasgow incunabula projects emphasize copy-specific features such as decoration, illumination, rubrication, binding and provenance. Individual Eton catalogue records also classify hand-coloured frontispieces/maps as copy-specific notes.

Sources:
- Folger Shakespeare Library, “Mid-20th-Century Rare Book Rebindings.”
- Durham, *Pictures in Print* catalogue data profile.
- Cambridge University Library Incunabula Cataloguing Project.
- University of Glasgow Incunabula Project.
- Eton College Library copy-specific catalogue examples.

### Consequence for frontispieces

For every colour claim discovered through a catalogue search, ask:

1. is colour described at work/edition/copy level?
2. is the catalogue silent because the copy is monochrome, or because colour was outside the cataloguer's descriptive priorities?
3. does another copy have a richer item-level record?
4. does the scan contradict or exceed the catalogue description?

`catalogue silence` must never be coded as `colour absent`.

## 11. Revised method: six gates rather than one colour variable

The web sweep supports replacing a single colour field with six analytically distinct gates.

### Gate A — availability

**Can colour enter the observer's field at all?**

Variables:
- copy survival
- pigment survival/fading
- binding/reuse
- illumination/viewing conditions
- digitisation mode
- catalogue discoverability

### Gate B — selection

**Which visible differences are treated as relevant?**

Variables:
- disciplinary vocabulary
- task definition
- exclusion rules
- conventions of description

Werner is a clean case: some surface colour phenomena are visible but excluded from his target classificatory operation.

### Gate C — discretisation

**How is a continuum converted into finite categories?**

Variables:
- named colours
- swatches
- hatching
- numbered types
- principal/basic colour schemes
- acknowledged intermediate states

### Gate D — observer formation

**What must the observer learn to use the system?**

Variables:
- visual matching
- component-colour analysis
- memory
- use of a key/decoder
- controlled measurement procedure

Syme is the strongest case.

### Gate E — operation

**What task does the colour distinction make possible?**

Variables:
- identification
- reconstruction
- comparison
- navigation
- chronology
- classification
- measurement
- relation externalisation

### Gate F — inference jurisdiction

**What proposition is the colour operation allowed to bear?**

Retain the existing ladder:

`description`
< `identification`
< `relation`
< `genealogy/origin`
< `essential difference`
< `causal explanation`.

But add one critical rule:

> **Improved reproducibility at Gates C–E does not automatically enlarge jurisdiction at Gate F.**

Broca is the later control: a numbered chart may improve reproducibility of observations while leaving the racial ontology independently contestable.

## 14. What this changes in the project argument

The strongest formulation is no longer only:

> Colour is a methodological probe for the conversion of visible difference into historical knowledge.

It becomes:

> **Colour is a probe for the conditions under which a difference becomes visible, describable, classifiable and inferentially usable as historical knowledge. Its evidentiary force depends not only on what historical actors saw, but on the material and disciplinary infrastructures that selected, encoded and preserved what could count as seeing.**

This formulation has three advantages.

First, it keeps Wilson's reflexive move: the material object can interrogate the method that claims to describe it.

Second, it prevents the project from confusing standardisation with truth. A standard can make observations comparable while leaving the stronger inference contested.

Third, it makes the archive/digital-surrogate problem symmetrical with the eighteenth-century problem. Historical actors selected and encoded visible difference; modern institutions select and encode what survives of that selection for us.

## 15. New stop rules

1. **No catalogue-silence inference.** No colour mention in a catalogue is not evidence of a colourless copy.
2. **No surrogate-to-original collapse.** A digital RGB value is a property of a surrogate workflow unless independently calibrated to the original.
3. **No standardisation-to-ontology jump.** A repeatable colour category can be operationally successful without mapping onto a natural discrete kind.
4. **No trained-eye neutrality assumption.** A chart or table can train the observer by teaching both what to notice and what to ignore.
5. **No constraint-equals-loss assumption.** Material limitations may generate codes, abstractions or new comparison operations.
6. **No reuse-equals-corruption assumption.** Reuse can erase one function while preserving the only evidence for it.
7. **No visual-obviousness-to-warrant jump.** `sinnlich dargestellt` / perceptual Evidenz may reduce cognitive cost without supplying independent source warrant.
8. **No operational-homology-to-genealogy jump.** Similar solutions across heraldry, mineralogy, tables and anthropology do not establish transfer without citation, teaching, shared personnel, institutional contact or other independent evidence.
9. **No observer-free measurement assumption.** Even numbered colour comparison remains a procedure involving trained perception, material standards and viewing conditions.
10. **Always record the modern evidence channel.** Copy, catalogue, digitisation and analyst code are part of the provenance of the historical claim.

## 18. Core methodological sentence after the web sweep

> **Never ask only what a colour means or what it proves. Ask first what material and disciplinary conditions made that colour available to be noticed; then reconstruct how it was selected, discretised, encoded and learned; only then ask what inference actors allowed it to carry, and finally whether the modern copy–catalogue–digitisation chain lets us see the same thing.**
