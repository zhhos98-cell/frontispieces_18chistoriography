# Spener 1680: heraldic information intake, correction, source accountability and plate scheduling

## Status

Philipp Jakob Spener's preface to *Historia insignium illustrium sive Operis heraldici pars specialis* supplies a direct actor-side description of how a large heraldic reference work was assembled, checked, engraved, corrected and kept open for later supplementation. The first edition is 1680; the CAMENA text presents the 1717 second edition while preserving the preface text and marking variant spellings. The methodological passages discussed below therefore belong to Spener's 1680 publication programme, although any quotation requiring edition-specific typography should be checked against the 1680 image.

Primary text layer: CAMENA, `spener2`, *Historia insignium illustrium*, `LECTORI BENEVOLO SALUTEM`, especially images/pages `as009–as014`; stable text URL:

`https://mateo.uni-mannheim.de/camenaref/spener/spener2/spener2.html`

This source materially strengthens the repository's current model because it makes **information intake, source criticism, correction, plate production, indexing and publication scheduling** explicit within one seventeenth-century heraldic project.

## 1. The work depends on information arriving from outside

Spener says that a fourth book must contain heraldic cases for which his own apparatus was insufficient or for which the families concerned had not communicated the necessary information in time. The work is therefore openly conditioned by the timing and completeness of external submissions.

His production sequence is not:

`author knows corpus → engraver reproduces corpus`.

It is closer to:

`partial apparatus`
+
`submissions from families and intermediaries`
+
`independent searching`
→
`enough material for one entry`
→
`entry enters a publishable batch`
→
`remaining cases deferred until further information arrives`.

This matters because Spener later explains that the three published books were ordered partly by **when sufficient information became available**. The first book received families for which he already had fuller knowledge; the second received those whose materials arrived later or which he found through further research; the third contained cases for which he had waited longer and which came in while the work was nearing completion.

The sequence of a printed scholarly corpus can therefore register **information-arrival chronology**, not only conceptual classification.

## 2. Alphabetical order solves a production problem

Spener explicitly discusses why he rejected a hierarchy by dignity or nation. Such an order would have required him to wait until the arms of the highest ranks were adequately explained before printing could begin. Because even royal examples still lacked sufficient information, he chose alphabetical order.

The technical advantage is explicit: as soon as some number of entries was ready, printing could begin and continue.

This gives a strong historical control for the repository's wider interest in page architecture and publication rhythm:

`classification system`
↔
`availability of evidence`
↔
`ability to start printing`.

Alphabetical order is not only a neutral retrieval device. Here it is also a **production technology for an incompletely known corpus**.

The resulting disorder is then repaired through a **triple index**. Spener therefore separates:

- order of research readiness;
- order of printing;
- order of reader retrieval.

That three-way distinction is highly useful for later Wappenkalender / cumulative Wappenbuch comparison.

## 3. Siebmacher–Fürst is a foundation, not an authority immune from correction

For German families Spener says that he used the armorial begun by Johann Siebmacher and continued by Paul Fürst as a foundation (`fundamenti loco substratum`). He retained it except where he learned of its errors from elsewhere and corrected it.

Safe formulation:

> Spener treated the Siebmacher–Fürst corpus as a default reference layer that could be confirmed or corrected by independent information.

He further states that a very large part of his information was supplied by the families themselves or gathered elsewhere by him. These materials either confirmed the Siebmacher record or taught him more correctly.

This is exactly the kind of relation the repository needs to distinguish:

`inherited visual/reference corpus`
≠
`current verified description`.

The inherited Wappenbuch supplies a baseline, while later information can validate, modify or overturn it.

## 4. Source provenance is reported to the reader

Spener says that he usually prefaces heraldic discussion with genealogical information because genealogy illuminates the arms. More importantly, he normally names the authors used for individual cases so that the reader can know **where the information came from and on what faith/authority it is transmitted**.

This is not modern source criticism in twentieth-century terminology, but it is a direct early-modern statement of **source-accountability**.

A useful model is:

`claim / representation`
+
`named source`
+
`declared evidentiary dependence`
→
`reader can distinguish provenance of information`.

The repository's `source_evidence` layer therefore has a strong historical analogue in heraldic practice rather than being only a modern data-management convenience.

## 5. First-party information is useful but not self-authenticating

Spener is unusually explicit about the limits of family-supplied data. Many materials came from the people whose arms he treated and rested on their own testimony. He says he does not therefore guarantee them automatically.

He had received material from distinguished places whose truth he strongly doubted. He also notes the scarcity of older archival documents after wars and earlier failures to preserve monuments, and he distrusts old traditions in particular.

His governing rule is close to:

`received information can be transmitted without being fully believed`.

He invokes Curtius to explain that he sometimes records more than he believes because he will neither affirm what he doubts nor suppress what he has received.

This provides a very important historical distinction between:

- **provenance of a report**;
- **decision to print a report**;
- **editor's degree of belief**.

Those three must not be collapsed.

## 6. Plate order can diverge from text order because evidence arrives late

Spener explicitly says the engraved tables are even more disordered than the chapter sequence. Some shields appear on early plates although their explanations were postponed to later books because necessary supporting material was still missing when the relevant text location was reached.

The index repairs this `ataxia`.

This is a direct historical example of:

`plate production schedule ≠ textual exposition schedule ≠ evidence-readiness schedule`.

It strongly supports the repository-wide rule that plate history and text history can diverge even inside one authorial production process, before later rebinding or publisher reuse is considered.

## 7. Tincture encoding is an information-design choice

Spener explains that his copperplates use the French method of dots and different directions of lines to encode metals and colours, rather than the German habit of marking colours by initials. Where figures are too small for those line systems to distinguish tinctures adequately, the reader must fall back on the printed verbal explanation.

This gives a seventeenth-century actor-side statement of the same problem later diagnosed in the 1772 Weigel/Raspe retrospective:

`monochrome engraving`
→ `surrogate coding of colour`
→ `coding works only above a threshold of graphic scale`
→ `text becomes the fallback when the image cannot carry the distinction`.

The key point is not that one encoding is universally superior. It is that **visual scale, coding system and adjacent prose form one information architecture**.

This substantially deepens the 1772 evidence because the relation between figure size and colour encoding is already technically articulated in Spener.

## 8. Printed proofs circulate before publication for correction

Spener says that, to avoid giving offence or misrepresenting disputed claims, he sent a large part of the printed sheets in advance to distinguished men and delegates at the Imperial Diet, asking to be warned in time if anything required correction.

This adds a production-stage verification loop:

`research / submissions`
→ `composition`
→ `printed sheets`
→ `external review by informed political/genealogical actors`
→ `correction if warning arrives in time`
→ `publication`.

The important material fact is that the object circulating for review is not merely a manuscript letter. It is **printed matter before final closure of the work**.

This is unusually close to a proof-correction network and provides an early control for later serial correction practices.

## 9. Corrections and additions enter paratextual machinery

Spener says that errors noticed during rereading were entered in the first index, together with additions sent meanwhile by distinguished correspondents. The index is therefore not only a navigation tool; it can become a **late correction and update layer**.

This produces another temporal stack:

`main text / plates already fixed`
→ `new error detection or newly arrived information`
→ `index carries corrections/additions`.

A nominally final printed volume can thus contain several temporal states of the knowledge-making process at once.

## 10. The project remains explicitly open to future submissions

Spener says that he still has many arms in hand that he has begun to describe but cannot yet treat to his satisfaction; in some cases shields survive while helmet ornaments are missing. He asks interested parties to send the necessary information to him **within six months** so that it can be inserted into the next part.

He gives a concrete example: Württemberg materials arrived only when insertion into the current work was no longer possible, so they were reserved for the following book.

This is direct evidence for an **open update window tied to print deadlines**.

The project has a temporal architecture:

`submission deadline`
→ `current volume cutoff`
→ `late arrival`
→ `defer to next publication unit`.

That is structurally very close to serial publication systems later used by calendars and supplements.

## 11. Named intermediaries form a distributed verification network

Spener thanks specific helpers who supplied and checked information. Especially important is the description of Anton Schott, a Saxon electoral councillor and delegate at Regensburg: Schott obtained information from other delegates concerning their princes and **offered Spener's sheets to them for inspection**.

The information network therefore contains at least:

`families / courts`
→ `officials, secretaries, delegates, scholars`
→ `Spener`
→ `printed sheets returned into the same political network for checking`.

This is not a one-way collection process. It is a **circulatory verification network**.

Spener also credits Gebhard von Alvensleben with a major share of additions and emendations, and Johann Moth with information on the Danish royal family and nobility. The finished book is thus materially dependent on distributed expert and courtly infrastructures.

## 12. Stronger long-run genealogy of the corpus problem

The repository can now replace a thinner sequence with a more discriminating one.

### 1653 Fürst

A fair-catalogue notice reportedly solicits newly granted arms from readers in order to complete the inherited Siebmacher corpus.

### 1680 Spener

Direct primary text shows a full workflow:

`inherit Siebmacher/Fürst baseline`
→ `receive family/court submissions`
→ `search independently`
→ `compare and correct inherited errors`
→ `state source provenance and degrees of confidence`
→ `encode tinctures visually + explain textually where scale fails`
→ `circulate printed sheets for checking`
→ `use indexes for late corrections/additions`
→ `defer incomplete or late cases to a future volume`.

### 1734 Köhler

Köhler's preface, presently controlled through later quotation, reports exact comparison of five Helmers copies that differed in the condition and number of their arms because of removal, substitution, correction, filling and addition.

### 1772 Weigel/Raspe retrospective

The publisher retrospectively diagnoses older large armorials as suffering from excessive compression, unstable tincture coding, insufficient descriptive space and the fact that coats of arms change over time; Köhler's 1723 calendar is presented as a remedy.

The long-run claim can now be stated much more precisely:

> **The armorial was maintained through interacting infrastructures of inherited plate stock, distributed information intake, source checking, graphic encoding, proof correction, indexing and serial supplementation. Its instability was not merely a defect of individual copies; it was built into the problem of representing a changing heraldic world through reusable print matrices.**

This is stronger than a generic history of increasingly complete Wappenbücher.

## 13. Analytical consequences for the frontispiece project

The frontispiece / plate-stock project should now track not only `matrix movement` but at least five coupled systems:

1. **material stock** — copperplates, impressions, replacements, altered matrices;
2. **information intake** — families, courts, correspondents, fairs, archives, scholarly searches;
3. **verification** — comparison against inherited Wappenbücher, independent evidence, proof circulation, explicit doubt;
4. **information design** — scale, tincture coding, image/text division of labour, index architecture;
5. **publication timing** — what was ready, what arrived late, what could still be corrected, what had to be deferred.

A visual reference object is produced only where these systems intersect.

This also reframes `seriality`. Serial publication is not merely repeated annual delivery. It is one way of coping with the fact that information and corrections arrive **after graphic and textual components have already begun to solidify**.

## Evidence guards

1. CAMENA presents the 1717 second-edition text while the preface dates itself to 1680. Use it as a direct textual control of Spener's programme, but inspect the 1680 image before making claims about edition-specific punctuation, lineation or possible second-edition alteration.
2. Spener's use of Siebmacher/Fürst as `fundamenti loco` does not establish a direct institutional succession between their publishing houses and Spener's publisher.
3. Family or court communication is not equivalent to verified truth; Spener explicitly distinguishes receipt from belief.
4. His proof-circulation network should not be labelled modern peer review without qualification. It is better described as pre-publication correction by informed correspondents and political/genealogical actors.
5. The mismatch between plate and text order is documented within Spener's own production. Do not automatically infer later rebinding from such mismatches in other books without copy-level evidence.
6. Spener's tincture discussion establishes a relation between scale and encoding but does not prove that every later publisher understood the problem identically.
