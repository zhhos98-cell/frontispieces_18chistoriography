# Paul Fürst's publisher `Protestatio`: a live armorial update network — 1657 primary control, 26 August 2026

## Status

Direct primary inspection of the publisher's `Nota. Bericht und respective Protestatio deß Verlegers / An den höflichen Leser`, preserved with the **1657 first part** of *Das erneuerte und vermehrte Teutsche Wappenbuch*, reveals an unusually explicit production account. Fürst describes the Wappenbuch not as a closed inherited plate corpus but as a continuously updated system linking:

- inherited Siebmacher copperplates;
- newly copied heraldic originals;
- court / Reichskanzlei expertise;
- hired artists and copper engravers;
- printed fair-catalogue solicitations;
- daily incoming submissions;
- correction notices from readers;
- acknowledged specialist contributors;
- subsequent parts held open for late-arriving material.

This is now `verified_primary` at the level of the inspected eight-page notice. The strongest control copy used here is the Stadtbibliothek Koblenz / dilibri scan of the notice; UB Freiburg and SLUB/DDB independently identify the same text within the 1657 first-part publication environment (VD17 `23:300403H`).

## I. The programme begins as continuation plus correction plus augmentation

Fürst opens by saying that high-ranking persons had for many years urged him to continue the well-known Siebmacher armorial, to which no substantial addition had been made for about fifty years, and to enlarge it with:

- still-missing old arms;
- arms improved/corrected since then;
- new arms.

He says he had resolved about **four years earlier** to undertake this expensive and laborious publishing project and had begun around the recently held Reichstag at Regensburg.

This internal chronology fits the 1653 transfer and solicitation evidence: the 1657 notice retrospectively locates the programme's start approximately four years earlier.

Safe formulation:

`continuation` in Fürst's vocabulary is already a mixed operation of `recovery + correction + new acquisition`.

It is not equivalent to simple reprinting.

## II. Imperial-court mediation and copying from originals

Fürst then describes assistance from the imperial-court environment. The court painter **Frans Luycx / Leux** is identified as a particularly supportive participant. Through his artistic personnel, several hundred arms were, at Fürst's expense, carefully **copied and drawn after originals** (`nach den Originalien fleissigst copiren und aufreissen lassen`). Fürst says the copper engravers he employed then followed these copies, and he stresses the care taken to correct them.

This gives a concrete production chain:

`original heraldic representation`
→ `court/artistic mediation`
→ `drawn/copy model`
→ `copper engraver`
→ `printed armorial image`.

Do not collapse the copied model and the engraved matrix into one object. The passage makes the mediating drawing stage explicit.

## III. Fair catalogues were used repeatedly to solicit missing and corrected arms

The next page is exceptionally important. Fürst says he had caused a request to be appended to the **Frankfurt catalogue for the second time**, asking lovers/knowers of noble heraldry to send him copies of arms that:

- were not contained in Siebmacher;
- or had since been improved/corrected.

He immediately connects this solicitation to plate ownership. He says he purchased the copperplates of the armorial **to this end**, so that he could advance the whole work toward the greatest possible completeness.

This is stronger than the previously recovered secondary report about the Leipzig autumn-fair catalogue of 1653. The primary notice demonstrates that solicitation was **repeated**, and that Fürst himself explicitly linked external information intake to the purpose of buying and revising inherited copper stock.

The infrastructure is therefore:

`purchase old plates`
+
`publish repeated calls for missing/corrected arms`
→
`receive external information`
→
`revise old stock + engrave additional material`
→
`move corpus toward greater completeness`.

The 1653 Leipzig notice should now be treated as one node in a broader fair-catalogue solicitation strategy, not an isolated advertisement.

## IV. Fürst explicitly says old Siebmacher copperplates were corrected

Fürst states that, in this spirit, he also had the old Siebmacher copperplates **improved and freed from their errors**, as far as those errors had been made known to him.

This is direct actor evidence for intervention in inherited matrices/plate stock. It changes the status of the older project-wide assumption that `same inherited copper stock` might mean stable graphic content.

The correct relation is:

`inherited plate ownership ≠ unchanged inherited plate state`.

Some old copperplates were actively corrected according to information reaching the publisher.

The notice does not identify, plate by plate, whether correction meant local reworking of a matrix, replacement of a plate, or another technical intervention. Use `old copperplates corrected/improved` at the actor-description level and require witness comparison for matrix-specific claims.

## V. Fürst builds correction into the publication cycle

Fürst asks readers to report anything still wrong in the arms or names and says that, once a **grounded/corroborated notice** (`gründliche Nachricht`) reaches him, he will have it corrected where possible.

He also says that for this purpose he has printed only a small number of copies on this occasion.

This is a striking feedback structure:

`publish limited batch`
→ `receive grounded corrections`
→ `correct where possible`
→ `continue production`.

The passage should not be modernised into a formal proof-stage system without further printing-house evidence. But it does show that Fürst understood correction as something that could continue after a publication state had already been exposed to readers.

## VI. The order of the book records the arrival order of information

Fürst explains why the many arms could not be arranged exactly according to the alphabet. While the work was under production, **changes and corrections to arms were being received from time to time**, and relevant material reached him at different times. He therefore followed a disposition/order communicated by the Imperial Chancery and other distinguished patrons.

This is direct evidence that layout/order is partly an artefact of information flow:

`arrival time of corrections/submissions`
→ `constraints on ordering`
→ `printed disposition`.

A non-alphabetical or seemingly irregular sequence can therefore encode production chronology rather than conceptual disorder.

That is highly relevant to any attempt to infer classification from plate sequence alone.

## VII. Fürst refuses to make the Wappenbuch a judicial authority

Because precedence and heraldic ordering were contentious, Fürst expressly protests that the ordering of families and arms is not intended to prejudice anyone's privileges, honour, rank, name or lineage. The book is not to be treated as a judicial decision. He characterises it instead as a **collection/assemblage of many arms copied from originals as they reached him**.

This is a major epistemic guard internal to the object itself:

`reference compilation ≠ legal adjudication`.

The publisher separates documentary/graphic aggregation from the authority to decide rank and precedence.

## VIII. Incoming material was continuous and exceeded current publication capacity

Fürst says **more and more arms were being sent to him almost daily**. Because the following new parts were already large, he had to leave some material aside for the moment.

In discussing the future fifth part, he again says that older and newer patrician/noble arms had arrived too late and that further ones were still coming in daily.

This means the corpus had a genuine queue:

`incoming submissions > immediate capacity of current part`
→ `defer late material`
→ `allocate to future part`.

The boundary between one part and another is thus partly a production cut through an open information stream, not necessarily an intellectual boundary in the heraldic universe.

## IX. The notice gives a submission protocol

Fürst repeats his earlier request in operational terms. Anyone who wishes to see a family/stem arm included should send it to him:

- genuine / exact (`echt`);
- with the **name** clearly indicated;
- and with the **region/landscape of origin or residence** clearly indicated.

This is more than a generic invitation to correspond. It is a rudimentary metadata specification for submissions.

The production pipeline can therefore be written:

`external arm image/info`
+
`name metadata`
+
`geographic provenance/residence metadata`
→
`publisher intake`
→
`comparison / ordering / artistic copying`
→
`engraving / correction`
→
`future armorial part`.

This is one of the clearest primary-source demonstrations in the repository that early modern graphic reference works were maintained through structured information intake.

## X. Named contributors expose the social network

Fürst explicitly thanks **Gabriel Bucelinus**, **Johann Wolfgang Fabricius**, and other lovers of heraldic matters for communicating a large number of coats of arms and offering further help. Frans Luycx / Leux appears earlier as an imperial-court artistic intermediary.

The Wappenbuch's production network therefore crosses several roles:

- publisher / plate owner — Paul Fürst;
- imperial court and Reichskanzlei channels;
- court painter / artistic mediator — Frans Luycx;
- specialist contributors — Bucelinus, Fabricius and others;
- hired copper engravers;
- dispersed readers/correspondents responding to fair-catalogue calls.

Authorship at the title level seriously understates the social machinery responsible for graphic content.

## XI. Revised long-run model

The earlier repository model can now be sharpened from four infrastructures to a live feedback loop:

### 1. inherited matrix reservoir
`old Siebmacher copperplates`

### 2. information intake
`court sources + originals + fair-catalogue respondents + specialist contributors + daily submissions`

### 3. mediation and verification
`copied drawings + Reichskanzlei disposition + grounded correction notices + publisher selection`

### 4. graphic production
`old plates corrected + engravers follow supplied drawings + new material engraved`

### 5. serialised release
`current part / limited batch / later parts`

### 6. reader feedback
`errors/names reported → grounded notice → further correction`.

So the corpus should be understood as a **recursive publication system**, not a one-way chain from manuscript information to finished plate.

## XII. Consequence for the 1723–1772 argument

This primary control materially deepens the later Nürnberg genealogy.

### ca. 1653–57 Fürst

The publisher describes old plates, new information, correction, original-copying, repeated solicitation, incoming submissions and future deferred parts as one integrated operation.

### 1734 Köhler

Köhler's later five-copy comparison detects material divergence within the Helmers armorial tradition: removals, substitutions, filled shields and additions.

### 1772 Weigel/Raspe retrospective

The publisher genealogy explicitly identifies recurrent change in coats of arms, alongside visual compression and description/tincture problems, as a reason older armorial formats require remedies and updating.

The strongest article-level formulation is now:

> **The Nürnberg heraldic print tradition did not merely preserve a plate archive. It maintained a changing graphic information system in which inherited matrices, external reports, original exemplars, specialist judgement, correction notices and serial publication were repeatedly recombined.**

This is stronger, and more historically specific, than `plate reuse` alone.

## XIII. Evidence guards

1. Date the inspected `Protestatio` to the **1657 first-part publication state / ca. 1657 internal horizon**. Its own chronology places the beginning of Fürst's project about four years earlier and speaks of the fifth part as future. Some later composite copies also preserve/repeat the notice; do not use the binding date of a later composite as the composition date of the statement.
2. The primary notice says Fürst had old Siebmacher copperplates improved/corrected. It does not tell us which individual matrices were reworked rather than replaced.
3. `nach den Originalien` establishes copying from objects Fürst or his intermediaries treated as originals; it does not automatically establish modern archival authenticity for every submitted coat of arms.
4. Fürst distinguishes grounded correction reports from arbitrary objections. Do not flatten all reader submissions into equally authoritative data.
5. The stated near-daily arrival of new arms is publisher testimony inside a promotional/defensive notice. Treat the existence of active intake as direct evidence; treat quantitative scale rhetorically unless correspondence/account records independently measure it.
6. The non-alphabetical order is explicitly connected to changing/corrected material arriving during production, but the Reichskanzlei/patron disposition also mattered. Do not reduce sequence to arrival chronology alone.
7. The notice's refusal of judicial authority is analytically important: printed arrangement should not be read automatically as a legally binding hierarchy.
