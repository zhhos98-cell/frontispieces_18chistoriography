# Winkelmann 1694 → 1697: long-range textual continuity to printed p.400 and a guardrail on the modern `1696` claim

Date: 2026-08-28
Status: STRONG TEXTUAL-STATE CONTINUITY CONTROL — PRE-PRINT MATERIAL MATCHES THE LATER FIRST EDITION DEEP INTO THE VOLUME; MODERN `1696` WORDING IS NOT AN INDEPENDENT SOURCE-STATE PROOF

## Result

The pre-publication Winkelmann material accessible to Leibniz was not merely an early introductory fragment.

Leibniz-Katalog ID 12220:

- shelfmark `LH 12, 1, 3 Bl. 123`;
- date `1694`;
- Akademieausgabe V `3100.108`;
- autograph short strip;
- ordering mark `Saxones sub Romanis`;
- source identified as Winkelmann, *Beschreibung der Fürstenthümer Hessen und Hersfeld*, Bremen 1697;
- exact later-print locator: **S. 400**.

The note concerns the claim that St Aegistus preached at Bardowick and was killed by unbelieving pagans, with supporting references to Henricus Hervordiensis, the Centuriators and Nicolaus Marschall.

Thus a Leibniz note assigned to 1694 can be textually matched by the editors to material appearing around p.400 of the 1697 publication.

This extends the pre-print continuity model dramatically:

`1694 accessible source material`
→ `later printed content not only at pp. 40–96 but also at p. 400`.

The source available before publication therefore appears to have represented a substantial portion of the eventual work rather than a small sample or opening fascicle.

---

## 1. Relation to the page-number evidence

ID 11623 already shows Leibniz's own source locators:

`p. 6 / p. 41 / p. 56 / p. 59 / p. 96`.

The editors' print comparison independently identifies strong correspondences at several of those same numbers in the later print tradition.

ID 12220 adds a second kind of control:

- Leibniz's surviving strip does **not** preserve an autograph `p.400` locator;
- modern critical cataloguing nevertheless matches its wording directly to final printed p.400.

So two independent phenomena are present:

1. **pagination continuity** where autograph source coordinates survive;
2. **textual continuity** where later editors can identify the printed descendant even without an autograph locator.

Together they support a substantial, structured pre-print source state.

---

## 2. The source-state was likely extensive

The evidence now spans:

- early antiquarian/ethnographic material near the beginning of the eventual work;
- material around final print pp. 40–96;
- material at final print p.400;
- separate dynastic/heraldic materials associated with Winkelmann's working projects.

This makes a model of one tiny borrowed excerpt less plausible.

The safer formulation is:

> Leibniz had access to a distributed excerpt reservoir derived from an already extensive Winkelmann Hessian Chronicle/Landesbeschreibung textual state.

Whether the reservoir was made directly from the whole manuscript, from several exchanged packets, or from a paginated intermediary copy remains unresolved.

---

## 3. Modern scholarship's `1696` wording does not independently prove a 1696 witness

Christian Presche (2018), discussing Landgrave Wilhelm IV, writes that Winkelmann looked back on the period `1696 in seiner hessischen Landesbeschreibung`.

However, the accompanying footnote cites only:

Johann Justus Winkelmann, *Gründliche und warhafte Beschreibung der Fürstenthümer Hessen und Herßfeld*, **Bremen 1697**, pp.275–276.

No manuscript shelfmark, proof, dated dedication, licence, correspondence item or 1696 edition/witness is supplied there.

Therefore repository rule:

`Presche 2018 wording "1696"`
≠
`independent primary proof of a 1696 textual witness`.

It may reflect composition dating known elsewhere, an editorial shorthand, or a simple date slip. Until its basis is recovered, do not use it as a source-state control.

---

## 4. Revised chronology

Current strongest chronology:

`1690` — Winkelmann/Hessian excerpts already referenced in Leibniz's working files;

`1691` — written Hessian Chronicle explicitly exists; Leibniz wishes to read the `geschriebene heßische Chronick selbst`; printing is expected to be delayed;

`1694` — Leibniz working notes preserve page-addressed Hessian Chronicle extraction and additional excerpts later identifiable far into the 1697 print, including material at final p.400;

`1697` — Bremen first edition publishes five parts with continuous pagination;

`1711` — later Bremen print/reissue;

`1754` — posthumous sixth part.

The 1694→1697 interval should therefore be treated as a **textual stabilization / publication transition**, not as a gap between an unrelated manuscript and a newly composed printed work.

---

## 5. Broader implication

The archive now preserves evidence for a source that is simultaneously:

- unpublished;
- extensive;
- page-addressable;
- excerptable;
- distributable across problem files;
- recognizably ancestral to the later print.

This is a stronger case of `addressability before publication` than the previous pagination result alone.

The important historical unit is not simply `manuscript` versus `print`, but a succession of **operational source states**.
