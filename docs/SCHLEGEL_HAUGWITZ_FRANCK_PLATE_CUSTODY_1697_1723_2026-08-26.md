# Schlegel–Haugwitz–Tentzel–Franck: manuscript/plate custody and delayed publication, 1697–1723

## Status

**High-value item-level control. Christian Schlegel's unpublished `Historiae Saxonicae Antiquioris Veritas ex Nummis Demonstrata` survives in Gotha as both fair copy and rough draft, while the eight folio copperplates made for the project followed the Haugwitz coin collection to Weimar in 1701. Salomon Franck's 1723 Weimar catalogue prints eight folio plates of the same collection and format. Martin Mulsow argues that these are very probably Schlegel's plates or close variants, but one numbering discrepancy prevents promotion to proven `same_copperplate_as`. This is an unusually clean early case of text history, plate history, object-collection history, and later host-publication history diverging.**

## 1. Surviving manuscript witnesses in Gotha

Mulsow identifies two direct manuscript witnesses in the Forschungsbibliothek Gotha:

- **FB Gotha, Ch. B 1740** — Christian Schlegel, *Historiae Saxonicae Antiquioris Veritas ex Nummis Demonstrata*; fair copy / Reinschrift, ca. 230 pages;
- **FB Gotha, Ch. B 1739** — rough draft / Rohentwurf of the same work.

The fair copy is not in Schlegel's disorderly hand; the rough draft preserves the working stage.

Related correspondence/provenance controls include:

- **FB Gotha, Ch. B 201**, fols. 344–529 — Schlegel's letters to Tentzel;
- **Stolbergische Bibliothek Wernigerode, Zm 37** — sixty letters from Tentzel to Schlegel;
- **FB Gotha, Ch. A 419** — a ca. 500-leaf correspondence volume containing, among other material, letters to Schlegel;
- **FB Gotha, Ch. A 1220–1226** — inventories of the gold collection, partly illustrated;
- **FB Gotha, Ch. A 1227 I–II** — thaler collection with images;
- **FB Gotha, Ch. A 1228** — bracteate collection, images only.

The manuscript therefore sits inside a much larger ecology of inventories, drawings, correspondence, and object cataloguing.

## 2. Haugwitz financed eight folio copperplates

Schlegel later states explicitly in *De nummis antiquis Gothanis* (p.116) that more than two hundred Saxon coins, from Conrad to Ernst and Albrecht, had been engraved:

- on **eight plates**;
- in **folio format**;
- while Schlegel was still in Dresden;
- **at Friedrich Adolph von Haugwitz's expense**;
- with Schlegel causing them to be engraved `multa cum accuratione`;
- intended eventually to appear together with a fuller history.

The key Latin control is Mulsow's note 149:

`... numero ultra 200 octo tabulis in forma folii adhuc Dresdae haerens Illustriss. Haugwizii sumptibus multa cum accuratione aeri incidi curavi, luci publicae aliquando cum prolixiori eorundem historia a me exponendos ...`

This establishes something stronger than a planned illustration programme:

> **The plates had a separate commissioned material existence, financed by a patron, while the historical text remained unpublished.**

## 3. Patronage collapse separated text from its intended visual apparatus

Haugwitz's political fall in 1697 ended the patronage environment in which Schlegel's ambitious Saxon `histoire métallique` could have appeared as a lavish illustrated work.

Mulsow's reconstruction is careful about exact plate completion date, but Schlegel's later testimony proves that the eight folio plates did in fact exist.

The project therefore fractured:

`historical manuscript / interpretation`
≠
`copperplate set`
≠
`coin collection`

rather than simply remaining an unpublished book manuscript.

## 4. 1701: collection and plates move to Weimar through Tentzel

The Haugwitz Saxon coin collection was sold in **1701**, after Haugwitz had left Saxony and withdrawn from office.

Mulsow states that Schlegel's friend **Wilhelm Ernst Tentzel mediated the sale** to Duke Wilhelm Ernst of Saxe-Weimar.

Schlegel's own later note says that the plates had gone together with the entire Saxon coin collection into the possession of the Weimar duke.

Thus the material route is:

`Dresden / Haugwitz patronage + Schlegel project`
→ `Haugwitz collection + eight copperplates`
→ **1701 Tentzel-mediated sale**
→ `Weimar court collection`.

Meanwhile the manuscript eventually remained in / came to **Gotha**.

This produces an explicit geographical split:

`text → Gotha`

while

`objects + copperplates → Weimar`.

## 5. Object collection still survives in Weimar

Mulsow reports that the Haugwitz coin cabinet remains part of the Weimar art collections and that specific coins used by Schlegel can still be identified there, although later acquisitions were intermixed.

He also notes two handwritten inventory volumes for the Haugwitz collection, each containing roughly one thousand coin/medal entries, focused especially on 1650–1700.

This means the evidence chain can, in principle, be reconstructed at three distinct material levels:

1. Schlegel's manuscript descriptions;
2. the actual surviving coins;
3. the printed/engraved images.

That is exceptionally valuable for testing representation error and object–surrogate correspondence.

## 6. 1723 Franck: eight folio plates reappear in print

Salomon Franck published at Weimar in 1723:

*Illustris numophylacii Wilhelmo-Ernestini, quod Vinariae fulget, rariores Bracteati nummique figuris aeneis expressi breviter explicati*.

Mulsow observes that:

- the book contains **eight copperplates**;
- they are in the same folio format Schlegel described;
- they depict the Haugwitz-derived collection now in Weimar;
- Franck was a Schlegel intimate/associate but not himself a major numismatic specialist;
- the printed work appeared one year after Schlegel's death;
- the accompanying explanation is sparse and does not name Schlegel.

Mulsow's conclusion is strong but explicitly cautious:

> Franck may not have commissioned an independent new set at all; he likely printed Schlegel's plates, or lightly modified variants of them.

## 7. Why `same copperplate` is not yet proven

Mulsow identifies a specific numbering mismatch.

In Schlegel's *Historiae Saxonicae ... Veritas*:

- the Eisenach hollow penny is no.1;
- the `FRIDERICUS CONES` bracteate is no.2.

In Franck 1723 an additional coin precedes them, shifting them to nos.2 and 3.

Possible explanations:

1. the plates and Schlegel's completed manuscript had already diverged before publication;
2. the Weimar collection gained additional coins after the manuscript was finished;
3. Franck modified an existing plate;
4. Franck used newly cut but closely derived plates.

Mulsow notes that altering existing copperplates would be technically awkward, but commissioning an entirely fresh set when usable copperplates already existed would also have been expensive.

Therefore current relationship vocabulary should be:

- `probable_plate_reuse_or_close_variant`;
- `shared_collection_and_plate_program`;
- `same_format_eight_plate_set`;
- NOT yet `same_copperplate_as`.

## 8. Candidate engraver: Johann Georg Mentzel remains a hypothesis

Mulsow notes that Schlegel and Tentzel were commissioning copperplate work at the same time and that Tentzel used **Johann Georg Mentzel of Leipzig**, a portrait engraver who also made coin images.

He therefore raises the possibility that the same coin draughtsman/engraver could have been involved in Schlegel's plates.

This remains a candidate, not an attribution.

Do not assign the Schlegel/Haugwitz plates to Mentzel without a signature, payment record, or direct correspondence.

## 9. A deeper operational point: plate and text could become non-congruent before publication

The numbering discrepancy matters methodologically.

The case shows that a visual apparatus commissioned for one textual argument can continue to develop after the text has stabilized:

`manuscript closes`
→ `collection changes / plate sequence changes`
→ `plate-text correspondence weakens`
→ `plates later acquire a new host with minimal explanation`.

This is an exact early control for the repository rule:

> **plate history ≠ text history ≠ collection history ≠ publication history.**

It also anticipates the later Quedlinburg case from the opposite direction. There, typography corrupts a visual distinction and a later edition repairs it. Here, the visual apparatus outlives and detaches from its intended interpretive text.

## 10. Schlegel–Tentzel: the plates were actively interpreted before 1723

Tentzel knew that the Haugwitz plates were in Weimar because he had mediated the collection sale. In his 1702 dispute with Schlegel he tells Schlegel to inspect the Meissen margraves on the Haugwitz bracteate plates and disputes Schlegel's reading of figures/headgear.

This is crucial:

- the plates were not dormant storage objects;
- learned disagreement operated through visual details on the plates;
- two scholars could perceive the same engraved figure differently;
- plate access and visual comparison were already part of scholarly controversy.

The case therefore contains both **material custody** and **interpretive contestation**.

## 11. Wider custody analogue: Morell copperplates

Mulsow notes a parallel fate for Andreas Morell's coin-image copperplates: they remained for a long time in Arnstadt, moved to Leipzig, were sold to Amsterdam, and were only printed decades later.

That parallel prevents us from treating Schlegel's case as an eccentric accident. Copperplates could have long independent afterlives across cities, owners, and publications.

## 12. Article-level consequence

This case now deserves a stronger role in the frontispiece project.

It supplies a pre-1700/early-1700 control in which historical evidence travels through four separable carriers:

`object collection`
↔ `historical manuscript`
↔ `engraved surrogate set`
↔ `later host publication`.

The carriers split geographically and institutionally, then partially recombine decades later.

That makes the later Weigel/Tyroff Clio plate less anomalous. A copperplate's meaning and evidentiary role need not remain attached to the text, patron, editor, or publication regime for which it was first created.

Highest-value future archival/digital checks:

- FB Gotha **Ch. B 1740** — fair copy;
- FB Gotha **Ch. B 1739** — rough draft;
- FB Gotha **Ch. B 201**, fols. 344–529 — Schlegel→Tentzel letters;
- Stolbergische Bibliothek Wernigerode **Zm 37** — sixty Tentzel→Schlegel letters, including late material around the plates;
- Zm 37 nos.59–61 — three Oppermann letters from Aug.–Sept. 1722 that Mulsow specifically flags as worth checking for Haugwitz collection/copperplate information;
- Weimar Haugwitz collection / its two handwritten inventory volumes;
- Franck 1723 eight folio plates, ideally plate-by-plate against manuscript numbering.

These are archival/item-level targets, not current user-download tasks unless stable digital carriers are located.
