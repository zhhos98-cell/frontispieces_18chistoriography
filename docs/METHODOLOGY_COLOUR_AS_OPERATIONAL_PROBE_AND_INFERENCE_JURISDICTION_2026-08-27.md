# Methodology — colour as operational probe and inference jurisdiction

Date: 2026-08-27  
Status: project-level method protocol

## 1. Purpose

This document converts the colour branch from a sequence of case studies into a reusable methodological protocol for the larger project.

The project does **not** treat colour as one stable historical object, one transferable technique, or one explanatory cause. It treats colour as a **probe** that reveals how historical knowledge moved through successive operations of perception, encoding, reproduction, classification and inference.

The governing question is therefore not simply:

> How was colour used?

but:

> **At what stage did colour enter an evidentiary operation, what work was it permitted to perform there, and how far could the resulting visible difference legitimately carry historical inference?**

Colour is methodologically useful precisely because apparently similar chromatic distinctions can occupy very different epistemic locations.

## 2. The basic separation: carrier, referent, operation, inference

Every colour case must first be decomposed into four non-equivalent components.

### A. `carrier`
Where is chromatic information physically or graphically borne?

Examples:
- pigment on one impression;
- hatch lines in a monochrome copperplate;
- a letter code;
- a verbal colour name;
- a legend/key;
- a coloured region in a synchronistic table.

### B. `referent`
What is the colour information about?

Examples:
- a heraldic tincture;
- the material appearance of an object;
- a human bodily feature;
- a political relation;
- a duration, boundary or historical rupture.

### C. `operation`
What practical or epistemic work is colour performing?

Examples:
- discriminate;
- encode/decode;
- reconstruct;
- classify;
- compare;
- navigate;
- mark relation;
- mark change;
- compress information;
- support memory;
- increase perceptual salience.

### D. `inference target`
What proposition is colour being asked to support?

Examples:
- object identity;
- semantic category;
- chronology;
- genealogy/origin;
- political relation;
- essential difference;
- causal explanation.

The central rule is:

`same visible difference` ≠ `same operation` ≠ `same inference entitlement`.

## 3. The colour-operation chain

For analytical purposes, colour cases should be placed on the following chain whenever applicable:

`source / observed variation`
→ `observation or measurement`
→ `discretisation / naming / binning`
→ `category`
→ `encoding or graphical externalisation`
→ `classification / comparison`
→ `historical inference`.

Each transition requires its own warrant. Success at an earlier stage does not automatically license the next.

A visible difference may therefore be:

- perceptually discriminable but difficult to discretise;
- discretised but only conventionally rather than naturally;
- useful for descriptive classification but weak for genealogy;
- graphically salient but evidentially redundant;
- mnemonically effective while adding no new factual warrant;
- historically recurrent without proving a causal mechanism.

This chain is the methodological core of the colour branch.

## 4. The jurisdiction test

For every case, ask five ordered questions.

### 1. Perceptual jurisdiction
Can the difference actually be seen, reported or reconstructed with sufficient stability?

Potential failure modes:
- poor observation;
- surrogate distortion;
- pigment degradation;
- scale limits;
- copy divergence.

### 2. Discretisation jurisdiction
Can continuous or variable appearances be partitioned into stable categories?

Potential failure modes:
- too many nuances;
- unstable category boundaries;
- nonstandard names;
- coarse binning;
- category dependence on local convention.

### 3. Classificatory jurisdiction
Can the resulting category reliably group the relevant units for the stated task?

Potential failure modes:
- category does not map stably onto the classified units;
- conventional distinctions are mistaken for natural divisions;
- descriptive convenience is promoted into ontology.

### 4. Evidentiary jurisdiction
What historical proposition may the classification support?

The project should distinguish at least:

`description`
< `identification`
< `relation`
< `genealogy/origin`
< `essential difference`
< `causal explanation`.

This ordering is not a universal hierarchy of truth. It is a practical reminder that stronger inference targets usually require additional warrant beyond perceptual visibility.

### 5. Display jurisdiction
What does making the distinction visually obvious add?

A graphical or chromatic display may:
- reduce search cost;
- aid overview;
- expose simultaneity;
- stabilize navigation;
- improve memory;
- make a relation perceptually immediate.

It may do all of these without increasing the evidentiary warrant of the underlying proposition.

The project therefore keeps separate:

`perceptual Evidenz / obviousness`
≠ `source warrant`
≠ `classificatory warrant`
≠ `causal warrant`.

## 5. The colour stress test

Any claim involving colour should be passed through the following protocol before promotion to a stronger evidence status.

1. **Locate the colour.** Is it on the source object, represented object, matrix, impression, copy, later intervention, textual description or analytical reconstruction?
2. **Identify the carrier.** Pigment, hatching, letter, word, key, table region, sample or other sign?
3. **Identify the referent.** Literal object property, semantic category, arbitrary relation, bodily feature, chronology, political grouping, etc.?
4. **Identify the operation.** What task becomes possible or easier because of the colour distinction?
5. **Test discretisation.** Are the categories stable, finite and reproducible, or coarse/continuous/local?
6. **Name the inference target.** Exactly what proposition is being drawn from the distinction?
7. **Test warrant relative to target.** Is colour direct evidence, redundant evidence, a fallback under data scarcity, a mnemonic aid, an encoding convention, or insufficient for the claimed inference?
8. **Check copy/edition invariance.** Is the relevant chromatic information stable across impressions or only present in particular copies?
9. **Separate actor claim from analyst comparison.** Did historical actors explicitly connect the domains, or is the cross-domain comparison ours?
10. **Apply a stop rule.** Do not generalise from perceptual clarity to historical truth without an independently controlled inferential bridge.

## 6. Why heraldic colour is a crucial control domain

Heraldry supplies a particularly clean comparison because its tinctures occupy a finite conventional category-space.

A typical chain is:

`tincture category`
→ `stable verbal name`
→ `letter/hatch code`
→ `decoder`
→ `reproducible monochrome transmission`.

The colour can be absent materially while chromatic information remains recoverable.

This creates a case in which **discretisation is largely supplied by convention before the image is read**. The main historical problems then shift to encoding, scale, decoder survival, copy completeness and correct reconstruction.

Human bodily colour in eighteenth-century geography/anthropography creates a different structure:

`visible continuum`
→ `uncertain observation`
→ `naming/binning problem`
→ `coarse categories`
→ `restricted classificatory and inferential warrant`.

The comparison is methodological rather than genealogical. It does not establish transfer from heraldry into anthropology.

## 7. Why synchronistic tables are a second control domain

Gatterer's synchronistic tables introduce another regime. Here pigment can bear a relation that is not literally chromatic.

The chain becomes:

`historical relation / duration / rupture`
→ `assigned graphical distinction`
→ `pigment on table`
→ `rapid visual comparison / overview`.

The principal methodological question is therefore not fidelity to an object's colour. It is whether the chromatic layer changes the reader's ability to inspect an already organised relation.

This makes the tables an ideal control for `display jurisdiction`:

- line geometry may carry the relation structurally;
- pigment may amplify salience;
- coloured and uncoloured copies may preserve different levels of perceptual convenience;
- copy variance does not necessarily entail variance in the underlying historical proposition.

Thus:

`added visibility` need not equal `added warrant`.

## 8. Negative cases are analytically productive

The project should record colour failure by the epistemic stage at which it occurs rather than under a single generic error field.

At minimum distinguish:

### `fact_proposition_overreach`
Colour alters or embellishes the fact being asserted.

### `observation_failure`
The colour cannot be reliably perceived or transmitted.

### `discretisation_failure`
Continuous variation cannot be stably converted into categories.

### `classification_failure`
The categories do not map securely onto the units being classified.

### `inference_target_overreach`
A valid visible/classificatory distinction is asked to bear a stronger proposition such as genealogy, essence or cause without sufficient warrant.

### `display_to_evidence_overreach`
Visual obviousness is mistaken for increased historical certainty.

These failures are non-equivalent and should remain separately queryable.

## 9. Cross-domain comparison without false genealogy

The method permits heraldry, historical tables, geography/Völkerkunde, natural-history colour standards and other domains to be compared at the level of operation while preserving historical discontinuity.

The correct comparative form is:

> Domain A and Domain B confront structurally comparable problems of encoding, discretisation, reproduction or inference.

The stronger historical claim:

> Domain A supplied the method used in Domain B.

requires independent evidence of transfer such as citation, teaching, shared personnel, institutional interaction, explicit vocabulary, instrument circulation or demonstrable borrowing.

Operational homology is not influence proof.

## 10. Relation to provenance-first evidence discipline

The colour protocol inherits the repository's general provenance rules.

Colour claims must preserve the distinction between:

`design identity`
≠ `matrix identity`
≠ `impression identity`
≠ `copy state`.

Especially for applied pigment:

`colour present in one digitised copy`
≠ `colour required by the edition`
≠ `colour applied at publication`
≠ `colour present in all impressions`.

Whenever possible, record the chromatic claim at the lowest level directly supported by evidence.

## 11. Minimal fields for canonical colour-method records

A colour-method assertion should, where relevant, encode:

- `epistemic_location`;
- `colour_role`;
- `colour_carrier`;
- `colour_referent`;
- `colour_operation`;
- `discretisation_status`;
- `classification_target`;
- `inference_target`;
- `warrant_relative_to_target`;
- `copy_or_edition_scope`;
- `failure_mode`;
- `evidence_basis`;
- `assertion_status`;
- `claim_limit`.

The purpose is not maximal metadata. The purpose is to prevent one observed chromatic fact from silently changing epistemic level as it moves through an argument.

## 12. Claim discipline

The project may safely make three levels of claim, provided the evidence is controlled.

### Level 1 — object-level
A particular copy, plate, text or table contains a specified colour carrier or colour statement.

### Level 2 — operation-level
The colour carrier performs a specified task such as decoding, classifying, navigating or marking relations.

### Level 3 — inference-level
Historical actors explicitly permit, restrict or contest a stronger inference from the chromatic distinction.

Movement from Level 1 to Level 3 requires an explicit evidentiary bridge. The visual fact alone is insufficient.

## 13. Current methodological formulation

The strongest project-level formulation is:

> **Colour is a methodological probe for the conversion of visible difference into historical knowledge. The analytical problem is to reconstruct the chain by which a difference is observed, discretised, encoded, classified and finally authorised — or refused — as evidence for a specific historical inference.**

A compact version:

> **When may a visible difference become a historical difference?**

An operational version:

> **Never ask only what a colour means. Ask where it enters the chain, what operation it performs, and what inference it is permitted to carry.**
