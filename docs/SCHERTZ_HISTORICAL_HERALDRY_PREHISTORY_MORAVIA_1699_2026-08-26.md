# Schertz 1699: an early Nürnberg model for treating a coat of arms through history and heraldic art

## Result

Carl Ferdinand von Schertz's *Mährisches Landes-Wappen Aus der Historie Und Herolds-Kunst Illuminiret* (Nürnberg, 1699) is a high-value prehistory comparator for the repository's emerging Gatterer question: **what does it mean to describe a coat of arms historically rather than merely blazon it?**

The title itself already joins `Historie` and `Herolds-Kunst`. More importantly, the surviving detailed transcription shows that Schertz structures the work around successive historical questions concerning one territorial coat of arms, the Moravian eagle.

The safe claim is not influence on Gatterer. It is that **Nürnberg print culture had, by 1699, a worked example of one armorial object being treated through a combined historical and heraldic procedure.**

## I. Institutional and bibliographic control

The Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek authority record for Carl Ferdinand von Schertz identifies him as a historian and lists *Mährisches Landes-Wappen* among his works.

A detailed modern transcription identifies the 1699 Nürnberg edition as:

Carl Ferdinand von Schertz, *Mährisches Landes-Wappen Aus der Historie Und Herolds-Kunst Illuminiret*, Nürnberg, in Verlegung Johann Ziegern, gedruckt bey Christian Sigmund Froberg, 1699.

The transcription also points to digitized copies at the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, SLUB Dresden and the Austrian National Library. Those institutional scans remain the target for direct page-level verification.

## II. Schertz defines one Wappen as the target of historical inquiry

In the prefatory material Schertz states that the present Moravian territorial arms are the **`Ziel`** toward which his description is directed.

That object-centred formulation matters. The work is not simply a general history of Moravia with heraldic decoration added. It organizes historical material around one visual-political object.

The basic operation is therefore:

`given territorial Wappen`
→ `questions about origin, meaning and use`
→ `historical and heraldic sources`
→ `explanation of the observed configuration`.

## III. Question structure: origin, form, meaning, use

The visible transcription preserves a question-driven architecture. Among the problems Schertz asks are:

- **where the Moravian arms came from**;
- what the configured arms mean;
- what the colours, eagle and chequered pattern signify;
- what the arms are used for;
- who shares or participates in the territorial arms.

This is more than a lexical guide to tinctures and charges. The armorial image becomes the organizing object for historical inquiry.

## IV. Source ecology

Schertz draws on a heterogeneous evidentiary field.

### Historical writers

He cites and evaluates authors such as Pessina, Paprocki, Stranský, Cromer, Hájek and others. He also criticizes writers who reproduce fables or errors about Moravian history.

### Heraldic authorities

He invokes heraldic propositions and specialist literature, including Böckler's heraldic work, to interpret formal properties of the arms.

### Seals and charters

When discussing the actual use of the Moravian arms, Schertz refers to a surviving margravial seal and to charters sealed with the territorial arms.

### Standards and ceremonial/public use

He discusses military banners, estates' standards, coronation/funeral display and contemporary printed use on resolutions of the territorial diet.

The object is therefore reconstructed through:

`historical narrative`
+ `heraldic doctrine`
+ `seal evidence`
+ `chartered/official occurrence`
+ `ceremonial and political use`.

## V. Historical criticism is already part of the procedure

Schertz repeatedly distinguishes plausible reconstruction from inherited story.

He complains that writers ignorant of local language/history can transmit gross errors, fairy tales or fables. He identifies places where previous authors have stumbled in describing the arms and explicitly frames some questions as difficult historical problems rather than matters settled by tradition.

The work therefore contains a rudimentary but real critical layer:

`reported origin story`
→ `comparison with authorities / local evidence`
→ `assessment of plausibility`
→ `historical armorial explanation`.

Do not translate this directly into Gatterer's later source criticism. The important point is narrower: historical description of arms already required evaluating competing accounts, not only reproducing them.

## VI. The arms as a historically used object

One particularly useful section asks what the Moravian territorial arms are presently used for.

Schertz does not answer only in symbolic terms. He follows the arms across media and institutions:

- seals of Moravian margraves;
- charters bearing those seals;
- banners and standards;
- estates' political display;
- royal coronation/funeral contexts;
- printed territorial acts/resolutions.

This converts the Wappen from an abstract emblem into a **distributed historical object with multiple occurrences**.

For the repository this is an important comparator. It suggests a period practice in which an armorial identity can be tracked across materially different witnesses without treating those witnesses as literally identical objects.

## VII. Relation to Gatterer's later method problem

The Gatterer controls now distinguish:

- source hierarchy;
- blazon;
- historical elucidation;
- history/historization of a coat of arms;
- criticism;
- graphic construction.

Schertz 1699 predates that explicit differentiation. Yet his book already combines several of the operations in practice:

`describe form`
+ `ask historical origin`
+ `interpret meaning`
+ `compare historical writers`
+ `track seals / charters / public uses`.

This makes Schertz a strong **prehistory comparator** for Gatterer's 1767 question of methods for describing arms historically.

The relation should be stated as:

`earlier operational example`
≠ `demonstrated intellectual source`.

No Schertz → Gatterer citation chain has yet been established.

## VIII. Why Nürnberg matters, cautiously

The work was printed in Nürnberg in 1699, decades before the Weigel/Köhler calendar ecology and Gatterer's later Nürnberg heraldic publications.

That geographic continuity is suggestive because the repository is already reconstructing Nürnberg as a dense production and information environment for genealogy, heraldry, numismatics and historical evidence.

But city continuity is not network continuity. Until publisher, ownership, citation or teaching links are found, use only the modest formulation:

> Nürnberg supported printed experiments in historically explicating heraldic objects well before the Gatterer generation.

## IX. Implication for the visual corpus

Schertz gives a useful early control for distinguishing four questions that a visual witness can provoke:

1. **What is it?** — formal heraldic description.
2. **Where did it come from?** — origin/history.
3. **What does it signify?** — symbolic/heraldic interpretation.
4. **Where and how was it actually used?** — witness/occurrence history.

The fourth is especially important. It anticipates, without being identical to, the repository's witness-occurrence discipline.

A coat of arms can recur on:

`seal ≠ charter ≠ banner ≠ printed act ≠ monument`.

The shared armorial identity does not erase the distinct material and institutional status of those occurrences.

## Evidence guards

1. Current detailed textual control comes from a modern transcription, not yet direct page-by-page inspection of an institutional scan.
2. The Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek establishes Schertz and the work bibliographically but does not by itself verify every transcribed sentence.
3. Do not infer Schertz → Gatterer influence from methodological similarity or shared Nürnberg publication geography.
4. Do not treat Schertz's historical criticism as equivalent to Gatterer's later formal source-critical taxonomy.
5. Claims about seals, charters, banners and official uses should be promoted to `verified_primary` only after the BSB/SLUB/ONB copy is directly inspected.
