# Fürst heirs' General-Register: post-print extensibility built into the Wappenbuch — ca. 1667 control, 26 August 2026

## Status

Direct inspection of the two-page `Bericht An den Leser` prefixed to the **General-Register over all five parts and the appendix** of the Fürst/Siebmacher Wappenbuch reveals a stronger continuation mechanism than the already controlled 1657 publisher `Protestatio`.

The notice is signed:

`Paul Fürsten Seel. hinterlassene Wittib und Erben.`

Because it indexes all five parts **and the appendix to the fifth part**, it belongs to the completed/posthumous publication state, conventionally dated with the series through **1667**. The appendix itself has a preface dated 10 December 1667 in independent bibliographic control. Until a separate dated title for the General-Register is identified, use **ca. 1667 / posthumous Fürst-heirs state**, not an exact day.

Primary copy directly inspected: Stadtbibliothek Koblenz / dilibri, General-Register section, two-page `Bericht An den Leser`, plus its separate `Errata corrigenda` section.

This evidence is `verified_primary`.

## I. The General-Register is not merely an index

The notice begins by explaining that the General-Register has now been made for the five parts of the Wappenbuch and the appendix to the last part. Names are entered as they stand above the individual shields and arms.

The heirs then immediately explain why names can be wrong or inconsistent. They distribute possible responsibility across the production chain:

- the **copper engravers**, of whom many different people worked on the project and some were occasionally inattentive;
- the impossibility of supervising them at all times, although much had been corrected;
- the **writer/copyist**, whose handwriting could make letters difficult to distinguish;
- genuine variation in how one and the same family wrote its name.

They give concrete examples of orthographic instability: `Maier / Mair / Mayer / Mayr / Meier / Meir / Meyer` and `Behaimb / Behaim / Beheim / Behem / Böheim`.

This is a production history of error, not merely a spelling apology.

The chain is explicit:

`submitted/copied name`
→ `writer's graphic form`
→ `engraver reading / engraving`
→ `printed label`
→ `General-Register entry`.

Error can enter at more than one stage, and later indexing inherits earlier graphic/textual states.

## II. Duplicate arms can be generated by distributed submissions

The heirs state that the **same coat of arms may have been entered two or three times** because it had been sent in by different persons belonging to one family, while the family name had not been written in the same way. They add that members of the same family may live in different regions and bear the same arms.

This directly exposes a failure mode of the information-intake system already documented in Fürst's 1657 notice:

`multiple independent submissions`
+
`orthographic variation`
+
`regional dispersion`
→
`publisher fails to recognise identity`
→
`duplicate armorial entries`.

This is important for witness/corpus work. Repetition in the printed Wappenbuch does not automatically imply deliberate iconographic redundancy, separate heraldic identity, or multiple plate traditions. It can arise from **entity-resolution failure inside a distributed information network**.

Use that modern phrase only analytically; the primary actors describe the practical causes rather than a formal data model.

## III. The heirs explicitly continue the first- and fifth-part correction promise

The notice says that the publisher remains by the standing offer found in the **Protestation of the first and fifth parts**: if something is still wrong in a coat of arms or name, then, once a `gründliche und deutliche Nachrichtung` is sent, it should be corrected as far as possible.

This is decisive for the previously only partially inspectable fifth-part `widerholte respective Protestation`.

We no longer need to infer continuity from its title alone. The later General-Register report explicitly tells us that:

`first-part Protestation`
→ `fifth-part Protestation`
→ `posthumous General-Register report`

all belong to a continuing correction regime.

The wording also preserves the epistemic filter already present in 1657: the publisher asks for **grounded and clear information**, not arbitrary correction claims.

## IV. Empty shields were deliberately reserved for future additions

The strongest passage follows. Anyone who still wishes to have his family/stem arms represented should report:

- under which `Rubric` or `Landschaft` the family belongs, e.g. Austrian, Franconian, Saxon, etc.;
- the clearly written **name**;
- the **colours**;
- the person's **estate/status**.

The heirs then explain that the arms can still be inserted because **at the end of almost every rubric in all the parts, empty shields had deliberately been left for this purpose** (`leere Schild ... zu dem Ende mit Fleiß gelassen worden`).

This is direct evidence for designed post-print extensibility.

The printed armorial is not conceptually closed when the sheets are printed. Its page architecture anticipates later intake:

`pre-engraved / reserved empty shield slots`
+
`future external submission`
→
`later armorial insertion`.

This should be distinguished from an ordinary blank page or accidental unused space. The heirs explicitly state the **purpose** of the empty shields.

## V. The index itself is designed for manuscript updating

The same sentence continues: after a new arm is inserted, the corresponding name can be entered **with ink** into the General-Register at its proper alphabetical location, or found at the end of the relevant rubric.

The system is therefore hybrid by design:

`printed plate architecture`
+
`reserved graphical vacancy`
+
`later heraldic addition`
+
`manuscript ink update to printed index`.

This is stronger than saying users annotated books. Here the publisher/heirs explicitly instruct readers how the printed reference system can be extended after production.

The proper conceptual distinction is:

`printed completion ≠ information-system closure`.

The Wappenbuch was built so that later information could be absorbed materially into both the visual corpus and its retrieval layer.

## VI. A separate `Errata corrigenda` centralises cross-part correction

The same General-Register package contains a separate two-page `Errata corrigenda` list. It is alphabetically organised by names and points back into the five-part system with Roman numerals for part numbers and leaf/page references; some entries use `a.` for the appendix.

Examples visible in direct inspection include references such as:

- `Halen V.197`;
- `Rechberg III.107`;
- `Scheckenda III.101`;
- `Volckendorff I.27`;
- entries marked `a.` for appendix locations.

The exact correction represented by each item should be established by comparing the list to the target armorial leaf and General-Register entry. Do not assume from the heading alone whether every entry gives a corrected spelling, an omitted index term, or another error class.

What is secure is structural:

`five parts + appendix`
→ `one retrospective General-Register`
+
`one cross-part Errata corrigenda layer`.

The finished multi-part corpus is therefore subjected to a later, centralised retrieval-and-correction pass.

## VII. Production error and user/correspondent correction form one loop

The 1657 Fürst notice already established:

- inherited plates corrected;
- new arms copied from originals;
- court and Reichskanzlei mediation;
- repeated fair-catalogue solicitations;
- almost daily incoming arms;
- reader correction reports;
- future parts held open for late-arriving material.

The heirs' General-Register now shows what that system looks like at the completed-series end:

1. distributed submissions have produced both additions and duplicate records;
2. engravers and copyists have introduced further textual error;
3. much has already been corrected, but residual errors remain;
4. the fifth-part Protestation has renewed the correction promise;
5. the General-Register creates a corpus-wide retrieval layer;
6. `Errata corrigenda` creates a corpus-wide correction layer;
7. deliberate empty shields preserve capacity for further visual additions;
8. manuscript ink additions keep the index synchronised with post-print changes.

This is a genuinely recursive reference apparatus.

## VIII. Consequence for the repository's object model

The evidence requires a distinction among at least six states that an edition-level record can obscure:

1. **matrix state** — old plate, corrected plate, new plate;
2. **printed armorial slot state** — occupied shield / deliberately empty reserved shield;
3. **submission state** — newly received, duplicate, corrected, unresolved identity;
4. **copy state** — whether a later insertion was actually made in this physical exemplar;
5. **index state** — original printed index vs manuscript-added name;
6. **retrospective correction state** — item included in the General-Register `Errata corrigenda` or later correction layer.

This matters directly for later Weigel/Köhler calendar and Wappenbuch witnesses. A printed empty frame or shield can be **functional reserve capacity**, not missing content. A handwritten addition can be part of a publisher-sanctioned update workflow rather than idiosyncratic ownership annotation. Duplicate graphic entries can be intake artefacts rather than independent iconographic witnesses.

The safest data relation vocabulary now includes:

`reserved_for_future_entry`
`postprint_inserted`
`manuscript_index_added`
`duplicate_submission_candidate`
`publisher_correction_requested`
`retrospective_errata_reference`.

These should be used only where copy- or passage-specific evidence supports them.

## IX. Long-run historiographical significance

The Fürst sequence can now be stated more precisely:

### ca. 1653
`acquire inherited Siebmacher plates + solicit new heraldic information through fair catalogues`.

### 1657 first-part state
`copy from originals + correct inherited plates + accept grounded corrections + receive continuing submissions + defer late material to future parts`.

### fifth-part state, ca. 1665
`widerholte Protestation` explicitly renews the publisher's correction regime, as later confirmed by the General-Register report.

### ca. 1667 / posthumous heirs state
`cross-part General-Register + cross-part Errata corrigenda + reserved empty shields + manuscript index-updating instructions`.

The Nürnberg heraldic publication system therefore moved through **intake, mediation, engraving, serial release, retrospective indexing, correction, and designed post-print extension** without a hard boundary between production and use.

For article prose, a compact formulation is now defensible:

> The Fürst Wappenbuch was not merely revised between editions. Its publishers designed the multi-part printed object to remain corrigible and extensible after printing: correspondents could supply grounded corrections and new arms, empty shield slots had been deliberately reserved for additions, and the printed General-Register could be synchronised by manuscript entries in ink.

## X. Evidence guards

1. Date the General-Register report conservatively as **ca. 1667 / posthumous Fürst-heirs state** unless a separate dated title or colophon for the register itself is controlled. Its inclusion of the appendix and widow/heirs signature places it after Paul Fürst's death and after the fifth-part expansion; independent copies describe the five parts + appendix complex as 1655–1667.
2. `leere Schild ... mit Fleiß gelassen` proves intentional reserve capacity at the actor-description level. Do not claim that every blank shield in every surviving copy served this function without a page-level check.
3. The report says arms `can` still be inserted. It does not prove that every copy owner or publisher actually filled the reserved slots.
4. The instruction to add a name `mit Dinten` to the Haupt-Register is direct evidence for sanctioned manuscript updating, but each surviving handwritten index addition still needs copy-level attribution.
5. Duplicate arms are explained as possibly arising from different family members' submissions, name variation and regional dispersion. Do not force every duplicate in the corpus into that cause.
6. The `Errata corrigenda` exists and is cross-part. Its item-by-item correction semantics require comparison with the referenced leaf and register entry.
7. The heirs distinguish residual error from already completed correction. Do not read the presence of an errata layer as evidence that the work was regarded as generally unreliable.
