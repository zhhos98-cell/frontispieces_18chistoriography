# Winckelmann 1684 Oldenburg Horn — carrier firewall and direct-image boundary

Date: 2026-08-30  
Status: **FULL-SCAN CARRIER AUDIT COMPLETE; MAIN WUNDERHORN IMAGE IN THE DIGITIZED COPY IS A 1912 SCHOHUSEN TRACING, NOT AN ORIGINAL 1684 IMPRESSION; 1696 DEPENDENCE REMAINS STRONG BUT NOT DIRECTLY PROVEN; BROAD AND DIRECT-IMAGE RESEARCH CLOSED AT THE CARRIER LIMIT.**

## Result

The 1684 Oldenburg Horn branch is closed at its present evidentiary limit.

The historically significant chain remains controlled:

`physical Horn access`
→ `material source criticism`
→ `drawing / printed surrogate`
→ `reported heraldic discrepancy`
→ `1696 Museum Regium / Tab. XVI afterlife`
→ `1807–08 retrieval of Winckelmann as authoritative image source`
→ `literary threshold reuse`.

The full Landesbibliothek Oldenburg scan has now been locally audited. It resolves the carrier question that formerly sat under `UA-002`: **the digitized copy does not preserve a genuine 1684 impression of the main Oldenburg Wunderhorn woodcut.** Its principal Wunderhorn foldout is a twentieth-century substitute, explicitly labelled as a tracing after the original woodcut by Fr. Schohusen in 1912.

Therefore this scan can close the carrier-identification problem but cannot supply the missing direct 1684 print state needed for a defect-level 1684 ↔ 1696 comparison.

## 3. Comparison with Jacobaeus 1696 Tab. XVI

A direct first-edition image of Jacobaeus, *Museum Regium* (Copenhagen, 1696), Tab. XVI, `Cornu Oldenburgicum`, was recovered from a Sotheby's copy of the 1696 edition.

Visual comparison against the 1912 Schohusen tracing shows strong broad concordance:

- the same leftward narrow terminal and rightward monumental cup orientation;
- the same strongly curved lower body;
- the same architectural/turreted organization at both ends;
- the same broad segmentation of the body into decorated architectural and heraldic zones;
- a closely comparable profile view rather than an arbitrary modern display angle.

This materially strengthens the proposition that the 1696 plate belongs to the same seventeenth-century visual tradition as the lost 1684 woodcut.

However, **it does not promote `1696 derived_from 1684` to direct-print proof**, for two reasons:

1. the only surviving image of the 1684 main Wunderhorn in this copy is a 1912 tracing, not the 1684 impression itself;
2. Jacobaeus had access to the Horn in the royal collection after its transfer to Copenhagen, so a similar profile could in principle have been redrawn from the object or another intermediary.

Final relation:

`1696_TAB_XVI ↔ 1684_visual_tradition = STRONG_DIRECT_VISUAL_CONCORDANCE_USING_SECOND_ORDER_1684_PROXY`

`1696_DERIVED_FROM_1684_PRINT = STRONG_SCHOLARLY_REPORTED / NOT DIRECTLY PROVEN BY THIS CARRIER`

No defect-level or plate-to-block dependency claim is licensed.

## 4. Comparison with the surviving Horn

The Royal Danish Collection currently preserves the Oldenborg Horn at Rosenborg Castle and provides modern object photography.

The 1912 tracing and the 1696 plate are recognizably representations of the same object type and preserve major object-level structures: the large architectural cup, the narrow turreted terminal, the curved body and densely articulated surface programme.

But the modern object photographs are views of the surviving three-dimensional object, while the historical images are selected profile constructions. Orientation and visible side-detail therefore vary. The full-scan audit does not justify treating every visible discrepancy as a seventeenth-century representational alteration.

Safe relation:

`historical visual tradition ↔ surviving object identity = SECURE`

`individual detail discrepancy mechanism = OPEN unless controlled by a genuine 1684 impression or object-history evidence`.

## 5. Heraldic discrepancy — what the full scan does and does not settle

Gitte Kjær's object-history study reports that Winckelmann's representation shows Oldenburg heraldic bars/arms absent from the surviving object and that this feature propagated through later representations/cataloguing traditions.

The full scan **does not falsify that report**, but it changes the direct-image evidentiary status.

Because the principal Wunderhorn image in this copy is the 1912 Schohusen tracing:

`HERALDIC DISCREPANCY AS A FEATURE OF WINCKELMANN'S VISUAL TRADITION = SCHOLARLY CONTROLLED`

`HERALDIC DISCREPANCY AS DIRECTLY OBSERVED ON AN EXTANT 1684 IMPRESSION IN GE_IX_B_70_A = NOT AVAILABLE`

The tracing visibly carries heraldic/architectural detail, but any disputed bars must not be attributed to the surviving 1684 print state from this carrier alone. The tracer may have preserved the original faithfully, but that is still one transmission step removed.

## 6. Material-source-criticism chain remains secure

Existing controls remain valid because they do not depend on the 1912 replacement image:

- Winckelmann's 1684 argument uses object-specific inscription/break details to infer that Happel had not seen the Horn;
- Happel's contemporary response acknowledges Winckelmann's superior in-loco/material access;
- modern object history reports that Winckelmann had the Horn in his home for several weeks for drawing/engraving preparation.

Thus:

`DIRECT OBJECT ACCESS AS AN EPISTEMIC WARRANT = CONTROLLED`.

The absence of the original woodcut impression in this particular copy does not weaken that source-critical chain.

---

## 7. 1807–08 long-range reuse remains controlled

The existing correspondence/research chain records that Göttingen librarian Beneke supplied `den Winkelmann` in response to a request for a good Horn image because it contained `die beste Abbildung`; modern scholarship identifies Winckelmann's image as the model for the 1808 *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* threshold/title copper.

This relation is not endangered by the 1912 carrier problem because nineteenth-century actors were consulting historical exemplars, not the later Oldenburg replacement sheet.

Functional shift remains:

`antiquarian object surrogate`
→ `authoritative visual reference`
→ `literary threshold apparatus`.
