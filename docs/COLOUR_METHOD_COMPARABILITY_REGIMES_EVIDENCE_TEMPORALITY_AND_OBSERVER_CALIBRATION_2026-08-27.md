# Colour method — comparability regimes, evidence temporality, and observer calibration

Date: 2026-08-27  
Status: Wilson-informed third web sweep  
Scope: Göttingen / German-speaking learned world, c. 1770–1805; later scholarship used for reconstruction and source control

## 0. Why this file exists

The previous two sweeps moved the colour project from a downstream chain —

`visible difference → discretisation → classification → inference`

— to a broader Wilson-informed chain:

`material affordance / constraint → legibility → selection → discretisation → observer formation → operation → inference jurisdiction`.

The Prange sweep then showed that a material colour standard could be locally available, explicitly discussed in Göttingen, and actually used in some domains without becoming the default apparatus for every inquiry involving colour.

This third sweep sharpens the consequence:

> **standardisation is only one technology for producing comparability.**

Eighteenth-century scholars could make observations comparable through at least four different regimes:

1. material reference standards;
2. accredited and aggregated witness testimony;
3. explicit calibration of the observer and the observer's perspective;
4. persistent specimens combined with autopsy, provenance and cross-comparison.

The historical question should therefore not be phrased as a teleology from unstable words to increasingly precise standards.

A better question is:

> **Which comparability regime did a task select, what did that regime make comparable, and what inference did it authorize?**

A second, related question is:

> **What makes historical evidence retro-standardisable?**

This matters directly for colour. A preserved coloured sheet, hair sample, calculus, mineral or pigment can potentially be reobserved and compared with a later standard. A traveller's decades-old verbal statement that a population was `brown`, `olive`, `blackish`, or `reddish` cannot be retroactively converted into a numbered swatch unless the original referent or a materially adequate surrogate survives.

---

## 1. Comparison is prior to classification, but comparability is historically produced

The project should distinguish `comparison` from `comparability`.

Objects or reports do not become comparable merely because a historian places them next to each other. A comparison requires a carrier, protocol or stabilising operation that decides what counts as the same dimension of difference.

Examples from the present sweep:

- Prange: colour is made comparable through material swatches + chart/sample numbers;
- Meiners: population-level traits are made comparable through long-duration, large-N, convergent witness reports;
- Forster: heterogeneous travel reports are made comparable by identifying and correcting for observer-specific perspective;
- Blumenbach: bodies/specimens are made comparable through direct observation, provenance control, collections, images, and supplementation by credible reports.

Thus:

`available observations`
→ `comparability regime`
→ `commensurable units`
→ `classification / ordering`
→ `inference`.

The project should not assume that one regime is intrinsically more scientific than another. It should reconstruct what each regime solves and what each regime cannot solve.

## 2. Regime A — Prange: object calibration by material reference

The previous sweep established Christian Friedrich Prange's *Farbenlexicon* (1782) as a material comparison apparatus:

`object`
→ `visual matching to a manufactured swatch`
→ `Tafel + Nummer`
→ `portable record`
→ `later recovery / reconstruction`.

The key methodological properties are:

- the referent is present at capture time;
- the reference standard is also materially present;
- the result can be compressed into a portable code;
- later users can in principle recover the comparison relation;
- reproducibility depends on the material consistency of the standard itself.

Beckmann's Göttingen review is crucial because it separates the idea of a useful standard from the problem of manufacturing identical standards across copies. A standard therefore needs provenance and material history.

The Prange/Widenmann comparison further established:

> `more granular standard` ≠ `more usable standard`.

Prange's 4,608 samples could impose an excessive matching burden. Widenmann's mineralogical reduction to a task-specific set demonstrates that successful standardisation often requires compression rather than maximal discrimination.

## 3. Regime B — Meiners: population comparability through accredited testimony

### 3.1 Primary methodological statement, 1790

Johann Friedrich Blumenbach's *Beyträge zur Naturgeschichte*, first part (Göttingen, 1790), reproduces and answers a critique by Christoph Meiners. Blumenbach Online provides the first-edition text and identifies the relevant exchange on pp. 62–78.

Primary digital text:
- Johann Friedrich Blumenbach, *Beyträge zur Naturgeschichte. Erster Theil* (Göttingen: Dieterich, 1790), Blumenbach Online, item 000111:
  https://blumenbach-online.de/item/000111
- HTML transcription:
  https://blumenbach-online.de/fileadmin/wikiuser/Daten_Digitalisierung/Digitalisate_html/Texte/000111/000111.html

Blumenbach identifies the material he quotes as Meiners's response to his anthropological collection / skull work. The original Meiners remarks were printed in the *Göttingisches historisches Magazin*, vol. VI, no. 3, pp. 406–408.

Meiners explicitly states that the study of humankind cannot be based exclusively on the author's own direct inspection. Where personal experience ends, the investigator must, like historians generally, rely on reports by capable and credible witnesses.

More importantly, Meiners defines what makes such witnesses epistemically valuable. He appeals to concordant testimony by reliable observers who had kept many hundreds or thousands of members of the same people before their eyes for years and were therefore able to distinguish individual variation from what appeared common to the population.

Blumenbach immediately says he agrees with this paragraph wholeheartedly: concordant testimony by reliable observers can say more than one or a few skulls of uncertain provenance.

This is not an incidental concession to imperfect evidence. It is a **witness-accreditation protocol**.

Meiners's implied criteria are:

- observer competence / reliability;
- long duration of exposure;
- large population sample;
- repeated observation rather than a single encounter;
- capacity to separate individual variation from recurrent population traits;
- convergence among independent or multiple observers.

The resulting workflow is:

`population`
→ `long-duration / large-N exposure`
→ `accredited observer`
→ `description abstracting recurrent traits`
→ `convergence across reports`
→ `population-level comparability`
→ `historical / genealogical inference`.

### 3.2 Consequence for the Prange question

This makes the question “why did Meiners not simply use a colour chart?” less straightforward.

A material colour chart can precisely stabilise an encounter between one observer and one presently visible body. But Meiners's evidentiary unit is often not one body. It is a population regularity reconstructed from geographically and temporally dispersed reports.

For a Prange-like apparatus to transform this evidence architecture, it would have to enter **upstream at the witness's moment of observation**.

A Göttingen author receiving a thirty-year-old report cannot retroactively turn `brown` into `Tafel x, Nr. y` once the observed persons are absent.

Therefore:

> **standardisation must enter at evidence capture unless the referent persists and can be reobserved.**

This is the central rule of `retrostandardisability`.

### 3.3 Meiners did not treat colour as a sufficient single classifier

The same 1790 exchange is important for interpreting the 1792 essay on human colour.

Meiners says that anyone wishing to divide humanity may do so by shades of colour, heads, noses, ears, etc., but that classifications resting on individual bodily marks are less secure and less fruitful than classifications attending to all known distinguishing marks of the external and internal human being.

This prevents a misleading reconstruction in which Meiners is treated as simply making colour a self-sufficient racial metric. His 1792 colour essay should instead be read as testing colour's causal, genealogical and classificatory capacity inside a broader anthropology.

His 1792 opening questions — whether and how far colour is a secure sign of races/descent, how many principal colours exist, and whether some function as ground colours — belong to this problem of **inference jurisdiction**, not merely nomenclature.

## 4. Regime C — Forster: calibrating the observer rather than eliminating perspective

Georg Forster provides a third solution to the comparability problem.

In the preface to *Reise um die Welt*, Forster says that two travellers rarely see the same object in the same way; each gives an account according to individual sensibility and mode of thought. Consequently, one must first become acquainted with the observer before making use of the observer's remarks.

Accessible text:
- Georg Forster, *Reise um die Welt*, preface, reproduced in *Vom Reisen*:
  https://www.legimi.de/e-book-vom-reisen-georg-forster%2Cb338666.html

The principle is methodological rather than merely literary:

`report` is not separable from `observer metadata`.

Forster makes the point even more explicitly in “O-Taheiti”. Madhuvanti Karyekar's open-access discussion reproduces Forster's language: national character, national politics, education, climate and other factors act like invisible membranes in the eye that refract rays differently. The philosopher must calculate what colours these incorporeal spectacles produce and combine the differently coloured results into a clearer truth.

Secondary/open scholarly source:
- Madhuvanti Karyekar, “Comparative Anthropology in Travel Literature: Georg Forster's O-Taheiti (1779),” *Colloquia Germanica* 46.3 (2013):
  https://elibrary.narr.digital/xibrary/cg201330211

This is extremely important for the present colour method because Forster uses a colour/optical metaphor to describe **observer calibration**.

His solution is not:

`subjective report → discard`.

Nor is it:

`subjectivity → impossible truth`.

It is:

`observer position / task / formation`
→ `selective seeing and refraction`
→ `report`
→ `make observer characteristics explicit`
→ `compare heterogeneous reports`
→ `perspectival correction`
→ `qualified synthesis`.

### Object calibration vs observer calibration

Prange attempts to stabilise the **object-reference relation**.

Forster attempts to stabilise the **report-observer relation**.

These are different comparability technologies.

A field dependent on mobile witnesses can therefore pursue comparability without a material swatch standard if it develops protocols for characterising and cross-checking observers.

This should become a general project distinction:

```yaml
calibration_target:
  - object_reference
  - observer
  - report
  - instrument
  - surrogate
```

## 5. Regime D — Blumenbach: autopsy, provenance, persistence and cross-material control

The Meiners–Blumenbach exchange should not be coded as “textual evidence versus empirical evidence”. Blumenbach agrees that reliable testimony is indispensable where direct experience cannot reach.

His distinction is instead hierarchical and procedural.

### 5.1 Direct experience as an obligation, not an exhaustive requirement

In the 1790 *Beyträge*, Blumenbach argues that a naturalist has an obligation to acquire as much personal/direct experience as possible before relying on indirect reports. He compares even capable and credible testimony to `symbolische Bücher`: it can be subscribed to only insofar as it agrees with the “book of nature”.

This does not mean reports are worthless. It means indirect evidence is subjected to a different validation relation.

Blumenbach also explains that he had first worked by reading and excerpting travel accounts, then came to recognise the need for greater `Autopsie` and built up material collections and comparative practices.

### 5.2 His comparison set is not skulls alone

In answering Meiners, Blumenbach stresses that his inquiry did not rest on one bodily mark or on skulls alone. He refers to:

- skulls with attention to provenance;
- embryos and soft parts;
- hair;
- plaster casts;
- portrait-like / faithful images;
- direct observation of multiple individuals;
- comparison with credible witness reports.

This produces a different workflow:

`person / specimen`
→ `direct inspection where possible`
→ `provenance control`
→ `multiple individuals and material forms`
→ `cross-comparison`
→ `credible reports as supplementation`
→ `bounded natural-historical inference`.

Crucially, Blumenbach distinguishes secure from uncertain skull provenance and refuses at least one class of inference that Meiners's critique attributed to physical anthropology: he does not treat skull form as licensing conclusions about dispositions of the “heart”.

This is an unusually clear example of:

> **measurement or direct observation does not automatically expand inference jurisdiction.**

The project must code the `inference_ceiling` separately from the precision of the observation.

### 5.3 Hair as a high-retrostandardisability bodily trace

Sarah-Maria Schober's study of Blumenbach's hair collection provides a material control for evidence temporality.

Open article:
- Sarah-Maria Schober, “A Hairy Tale: Eighteenth-Century Strands of Albinism and Race,” *KNOW* 6.1 (2022): 177–213:
  https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/718478

Schober shows that hair could be easily procured, detached, transported, stored and compared for colour and texture. Samples arrived in Göttingen with letters; Blumenbach repeatedly compared them with a Savoy sample he had collected himself.

This makes hair epistemically unlike living skin colour.

Hair can persist after the encounter:

`body`
→ `detachable sample`
→ `transport`
→ `collection`
→ `reobservation`
→ `later comparison`.

Skin colour observed on a living person commonly follows a different trajectory:

`body at encounter`
→ `observation`
→ `word / image / note`
→ `referent departs`
→ `legacy description`.

Unless the person, a suitable specimen, or a materially adequate image remains accessible, later observers cannot rerun the original comparison.

Thus `retrostandardisability` is partly a property of the **material persistence of the evidence carrier**.

## 6. A decisive Göttingen control: source status is domain-specific

A major danger in the current argument would be to explain Meiners's witness dependence by claiming that Göttingen historians were generally indifferent or hostile to material objects. The evidence contradicts that.

### 6.1 Gatterer's diplomatic apparatus: historians could build object-intensive evidence systems

Johann Christoph Gatterer's teaching in diplomatics relied on a large `Anschauungsmaterial` apparatus of original charters, manuscripts, seals, seal matrices, writing materials, scripts, engravings and related exemplars.

Useful web controls:
- Gatterer-Apparat overview / historical quotations:
  https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatterer-Apparat
- Heidelberger Geschichtsverein overview:
  https://haidelberg.de/docs/gatterers-apparat
- LEO-BW overview of eighteenth-century diplomatic teaching collections:
  https://www.leo-bw.de/en/themenmodul/sudwestdeutsche-archivalienkunde/archivaliengattungen/urkunden

A 1772 report described Gatterer's diplomatic cabinet as containing hundreds of original charters alongside engravings, monograms, seals, coats of arms, writing materials and alphabets, organised and subdivided according to the best scientific method. Pütter likewise describes Gatterer making the cabinet usable to students in the teaching of diplomatics.

The point is not that Gatterer “liked objects”. It is stronger:

> in one historical subdomain, original material exemplars were explicitly authorised as a core comparison and teaching apparatus.

### 6.2 Yet the universal historians did not automatically treat ethnographica as sources

Michael Kraus's recent history of the Göttingen ethnological collection, drawing on Gudrun Bucher's detailed collection history, states that the Göttingen universal historians — especially Gatterer, Schlözer and Meiners — were not decisive actors in the collection's institutional development. Kraus quotes Bucher's conclusion that they did not work with ethnographica in their studies, did not regard them as sources, and did not engage in their acquisition; use of ethnographic objects in teaching is documented instead for Blumenbach and later Heeren.

Source:
- Michael Kraus, *Die Ethnologische Sammlung der Georg-August-Universität Göttingen: Eine Einführung* (Göttingen University Press, 2024), esp. PDF pp. 36–38 / internal PDF pages 14–15:
  https://univerlag.uni-goettingen.de/bitstream/handle/3/isbn-978-3-86395-633-2.1/ESG_BUCH_01_kraus.pdf?sequence=1

**Verification status:** the web text layer was inspected and locates the passage at internal PDF pages 14–15, citing Bucher 2023: 248–249. A web screenshot request for those pages failed with a cache-miss error in this sweep. Treat this item as `text-layer verified / page-image verification pending`; do not silently upgrade it to image-verified transcription.

This is a powerful Wilson-style distinction:

`object exists in local institution`
≠ `object selected as source by a discipline/task`.

### 6.3 Meiners was not anti-infrastructure

A separate academic history of Göttingen knowledge cultures reproduces a 1801 statement by Meiners praising the university's material installations — economic garden, natural-history and coin cabinets, picture and instrument collections, chemical laboratory, hospital and lying-in house — as the “feste Grund” on which the university rested.

Source:
- Marian Füssel, discussion of academic knowledge cultures / Göttingen collections:
  https://www.schwabeonline.ch/schwabe-xaveropp/elibrary/start.xav?start=%2F%2F%2A%5B%40attr_id%3D%27pdf.978-3-7965-3477-5.9783796534775_I._Wissens-_und_Expertenkulturen_als_Untersuchungsgegenstaende%27+and+%40outline_id%3D%27pdf.978-3-7965-3477-5.9783796534775_I._Wissens-_und_Expertenkulturen_als_Untersuchungsgegenstaende%27%5D

Therefore, Meiners's anthropological reliance on witness reports should not be explained as a philosophical rejection of material infrastructures.

The stronger model is:

`material infrastructure available and valued`
+
`domain-specific source authorization`
→ `different evidentiary architecture by task`.

## 7. Four comparability regimes side by side

| Regime | Unit | Carrier | Main calibration target | Main strength | Main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prange | individual observed colour | material swatch + number | object-reference relation | portable fine-grained reference | standard production / excessive granularity / matching burden |
| Meiners | population regularity | accredited convergent testimony | witness network | large-N, long-duration, geographically distributed knowledge | legacy descriptions not retro-standardisable; witness bias; inference may outrun observation |
| Forster | heterogeneous travel observation | observer-characterised report | observer perspective | turns plurality of viewpoints into corrigible evidence | observer metadata incomplete; correction itself interpretive |
| Blumenbach | individual/specimen feature across collections | autopsy + persistent specimen + provenance + reports | specimen/provenance/comparison chain | reobservation and cross-material control | collection bias; provenance gaps; preserved materials uneven by bodily feature |

The table is analytical, not a claim that the historical actors themselves named four mutually exclusive systems. Individual scholars can combine regimes.

## 8. Evidence temporality

The previous methodology treated provenance largely as the history of an object or copy. The present sweep requires a temporal dimension at the moment of observation.

### 8.1 Capture event

Every evidentiary claim should distinguish:

`historical phenomenon`
→ `capture event`
→ `carrier`
→ `transmission`
→ `later comparison`.

A colour can disappear as an available historical difference at any of these stages.

Examples:

1. a body is observed but only a vague colour word is written down;
2. a hand-coloured print survives but digitisation is grayscale;
3. a hair sample survives and can be re-compared;
4. a colour chart survives but its pigments have aged or copies differ;
5. an ethnographic object exists in a museum but the historian's domain does not authorise it as a source.

### 8.2 Retro-standardisability

Define:

> `retrostandardisability` = the degree to which evidence captured under an earlier or different protocol can later be subjected to a new comparison standard without inventing information absent from the surviving carrier.

High:
- persistent physical specimen with relevant property preserved;
- stable original colour object with controlled condition;
- adequately documented material reference.

Medium / conditional:
- high-quality colour image with known imaging conditions;
- faithful historical image whose relation to the referent is independently controlled.

Low:
- legacy verbal colour term without referent;
- copied description detached from observer metadata;
- grayscale surrogate of an originally coloured object when original is inaccessible.

Impossible / illegitimate:
- assigning an exact historical swatch number to a vanished referent solely from an imprecise historical adjective.

## 9. Observer networks are part of colour infrastructure

The Prange model might tempt the project to define infrastructure materially: colour charts, pigments, plates, printing processes, catalogues, cameras, scans.

Forster and Meiners show that this is too narrow.

An eighteenth-century witness network can itself be an infrastructure when it contains protocols for:

- who may observe;
- how long observation must continue;
- how many instances matter;
- how observer character affects the report;
- how reports are compared;
- what counts as convergence;
- where first-hand observation ends and synthesis begins.

Thus add:


Wilson-style consequence:

> A material difference becomes historically legible not only through an object's affordances but through the architecture of observation that decides which differences are repeatedly noticed, recorded, transported and compared.

## 10. A stronger interpretation of the Meiners–Prange non-adoption problem

The current evidence supports the following sequence of exclusions.

### Weak explanation 1: colour standards did not yet exist

Rejected.

Prange 1782 precedes Meiners 1792; Göttingen reviewed the apparatus; Walter later used it directly on human pathological specimens.

### Weak explanation 2: Göttingen historians were uninterested in material evidence

Rejected as a general claim.

Gatterer's diplomatic apparatus was explicitly object-intensive. Meiners praised Göttingen's material research/teaching facilities in 1801.

### Weak explanation 3: Prange was simply too precise / unusable

Partly relevant but insufficient.

The 4,608-colour system did create usability problems, and later naturalists compressed it by domain. But this does not explain the whole contrast because domain-specific adaptation was possible.

### Stronger explanation to test

Meiners's anthropology operated through a different comparability problem:

- the target was often population regularity rather than one specimen;
- the evidence was geographically dispersed;
- much of the evidence had already been captured in earlier travel accounts;
- the original bodies were no longer present for remeasurement;
- the credibility of the witness and convergence of reports therefore carried much of the stabilising work.

Hence:

`legacy travel corpus`
+ `non-persistent referents`
+ `population-level target`
→ `limited retro-standardisability`
→ `witness accreditation / aggregation remains central`.

This is not yet proof that Meiners consciously rejected Prange. No direct evidence currently establishes that he personally owned, consulted or discussed Prange's colour system.

Open archival checks remain:

1. Meiners's 1810 posthumous library catalogue for Prange / Lambert / Schäffer;
2. the 1788 Meiners → Beckmann note, SUB Göttingen `Cod. Ms. Beckmann 6:8`, between pp. 520/521;
3. Meiners–Lavater correspondence, 1782–1788, for observation language / complexion / physiognomic calibration.

These must remain `unresolved` until the actual text is inspected.

## 11. Implications for frontispieces and colour copies

The same apparatus applies to the frontispiece corpus.

### Case A — surviving coloured copy

`historical colouring`
→ `persistent object`
→ `present reobservation possible`
→ `modern colour measurement possible`

But this does not by itself prove historical standardisation or original-copy invariance.

### Case B — historical catalogue says “finely coloured”

`historical object`
→ `legacy verbal description`
→ `object absent / inaccessible`
→ `low retro-standardisability`.

Do not assign a modern colour category beyond what the description supports.

### Case C — original survives, available surrogate is grayscale

`coloured original`
→ `digitisation selects luminance / removes hue`
→ `modern analyst receives transformed evidence channel`.

Here the evidence exists materially but is unavailable through the current surrogate. This is a modern `availability gate`, not historical absence.

### Case D — monochrome process produces hatching syntax

`pigment reproduction constraint`
→ `alternative graphic operation`
→ `new categorical syntax`
→ `portable distinction without pigment`.

Constraint can therefore create a new comparability technology rather than merely degrade an older one.

## 13. Stop rules

1. **Do not infer standardisation from comparability.** A field may produce stable comparison through witness aggregation, observer calibration or specimens without a numbered standard.

2. **Do not infer historical absence from surrogate absence.** A grayscale scan or catalogue omission may erase a colour property that survives in the object.

3. **Do not retro-standardise vanished referents.** A vague historical colour adjective cannot be converted into a precise modern or eighteenth-century swatch code without surviving comparison evidence.

4. **Do not infer evidentiary authorization from local availability.** An object can exist in a Göttingen collection without functioning as a source for a particular historical domain.

5. **Do not infer inference jurisdiction from measurement precision.** A more precise description does not automatically authorize broader genealogical, moral, racial or causal claims.

6. **Do not rank comparability regimes teleologically.** Ask what task each regime solved and what its failure modes were.

7. **Treat observer metadata as evidence metadata.** For witness-based corpora, who saw, from what position, for how long, under what task and with what prior formation is part of the evidentiary object.

8. **Code persistence separately from provenance.** A perfectly provenanced report may still be non-retro-standardisable because the material referent is gone.

9. **Code source status by domain.** The same scholar may use originals as indispensable evidence in diplomatics while relying on reports rather than objects in universal history.

10. **Distinguish object calibration from observer calibration.** They solve different instability problems.

## 14. Current synthesis

The strongest formulation after this sweep is:

> **Eighteenth-century colour knowledge did not move linearly from words to standards. Different fields produced comparability through different carriers: numbered swatches, accredited witnesses, calibrated perspectives, persistent specimens, images, tables and collections. The historical question is not simply when colour became standardised, but which evidentiary regime made a colour difference portable, comparable and inferentially admissible.**

A still broader formulation is:

> **Comparison precedes classification, but the production of comparability is historically variable.**

And the Wilson-derived upstream question becomes:

> **How does a difference become available not merely to sight, but to comparison?**

This now sits immediately upstream of the project's existing question:

> **When may a visible difference become a historical difference?**

The combined chain is:

`material persistence / capture conditions`
→ `legibility`
→ `source authorization`
→ `comparability regime`
→ `discretisation / aggregation`
→ `classification`
→ `inference jurisdiction`.

This chain should replace any implicit narrative in which increasing measurement precision is treated as the default explanation for epistemic change.
