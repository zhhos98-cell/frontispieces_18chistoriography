## Evidence basis

Primary printed pp.114–115 of Zimmermann's 1778 first volume were visually inspected from a user-supplied full-page image pair. A user-supplied text derivative covering p.115 was also used as a transcription aid; where OCR and the scan diverge, the printed image controls.

The page pair is especially valuable because it preserves the argument immediately before and after the often-excerpted climate sentence. It therefore resolves not only a phrase-level climate claim but the architecture of Zimmermann's proposed derivation.

## p.114 — explicit derivation problem

After reviewing competing systems, Zimmermann says that, if one attends only to the differences of humankind described in the second section, each system presents extraordinary difficulties. He then introduces his own proposal with the explicit formulation:

> `Auf folgende Art dächte ich, daß sich einigermaßen die Racen der Menschen ungezwungen herleiten ließen.`

This is the first major Round 3 result. `Racen` here are not merely listed or mapped; Zimmermann explicitly proposes to **derive** them.

He locates a large inhabited elevation in eastern Asia, approximately 32–44/50 degrees latitude and 95–125 degrees longitude, from which major mountain chains extend. On the hypothesis, associated here with Buffon, Pallas and Bailly, that the oldest humans are placed there, Zimmermann says:

> `... ihre nachmaligen Varietäten ziemlich natürlich herleiten.`

The page then supplies a demographic and migratory mechanism:

> `Die zunehmende Volksmenge nöthigte sie, sich nach größern Ländern umzusehen, und nun stiegen einige Familien nach verschiedenen Weltgegenden von ihrem erhabenen Vaterlande herab.`

The remainder of p.114 begins to trace differentiated migration routes: one toward the plains between the Urals and Caucasus and eventually Europe; another along the northern side of the Altai toward northern Siberian populations and, continuing onto p.115, the Kurils and part of northern North America.

### Primary-direct inferences from p.114

1. Zimmermann explicitly frames the task as deriving human `Racen`.
2. His proposed derivation begins from a geographically concentrated `Vaterland` rather than from independent original creations for each present human group.
3. `Racen` and `Varietäten` both occur inside the same derivational passage; the page does not establish a rigid hierarchical relation between them.
4. Population increase (`zunehmende Volksmenge`) supplies the proximate pressure for dispersal.
5. The migrating units are `einige Familien`, not already fully formed present-day races.
6. Geographic branching precedes the climate-transformation statement on p.115.

## p.115 — climate as a gradual transforming mechanism

The migration reconstruction continues with a southern route from the Asian elevation into Arabia, India and associated islands, then potentially into Africa under a palaeogeographic conjecture linking Africa and Asia.

Zimmermann then states directly:

> `Der Mensch artete dem Klima nach und nach an; er wurde Neger.`

The crucial temporal operator is `nach und nach`: the human being becomes altered/adapted according to climate gradually, rather than appearing in the passage as a separately created fixed type.

After adding a south-eastern colony producing Chinese, Koreans and neighbouring peoples, Zimmermann generalises the mechanism:

> `Da der Mensch auf die Weise nach und nach in alle Klimate versezt wurde, so machte der Einfluß davon aus ihm bald einen Georgier, hier einen Neger, und dort einen Eskimo.`

The surrounding prose therefore ties together:

- displacement into different climates;
- gradual temporal process (`nach und nach`);
- climatic `Einfluß`;
- production of visibly/geographically differentiated human forms.

The user-supplied text derivative independently preserves the same p.115 causal chain, including the sequence from migration through climatic influence to `Georgier`, `Neger`, and `Eskimo`.

## Zimmermann's epistemic self-limitation

Immediately after the generalising climate sentence Zimmermann writes:

> `Dieser Einfall, denn für nichts weiter gebe ich dies aus ...`

This is essential to the evidential reading. The reconstruction is presented as a conjectural `Einfall`, not as demonstrated historical fact.

He nevertheless gives three reasons why it seems `natürlich` to him:

1. earlier discussion has, in his view, shown what changes of climate and nourishment can do to the human being;
2. no other major elevation so easily explains the origin of the different `Nationen`;
3. he claims historical certainty for the great antiquity of humans in this part of Asia.

The passage thus combines strong causal plausibility language with explicit epistemic downgrading.

## Answer to Round 3 Question 1 — common-origin / diffusion boundary

**Resolved at primary-direct level.** Zimmermann 1778 does construct a bounded one-homeland / dispersal account in which geographically concentrated early humans expand as families into different world regions and are gradually transformed under different climates. The operative sequence visible across pp.114–115 is:

`Vaterland / oldest humans → population growth → family migration → geographic branching → gradual climatic influence → later Racen / Varietäten / named human forms`.

This is stronger than a generic environmentalism claim because the causal and temporal links are contiguous in the actor's own argument.

At the same time, the whole historical reconstruction remains explicitly conjectural (`Einfall`). The strongest formulation is therefore:

> Zimmermann proposes, rather than demonstrates, a common-homeland dispersal model that derives later human races/varieties through family migration and gradual climatic transformation.

## Answer to Round 3 Question 3 — taxonomic vocabulary, partial resolution

The pp.114–115 cluster materially weakens any hypothesis of a rigid Zimmermann hierarchy.

Within one short derivational sequence Zimmermann moves among:

- `Racen der Menschen`;
- `nachmaligen Varietäten`;
- `Familien` as migrating demographic units;
- named forms such as `Georgier`, `Neger`, `Eskimo`;
- `verschiedenen Nationen` in the explanatory justification.

These words are not demonstrated here to form stable nested ranks. More plausibly, they operate at partially overlapping descriptive, genealogical, demographic and geographic levels. This must be tested against pp.53–59 and p.101 before making a global claim.

## Boundary against Meiners: first primary-direct formulation

This page cluster changes the Zimmermann ↔ Meiners comparison substantially.

What Zimmermann already has by 1778:

- explicit `Race` vocabulary;
- derivation rather than mere enumeration of human difference;
- a concentrated origin/homeland hypothesis;
- demographic dispersal through migrating families;
- geography as a branching mechanism;
- gradual climatic transformation;
- coexistence of `Race` and `Varietät` language;
- causal use of nourishment together with climate;
- an explicit conjectural-status marker for the reconstruction.

Therefore Meiners 1785/1793 cannot be distinguished simply by the presence of race, geography, climate, or derivation.

The remaining possible Meiners difference is more specific: **inferential governance**. Meiners may systematise group hierarchy and police admissible bodily evidence more explicitly than Zimmermann's comparatively mobile derivational vocabulary. That claim remains provisional until Zimmermann pp.53–59 and p.101 are primary-controlled.

## Important semantic distinction

`Der Mensch artete dem Klima ... an` should not be silently modernised into either `adaptation` or `degeneration` without qualification. In this passage it denotes a process of becoming altered in relation to climate and belongs to Zimmermann's `Ausartung` / `Abartung` / variation vocabulary. The safest English gloss is therefore contextual: **gradual climatic alteration / accommodation**, with the German retained in analytical writing where precision matters.

Likewise, the sentence does not by itself establish hereditary immutability. Its force is developmental-historical and causal.

## Relation to pp.31–34

The opening programme on p.31 asks whether climate, nourishment and other secondary causes can produce the observable `Ausartungen`, or whether several original individuals had to be created separately for their climates.

The pp.114–115 passage now supplies the later proposed answer: Zimmermann chooses a single-homeland dispersal reconstruction and makes climatic influence plus nourishment sufficient to render later human differentiation plausible, while marking the historical reconstruction itself as conjecture.

Thus p.31 and pp.114–115 form a coherent question/answer architecture inside the volume.
