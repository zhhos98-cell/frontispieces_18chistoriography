# Johann Adam Bernhard, 1748–1757: archival source supply, `unschuldige` documents, inedita risk, and manuscript purchase

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: HIGH-VALUE OPERATIONAL COMPARATOR — BERNHARD'S ADOLF-VON-NASSAU PROJECT DIRECTLY DOCUMENTS CROSS-ARCHIVE SOURCE REQUESTS, SELECTIVE RELEASE OF UNPUBLISHED MATERIAL, A 216-ITEM CODEX DIPLOMATICUS, GOVERNMENT PURCHASE OF THE MANUSCRIPT, AND CONCERN OVER POSTHUMOUS MISUSE OF INEDITA. THIS IS A CLOSE CONTEMPORARY CONTROL FOR BERNHARD'S HISTORICAL-PRODUCTION PRACTICE AROUND THE 1754 WINKELMANN CONTINUATION, NOT PROOF OF AN IDENTICAL SOURCE ROUTE.

## Result

Wolf-Heino Struck's 1952 reconstruction of eighteenth-century source collecting for the history of King Adolf of Nassau supplies a concrete institutional control for Johann Adam Bernhard's working practice immediately before and after the 1754 Winkelmann sixth part.

By 1748 Bernhard had completed a life/history of Adolf of Nassau after what Struck calls a diligent collection of accessible **chronicle and documentary sources**. At Bernhard's request, the Idstein archivist Johann Georg Hagelgans sent him additional **unpublished material from the Idstein archive**.

The source flow was selective rather than neutral. Struck's archival footnote states that Hagelgans restricted himself to sending Bernhard:

`"unschuldige" Dokumente`.

The project therefore exposes a controlled historical-production sequence:

`research problem / dynastic history`
→ `cross-archive request`
→ `archivist selection / access filtering`
→ `unpublished documentary supply`
→ `historical compilation`
→ `documentary apparatus`.

Source:

- Wolf-Heino Struck, `Eine neue Quelle zur Geschichte König Adolfs von Nassau`, *Nassauische Annalen* 63 (1952), p.72, with archival references; DOI page: https://doi.org/10.11588/diglit.62672#0084.

---

## 1. Bernhard was already using a distributed source network in 1748

Struck reconstructs a wider Nassau source ecology around Bernhard.

Material for an Adolf biography had already been assembled by Professor Schramm in Herborn. Because Schramm was overloaded with other work, he passed his collections to Bernhard.

Bernhard's own aids included earlier work by Rat Ehrenfried Klotz; a Klotz item was forwarded to Bernhard in Hanau in 1748.

At Bernhard's request, Idstein archivist Johann Georg Hagelgans supplied further unpublished archival material.

Thus the project was not built from one archive or one authorial notebook.

Working sequence:

`PREVIOUS SCHOLAR'S COLLECTANEA`
+
`PRINTED / PREVIOUS HISTORICAL WORK`
+
`REQUESTED ARCHIVAL INEDITA`
→ `BERNHARD HISTORICAL MANUSCRIPT`.

This is strikingly close to the repository's general model of distributed historical evidence.

---

## 2. Archival access was jurisdictionally filtered

The phrase `"unschuldige" Dokumente` is especially important.

Struck reports that Hagelgans limited what he sent Bernhard to documents considered harmless/innocuous.

This establishes:

`ARCHIVE POSSESSION`
≠ `RESEARCHER ACCESS`
≠ `MATERIAL RELEASED FOR HISTORICAL USE`.

The archive did not function as a passive reservoir. An archivist exercised selection over which unpublished records could safely leave the local institutional context and enter another historian's compilation.

Possible dimensions behind `unschuldig` include political, dynastic, legal, diplomatic, or reputational sensitivity, but the current passage does not disaggregate them. Do not over-specify the criterion without the underlying correspondence.

---

## 3. Bernhard constructed a large documentary apparatus

Struck states that the associated Bernhard `Codex diplomaticus` comprised **216 numbers/items**.

Hagelgans father and son were later tasked with collating and copying this diplomatic codex against the many books Bernhard had cited.

This yields a material workflow:

`historical narrative manuscript`
↔ `216-item documentary corpus`
↔ `bibliographic/source verification`
↔ `archival copy/collation labor`.

The historical work and its documentary apparatus therefore had partly separate production and verification lives.

This is directly relevant to the Winkelmann sixth-part stratigraphy, where the stable printed narrative may similarly conceal several documentary and editorial source layers.

---

## 4. Publication and ownership remained unstable after textual completion

Bernhard wrote on 11 March 1748 that the Adolf work would soon be sent to press, but publication did not occur as planned.

According to his own later account, the plan failed after the death of Prince William IV of Nassau-Orange in 1751.

In 1757 the Nassau authorities purchased the manuscript from Bernhard for **300 Taler** with the intention of controlling / undertaking publication themselves.

This provides a useful control:

`TEXT SUBSTANTIALLY COMPLETE`
≠ `PRINT PUBLICATION`.

A completed historical manuscript could remain dependent on dynastic sponsorship, political circumstances, ownership transfer, and institutional willingness to publish.

That strongly parallels the Winkelmann project, where a substantially completed sixth part remained unpublished for decades after the author's death.

---

## 5. The government explicitly worried about posthumous misuse of Bernhard's `Inedita`

Struck's archival note states that one consideration in the Nassau government's purchase was fear that:

`das eine oder andere Ineditum der Bernhardschen Sammlung könne nach dessen Tod mißbraucht werden`.

This is a major operational control.

Unpublished historical evidence was treated as something whose future custody and use mattered politically after the scholar's death.

The purchase therefore involved more than acquiring literary property.

It also functioned as:

`MANUSCRIPT ACQUISITION`
+
`POSTHUMOUS CUSTODY CONTROL`
+
`RISK MANAGEMENT OF UNPUBLISHED EVIDENCE`.

This should not be translated mechanically into modern copyright or secrecy categories. The important historical point is the institutional regulation of potentially consequential unpublished source material.

## 7. Relevance to the 1754 Winkelmann sixth part

Bernhard's role in the 1754 sixth part is currently controlled through historical attribution and modern source-state reconstruction:

- ADB: Bernhard completed the work; continuation from printed p.377 attributed to him;
- Fuchs/Rabeler: much of Winkelmann's original sixth-part manuscript was no longer findable by 1754 and Bernhard texts replaced missing material.

The Adolf project gives a close-in-time operational comparator showing how Bernhard actually worked:

`COLLECTANEA FROM ANOTHER SCHOLAR`
+
`ARCHIVAL REQUESTS`
+
`SELECTIVELY RELEASED INEDITA`
+
`LARGE DOCUMENTARY APPARATUS`
+
`COLLATION / COPY LABOR`
+
`DYNASTIC PUBLICATION INTEREST`
+
`MANUSCRIPT OWNERSHIP TRANSFER`.

This makes it methodologically plausible that the Winkelmann continuation was an archival reconstruction rather than simply literary continuation from memory or secondary books.

But current evidence does **not** establish that:

- Hagelgans or Nassau archives supplied material for the Winkelmann sixth part;
- the same 216-document corpus was reused;
- the same `unschuldig` filtering operated in Hesse-Kassel/Hanau;
- the 1754 printer's copy moved through the same ownership structure.

Use the Adolf project as:

`CONTEMPORARY BERNHARD WORKING-PRACTICE CONTROL`,

not as a direct source genealogy for the Winkelmann volume.

## 8. Wider analytical payoff

The Bernhard comparator joins several repository findings around the same structural problem.

Historical evidence becomes publishable through coordinated transformations:

`archive-held document`
→ `access decision`
→ `portable copy / excerpt`
→ `researcher collection`
→ `documentary corpus`
→ `historical narrative`
→ `verification / collation`
→ `ownership / patronage decision`
→ `possible publication`.

At every transition, the evidence can be selected, withheld, copied, recombined, bought, delayed, or lost.

This gives a stronger formulation for the frontispiece repository:

> The portability of historical witnesses was institutionally produced. A document, image, seal, coin, genealogical fact, or manuscript passage became usable across distance only through permission, reproduction, routing, custody, and verification regimes.

Bernhard's `unschuldige Dokumente` are especially useful because they show that **transportability itself could be politically filtered**.
