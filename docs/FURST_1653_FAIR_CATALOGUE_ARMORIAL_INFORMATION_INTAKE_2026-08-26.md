# Paul Fürst's 1653 fair-catalogue call for newly granted arms — information-intake control, 26 August 2026

## Status

A book-history source identifies a concrete information-gathering mechanism behind the mid-seventeenth-century expansion of the Siebmacher armorial. Marie-Kristin Hauke reports that in **1653** the Nürnberg publisher and art dealer **Paul Fürst** used the **Leipzig autumn book-fair catalogue** to ask its readers to send him **newly granted coats of arms**, so that he could complete the Siebmacher Wappenbuch he had taken into his publishing programme. Hauke cites the primary source directly as `Leipziger Herbstmeßkatalog 1653`.

The exact fair-catalogue notice has not yet been inspected directly in this pass. The evidence level is therefore: **specific secondary report with an exact primary-source locator**, not `verified_primary` quotation evidence.

This is independently anchored to the material history of the project. Andreas Tacke's *Neue Deutsche Biographie* entry on Hans Siebmacher records that in **1653** Siebmacher's widow sold remaining stock and the Wappenbuch copperplates to the Nürnberg book and art dealer Paulus Fürst. The acquisition of graphic stock and the solicitation of new heraldic information therefore belong to the same transition in ownership, although their exact chronological ordering within 1653 remains to be established from primary records.

## The operational structure

The 1653 notice is important because it exposes a stage that a plate-centred history alone can miss:

`publisher acquires inherited copper stock`
+
`publisher uses transregional fair catalogue as communication channel`
→
`readers are asked to submit newly granted arms`
→
`new heraldic information enters the publishing house`
→
`inherited armorial corpus can be completed / enlarged`
→
`new or revised printed representations`.

The copperplate stock is therefore only one half of the infrastructure. A changing heraldic reference work also needs an **information-intake network** capable of learning about objects that the inherited plates do not yet contain.

## Why the book-fair catalogue matters

Hauke's example occurs in her discussion of fair catalogues as more than lists of books for sale. Publishers could use them to circulate announcements and requests concerning projects. Fürst's notice turns the catalogue into a bidirectional scholarly-commercial medium:

`fair catalogue circulation`
→ `request for information`
→ `distributed response from readers`
→ `publisher-side corpus revision`.

It is safer to describe this as **distributed solicitation**, **reader-supplied information**, or a **publisher information-intake network** rather than importing the modern term `crowdsourcing` into the seventeenth century.

The audience also should not be assumed to mean every purchaser of the eventual Wappenbuch. Hauke says Fürst addressed the readers of the fair catalogue. The actual respondents, response rate, documentary form of submissions, verification procedures and conversion of submissions into engraved arms remain open.

## Material and informational succession must be separated

The Fürst case produces a useful distinction for the whole repository:

### Material succession

`Siebmacher / widow → surviving stock + copperplates → Fürst`.

### Informational succession

`new grants occurring in the social world → reports/submissions from readers → Fürst's publishing operation → possible additions/corrections to armorial corpus`.

These are different transmission systems. Ownership of old matrices gives the publisher the capacity to reproduce inherited information; it does not by itself tell the publisher what has changed since those matrices were made.

This sharpens the project's plate-stock ecology into a **plate-stock + information-flow ecology**.

## Connection to later controls

The 1653 notice makes the later Köhler and 1772 evidence form a longer sequence.

### 1653 — Fürst: acquire information

The inherited work is explicitly treated as completable. Readers of a major trade communication channel are asked to supply newly granted coats of arms.

### 1734 — Köhler: compare material witnesses

Köhler's reported 1734 preface says five Helmers copies were exactly compared and disagreed in the condition and number of their arms. He attributes the discrepancies to removal, corrected substitution, filling empty shields, insertion and addition.

This is a different operation:

`distributed information intake` is not the same as `multi-copy collation`.

But both arise because an armorial corpus is not materially or informationally self-stabilising.

### 1772 — publisher retrospective: explain the technical problem

The 1772 Weigel/Raspe `Vorbericht` states that older armorial compilations suffered from compression, unstable tincture coding, lack of descriptive space, and the fact that arms undergo changes and augmentations `von Zeit zu Zeit`; it retrospectively casts Köhler's 1723 Wappencalender as an attempt to remedy those inconveniences.

A bounded long-run formulation is therefore now available:

`1653 information solicitation`
→ `1734 exact comparison of divergent copies`
→ `1772 explicit diagnosis of temporal change and updating limits`.

This is not a simple line of intellectual influence. It is evidence that **maintaining a large heraldic visual corpus repeatedly generated problems of intake, correction, versioning and update across more than a century of Nürnberg publishing practice**.

## Relation to the later Fürst editions

The titles and catalogue records for Fürst's subsequent armorial parts reinforce the expansion context without proving that any particular arm originated in the 1653 solicitation.

- The 1655 work is explicitly `erneuert und vermehrt`.
- A third part appears in 1656.
- A fourth part in 1657 specifically represents nobles and others raised in rank and granted arms by imperial authority.
- Later parts and appendices continue enlarging the corpus.

Do not use these publication facts to claim that the fair-catalogue call directly generated a measurable subset of plates until a submission-to-plate relation can be traced.

## Analytical consequence

The corpus should distinguish at least four infrastructures that can otherwise collapse into the generic word `publication`:

1. **matrix infrastructure** — inherited, new, altered or replaced copperplates;
2. **information-intake infrastructure** — correspondents, readers, archives, fairs, catalogues and other routes by which new heraldic facts reach the publisher;
3. **verification/editorial infrastructure** — comparison, correction, selection, description and classification;
4. **distribution infrastructure** — books, annual calendars, supplements, handbooks, catalogues and later cumulative Wappenbücher.

A plate can persist while the information network changes; information can arrive without immediately generating a new plate; a new edition can combine inherited matrices with newly acquired content; different copies can preserve different results of those interventions.

This four-part model fits the evidence better than treating the visual corpus as a sequence of editions.

## Evidence guards

1. The Fürst solicitation is currently controlled through Hauke's research, with the exact primary locator `Leipziger Herbstmeßkatalog 1653`. Do not quote the 1653 notice diplomatically until the catalogue page itself is inspected.
2. Fürst's purchase of stock/copperplates in 1653 is independently documented, but the present pass does not establish that the solicitation occurred after the legal transfer on a specific date.
3. The notice asks for newly granted arms; it does not establish who responded, how submissions were authenticated, whether images or blazons were sent, or which later engraved arms derive from them.
4. Do not call the practice modern `crowdsourcing` in article prose without qualification.
5. Do not infer a direct institutional continuity from Fürst to Köhler solely from this notice. The stronger claim is continuity of a publishing problem: keeping a changing heraldic corpus current and materially usable.
