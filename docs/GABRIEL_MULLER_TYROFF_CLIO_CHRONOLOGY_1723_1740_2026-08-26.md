# Gabriel Müller–Tyroff chronology around the Clio plate, 1723–1740 — 26 August 2026

## Status

**Chronology correction. Gabriel Müller was already in Nürnberg by 1723, not 1733. A Müller design for the target Clio composition visible in a 1726 Wappenkalender is therefore chronologically possible. A documented Köhler–Müller–Tyroff illustration collaboration exists by 1733, and the British Museum attributes the 1740 target frontispiece to Martin Tyroff after Gabriel Müller. The exact relation between the 1726 impression and the 1740 Müller/Tyroff attribution remains an image/signature and plate-state question, not a chronological impossibility. A second chronological distinction is now essential: target-composition circulation by 1726 predates Tyroff's 1729 marriage into and management of the Weigel family firm.**

## I. Corrected Gabriel Müller chronology

A newer biographical study by Lilian Ruhe, `The Portrait Painter and Draughtsman Gabriel Müller (1688–1764), also Known as 'Kupezky-Müller'` (2019), gives the decisive chronology:

- Gabriel Müller was born in Ansbach in 1688;
- ca. 1713–1722 he worked as court painter to Count Questenberg at Jaroměřice;
- in **1723** he followed Johann Kupezky from Vienna to **Nürnberg**;
- he obtained Nürnberg citizenship in 1734;
- he married there in 1736.

Some older summary material places the Nürnberg move in 1733. That date should not be used for plate chronology. The current repository adopts **1723**.

This removes a false contradiction that briefly arose during the research pass: the target `Historia / Genealogia / Heraldica` composition is visibly present in a 1726 Wappenkalender witness, but a 1723 Nürnberg arrival leaves ample chronological room for Müller to have supplied a design before 1726.

## II. 1726 remains the earliest positive image-level target witness currently located

The current witness census records the 1726 *Geschichts-, Geschlechts- und Wappen-Calender* as the earliest positive image-level occurrence of the target composition.

The Auction Imperia surviving copy shows:

- the target allegorical frontispiece physically opposite/adjacent to the 1726 title material in the photographed opening;
- catalogue condition language referring specifically to a break in the book block at the frontispiece.

This is materially stronger than a bare bibliographic report of `Titelkupfer`.

It does **not** yet prove:

- that the 1726 impression carries a visible `G. Müller del.` or equivalent signature;
- that it is the exact same copperplate as the 1740 British Museum impression;
- that no earlier target impression existed;
- that the plate could not have been inserted into this particular copy at an early or later date.

The safe status remains: **target composition visibly present in a surviving 1726 host; designer/plate-state relation unresolved.**

## III. 1726 target circulation predates Tyroff's management of the Weigel family firm

The British Museum authority record for Martin Tyroff states that he:

- worked as an engraver and publisher in Nürnberg;
- **married the daughter of Christoph Weigel in 1729**;
- thereafter ran the family firm until his death.

The target Clio composition is already visible in a 1726 Wappenkalender witness.

Therefore two relations must be kept separate:

1. **graphic collaboration / possible commission** — Müller and/or Tyroff may have supplied design/engraving work to the Weigel publication ecology before 1729;
2. **firm succession / ownership-management integration** — Tyroff's position inside the Weigel family publishing house begins through the 1729 marriage/succession context.

Do not back-project Tyroff's later control of the inherited firm onto the 1726 host.

This produces a potentially important sequence:

`1726 or earlier: target image enters annual publication ecology`
→ `1729: Tyroff becomes family-firm insider/manager`
→ `1733: Köhler–Müller–Tyroff historical illustration collaboration directly documented`
→ `1740: target Clio catalogued as Tyroff after Müller under Weigel heirs`.

If the 1726 plate itself proves to be signed Müller/Tyroff, it would therefore document an **external or pre-succession artistic collaboration that later became internalized within the firm structure**.

## IV. By 1733 Müller and Tyroff are demonstrably collaborating inside a Köhler scholarly publication

A 1733 Altdorf dissertation, *Voluntarium Imperii consortium inter Fridericum Austriacum et Ludovicum Bavarum...*, was presided over by **Johann David Köhler**.

BSB / Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek metadata identifies its visual apparatus as:

- two engraved plates;
- six vignettes in total;
- **two vignettes after Gabriel Müller by Martin Tyroff**.

Thus by 1733 the following relation is institutionally catalogued:

`Johann David Köhler scholarly argument`
→ `Gabriel Müller design`
→ `Martin Tyroff copper engraving`.

The 2019 Müller oeuvre study additionally lists Tyroff's `Versöhnung der Gegenkaiser Friedrich von Österreich und Ludwig von Bayern` (1735) among prints after Müller. This is not merely a portrait-production pipeline: Müller→Tyroff collaboration reaches a historical-political composition tied to the same Köhler imperial-history problem.

This is an important production control for the 1740 Clio attribution. Müller and Tyroff were not merely independent Nürnberg figures later joined by a museum catalogue; they were already supplying designed/engraved historical images to a Köhler scholarly publication seven years earlier.

## V. 1740 British Museum target attribution

British Museum object `1910,0906.2`, taken from the 1740 Weigel Wappenkalender, catalogues the target frontispiece as:

- `Print made by Martin Tyroff`;
- `After: Gabriel Müller`;
- published by the Heirs of Christoph Weigel;
- Nürnberg, 1740;
- approximately 198 × 115 mm.

The British Museum's firm authority separately reconstructs the Weigel succession: after Christoph Weigel's death, his widow continued the firm with their daughter Barbara Sibylla and her second husband Martin Tyroff, whom she married in 1729; later issues used the `Christoph Weigels Erben` formula.

The iconography is the target `Historia / Genealogia / Heraldica / Testes temporum et veritatis` composition.

The museum attribution is high-quality institutional evidence, but the current web record does not expose a legible scan of the exact designer/engraver inscription. Therefore a remaining bounded question is whether the attribution expands a literal plate signature such as `G. Müller del. / M. Tyroff sc.` or derives from another cataloguing source.

## VI. International-template capacity in Tyroff's workshop

The British Museum also catalogues at least two Nürnberg prints by Martin Tyroff **after Bernard Picart** in the 1730s (`Bb,8.169` and `Bb,16.655`).

Without titles/images sufficient for an iconographic link, these cannot be used as source evidence for the Clio frontispiece. They do establish a narrower production fact:

> Tyroff's Nürnberg practice was capable of directly translating international French/Dutch graphic designs into local engraving during the same broad period.

This supports the plausibility of international-template circulation but does not identify Coypel, Picart, or any other foreign print as the model for the target Clio.

## VII. Current plate-genealogy models

The corrected chronology leaves several viable models.

### H1 — Müller design already in circulation by 1726

`Gabriel Müller in Nürnberg from 1723`
→ `Müller supplies target design by 1726`
→ `Tyroff or another engraver cuts the plate`
→ `Tyroff later becomes firm insider in 1729`
→ `same or related plate remains in Weigel stock through 1740`.

Chronologically plausible; requires signature/line-level control.

### H2 — 1726 target composition predates the documented Müller/Tyroff state

`1726 target design by another or unverified designer/engraver`
→ `Müller redraws/revises the design in the 1730s`
→ `Tyroff engraves a new plate`
→ `1740 BM state`.

Also possible until 1726 and 1737/1740 impressions are compared line by line.

### H3 — same design family, multiple plate states / re-engravings

The dimensions and long run make a re-engraving or second plate technically plausible. Design continuity must be separated from copperplate identity.

## VIII. What is now ruled out

Do **not** argue:

> `Gabriel Müller only arrived in Nürnberg in 1733, therefore he cannot be connected to a 1726 target frontispiece.`

That premise is obsolete under the newer 2019 biographical chronology.

Also do **not** argue:

> `Tyroff already ran the Weigel firm when the 1726 frontispiece appeared.`

The British Museum places his marriage into / management of the family firm in 1729. Earlier engraving collaboration and later firm control are distinct edges.

## IX. Stronger production-context implication

The combined sequence now looks less like one static `Weigel plate` and more like a changing production relationship:

`Müller arrives Nürnberg (1723)`
→ `target design visible in Weigel annual (1726)`
→ `Tyroff marries into / takes role in Weigel firm (1729)`
→ `Köhler–Müller–Tyroff historical-image collaboration documented (1733/35)`
→ `target image catalogued as Tyroff after Müller under Weigel heirs (1740)`.

This suggests an article-level distinction between **image entry**, **workshop collaboration**, **firm incorporation**, and **long-term plate custody**. Those events need not share a single date.
