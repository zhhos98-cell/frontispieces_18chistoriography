# Reader augmentation as a working heraldic information system, 1747–1762 — 26 August 2026

## Claim

Three independently described surviving copies show that the Wappenkalender / Handbuch apparatus could be treated by later users as an **extensible working heraldic information system**, not merely as a finished illustrated reference book.

The evidence is copy-specific and does not establish one continuous ownership tradition. What it does establish is a recurrent material practice: users expanded the printed visual-genealogical architecture with additional images, colour, annotations and source/origin information.

## Material progression

These three witnesses display different augmentation modes:

1. **printed-image accretion** — additional engraved coats of arms physically attached to the 1747 host;
2. **visual enhancement + pasted supplementation** — the 1750 host combines colouring/metal heightening of publisher-issued plates with added heraldic images;
3. **manuscript generation + provenance/source annotation** — the 1762 Handbuch is supplemented with hand-drawn/partly coloured arms and papers explaining origin and meaning.

The progression should not be read as a chronological evolution in user practice: the dates of individual interventions are generally unresolved. The useful analytical point is the coexistence of several ways to make the printed apparatus **open-ended**.

## Relation to the book's printed architecture

The annual/handbook format already separates and coordinates multiple information layers:

- genealogical tables;
- armorial images;
- descriptive text;
- threshold allegory / programme;
- indexes and registers;
- in some years medals, colour keys or other plates.

Reader augmentation exploits precisely that modularity. Blank spaces, versos, pastedowns, loose papers and the relation between image and explanatory text become sites where the information system can be expanded.

This makes the distinction between **edition state** and **copy state** central. A surviving copy may contain materially meaningful information that was never publisher-issued, while apparently missing or displaced elements may reflect later use rather than original production.

## Stronger historiographical implication

The evidence supports describing these books as **working visual-genealogical repositories** at copy level. That formulation is stronger and more precise than saying that readers merely annotated them.

The interventions add new witnesses and new relations:

`printed armorial witness → added armorial witness → name/identification → colour coding → explanatory/origin note`.

The 1762 copy is especially important because the seller specifically reports documents and notes concerning the *origin* of the arms. That pushes augmentation beyond decoration or ownership marking into evidentiary/source-oriented use.

## Relation to Gatterer's evidentiary programme

This reader-side evidence should remain distinct from claims about Gatterer's authorial intention. It nevertheless provides a material afterlife control for a broader point already visible in Gatterer's own verbal-visual apparatus: genealogical and heraldic information was useful when it could be assembled, compared, cross-referenced and visually inspected.

The augmented copies show later users materially performing those operations on the books themselves.

## Guardrails

- Do not date the 1747 or 1750 augmentations merely from the host year.
- Do not attribute the 1750 additions personally to Maria Josepha without direct evidence.
- Do not attribute every annotation/drawing in the 1762 copy to one hand, one owner or the eighteenth century.
- Do not mix added printed/manuscript material into publisher-issued plate counts.
- Do not treat these three copies as statistically representative of the entire print run.
- Use them as positive copy-level evidence for **possible and actually attested modes of use**.

## Next high-value control

If images of the 1762 Kümmerle copy become available, document:

1. physical placement of handwritten heraldic sheets relative to printed plates/tables;
2. whether annotations cross-reference printed page/table numbers;
3. whether colour conventions correspond to the printed heraldic colour-key tradition;
4. handwriting groups and paper types;
5. literal vocabulary used for `Herkunft`, source, proof, comparison or correction.

Those observations would determine whether the copy functioned merely as a collecting container or as a deliberately cross-referenced working research dossier.
