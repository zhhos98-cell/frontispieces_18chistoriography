# Gatterer 1766: heraldry taught through a hierarchy of sources, not only its vocabulary

## Result

Gatterer's 1766 *Abriß der Heraldik oder Wappenkunde* is more epistemically ambitious than a compact blazon manual. Its table of contents places a section titled **`Quellen der Wappenkunde`** immediately after the opening `Erklärung und Eintheilung der Wappen` and before the long technical sequence on divisions, figures, tinctures, terminology, combinations, helmets and other formal features.

The visible 1766 contents sequence includes:

- `Erklärung und Eintheilung der Wappen` — p. 1;
- `Quellen der Wappenkunde` — p. 7;
- sections on shield divisions / line forms;
- technical terms used in description;
- combination of arms;
- helmet ornaments;
- speaking arms.

The pedagogical order is therefore significant:

`what a coat of arms is`
→ `where knowledge of coats of arms comes from`
→ `how heraldic forms are analysed and described`.

The book does not train visual classification while leaving source authority implicit. Source theory is part of the elementary architecture.

A second control now sharpens this considerably. A transcription keyed explicitly to the expanded Nürnberg 1774 edition, p. 7, preserves Gatterer's division of heraldic sources into **`Quellen vom ersten Range`** and **`Quellen vom zweyten Range`**. The section already occupies p. 7 in the 1766 first edition, but exact first-edition identity of the wording still requires direct page comparison.

## Source hierarchy: first and second rank

### `Quellen vom ersten Range`

The 1774 p. 7 transcription places three source types in the first rank:

1. **Wappen- und Adelsbriefe** — armorial and ennoblement charters;
2. **Siegel** — seals;
3. **Münzen** — coins.

### `Quellen vom zweyten Range`

The second rank contains a much broader and more mediated set:

1. **Denkmäler** — with examples such as epitaphs, gravestones, paintings and church banners;
2. **Lehenbriefe** — feudal charters;
3. **Geschichtsschreiber** — historical writers;
4. **Turnierbeschreibungen** — descriptions of tournaments;
5. **alte Familien- oder Stammbücher**, together with descriptions of ceremonial entries, funerals and related occasions;
6. **Wappensammlungen** — armorial collections, etc.

The important result is not merely that Gatterer names sources. He **ranks heterogeneous source media**. Documentary grants, seals and coins are set apart from monuments, historical narratives, ceremonial descriptions, family books and compiled armorials.

The 1774 transcription also connects the distinction to transmission error: the first group is presented as being less exposed to the kinds of mistakes introduced by armorial copyists than the second. That rationale still needs direct primary-page verification before diplomatic quotation, but it makes the hierarchy an epistemic claim rather than a neutral bibliography.

## What this changes in the project

The earlier formulation `source practice → source theory` can now be made more exact:

`source practice`
→ `source types named as a teachable problem`
→ `source types ranked by evidentiary value / transmission risk`.

This matters because Gatterer's heraldry is not simply a formal grammar applied to whatever image happens to be available. At least by the 1774 text, knowledge begins with a differentiated evidentiary field in which an armorial charter, a seal, a coin, a monument, a chronicle and a copied armorial do not enter on equal terms.

The relevant methodological sequence becomes:

`source type`
→ `relative authority / transmission risk`
→ `heraldic knowledge claim`
→ `graphic representation and description`
→ `historical or critical operation`.

That sequence is much closer to the repository's own discipline of keeping object, representation, witness occurrence, copy state and inferred relation separate.

## Relation to the Wappenkalender/Handbuch ecology

By 1763 the Gatterer *Handbuch* advertised an embedded `Abriß der Heraldik`; by 1766 the methodological component was articulated as a student-oriented treatise `zum Nutzen der studirenden Jugend` and explained with eight copperplates.

This means that the same publication ecology increasingly joined:

- current genealogical tables;
- armorial representations;
- verbal descriptions;
- general heraldic rules;
- explicit discussion of the sources of heraldic knowledge;
- and, in the later controlled wording, a hierarchy among those sources.

A reader using the calendar/handbook was therefore potentially trained not only to decode symbols but also to distinguish stronger and weaker routes by which heraldic knowledge reached print.

## Longer source-critical lineage

This source section can be placed beside earlier primary controls without claiming a direct textual chain where none has been shown.

### Fürst, 1657

Fürst describes a live information network using originals, court mediation, copied models, correspondents, fair-catalogue submissions, reader corrections and specialist contributors. His production notice separates incoming information from the engraved plate that eventually represents it.

### Spener, 1680

Spener explicitly distinguishes copied/received family information, independent checking, doubtful reports and source accountability; he explains how he departs from Siebmacher/Fürst where other evidence shows them wrong.

### Köhler, 1734

Köhler's reported five-copy collation of Helmers witnesses uses exact comparison to diagnose divergent copy configurations, corrections, substitutions and additions.

### Gatterer, 1766 / 1774 control

The 1766 first edition places `Quellen der Wappenkunde` near the beginning of an elementary manual. The 1774 expanded-edition transcription shows the internal architecture explicitly as sources of first and second rank.

The safe project-level claim is therefore:

> By the mid-eighteenth century, questions that earlier publishers and compilers had handled operationally through originals, submissions, comparison and correction could themselves be abstracted into a teachable and hierarchically organised problem of the `sources of heraldry`.

This is a shift from **source practice** to **source theory and source ranking**, without requiring a simple line of influence from Fürst or Spener to Gatterer.

## Nineteenth-century reception control: Hefner rejects the hierarchy

Otto Titan von Hefner's *Handbuch der theoretischen und praktischen Heraldik* (1861) is useful because it treats Gatterer's source ordering as a substantive methodological position worth attacking.

Hefner says the difference between his own heraldry and Gatterer's school appears in their different assessment of the kinds and value of sources. He objects especially to treating armorial and ennoblement charters as the foremost source without an age limit. For Hefner, such charters are particularly useful through the fifteenth century but require increasingly severe criticism later; he explicitly warns that diplomas can contain errors and should not be treated as self-authenticating truth.

This later criticism is analytically valuable for two reasons:

1. it confirms that Gatterer's ranking was historically legible as a **hierarchy of evidentiary value**, not merely a list of reading matter;
2. it shows that the hierarchy itself could become an object of source criticism. A privileged documentary genre could later be demoted once its institutional production and historical variability were treated as problems.

Do not retroject Hefner's nineteenth-century standards into Gatterer. Use the comparison to expose the historical contingency of source ranking.

## Why this matters for visual evidence

The hierarchy blocks a common modern simplification. Heraldry in Gatterer's pedagogical scheme is not reducible to a grammar of lines, tinctures and figures, nor can every surviving image be treated as an equivalent visual witness.

The first-rank group is strikingly material and documentary: charters, seals and coins. The second rank mixes monuments with textual and compiled carriers. This makes medium, institutional origin and copying history potentially relevant to evidentiary status before any formal blazon begins.

For the frontispiece project this suggests a precise question whenever an engraved page places charters, seals, coins, monuments or armorials together:

> Does graphic co-presence flatten source hierarchy, or does the surrounding text restore differences of authority that the image itself cannot display?

That is a better test than treating all represented learned objects as one generic iconography of `evidence`.

## Relation to Gatterer's 1767 and 1791 methodological vocabulary

The repository already controls Gatterer's later *Praktische Heraldik* (1791), where practical heraldic work is divided into operations including `Blasonieren`, `Historisieren`, `Kritisieren`, and `Erfinden oder Aufreissen`.

A newly recovered 1767 passage adds an intermediate control: Gatterer can distinguish a writer who merely blazons arms from one who supplies historical elucidation or a history of the arms. This should be developed separately because it concerns **operations performed on heraldic knowledge**, whereas the 1766/1774 source section concerns **where heraldic knowledge comes from**.

The published chronology can therefore be tested as:

`1766: sources are an elementary topic`
→ `1774 control: those sources are explicitly ranked`
→ `1767: formal blazon is distinguishable from historical explanation/history`
→ `1791: practical heraldic operations are explicitly differentiated`.

This is a chronology of published distinctions, not yet proof of one unchanged conceptual system.

## Evidence guards

1. **1766 first-edition control:** direct bibliographic/contents evidence establishes `Quellen der Wappenkunde` at p. 7. The full p. 7ff prose has not yet been inspected directly in the first-edition scan.
2. **Rank wording:** the first-/second-rank categories are presently controlled through a transcription explicitly keyed to the expanded Nürnberg 1774 edition, p. 7. Do not silently promote that wording to a verbatim quotation from the 1766 first edition until the two pages are collated.
3. **Copy-error rationale:** the transcription associates the hierarchy with errors introduced through copying. Direct primary inspection is still required before exact quotation or fine-grained paraphrase of that rationale.
4. **Hefner 1861:** use as reception and critique of Gatterer's school, not as a substitute witness for the exact 1766 wording.
5. Do not assume that Gatterer's `Quellen` maps one-to-one onto modern source criticism.
6. Do not infer direct Fürst → Spener → Köhler → Gatterer influence from structural resemblance alone.
