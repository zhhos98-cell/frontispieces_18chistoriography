# An *Erklärung der Farben*: Colour, Books, and Historical Method from Nürnberg to Göttingen

**Working draft v5 — source-integrated / image-driven pass**  
**Date:** 4 September 2026  
**Target:** *Huntington Library Quarterly*  
**Structure locked:** Göttingen → Nürnberg → Göttingen  
**Core locked:** colour + book history first

## Abstract

In 1772 August Ludwig Schlözer warned historians against making or at least “colouring” their facts. In his reply to Johann Gottfried Herder, first issued in 1773 and revised in 1775, he nevertheless refused to dismiss *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen* as aids to historical synchronism; the revised edition also proposed the *Illumination der Tabelle* as a way of making unequal historical durations visible to the eye. Johann Christoph Gatterer, Schlözer’s Göttingen rival, had meanwhile published a *Synopsis historiae universalis* whose title advertised two copper-engraved tables *coloribusque illustratae*. Surviving copies do not preserve a uniform chromatic state. Most strikingly, a Tübingen copy contains one coloured and one uncoloured table within the relevant pair.

This article approaches those Göttingen books through the history of an older Nürnberg practice of communicating colour in print. The Weigel/Köhler *Geschichts-, Geschlechts- und Wappen-Calender* repeatedly included an engraved *Erklärung der Farben*: a monochrome page in which dots and hatchings stood for named heraldic colours. In a directly inspected 1744 copy the key is immediately followed by armorial plates using the same signs, while a later prose section names the tinctures again. Other copies reveal the key as a separable bibliographical component: a defective 1729 copy is explicitly described as lacking it. The annuals that carried the key were repeatedly revised and sometimes patched with additions and changes that arrived while printing was underway. A cumulative *Wappenbuch* preserved at Tübingen, catalogued under 1771 but bearing a 1772 title state, still places an *Erklärung der Farben* before its title page; its publisher states explicitly that arms formerly used in the *Wappencalender* supplied the basis of the new work.

Gatterer’s relation to this publication world was direct. He worked with Nürnberg engravers in 1755, contributed to the posthumous closure of Johann David Köhler’s numismatic serial in 1756, and in 1759 issued the *Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik* under the Weigel-heirs imprint. More significantly for colour, the expanded 1774 edition of Gatterer’s heraldic textbook discussed additional hatch signs for *Naturfarbe* and *Eisenfarbe*, tested their terminology against old *Wappenbriefe*, and explicitly referred readers to Köhler’s 1736 Göttingen programme on hachures by which metals and colours could be indicated *absque pigmentis*. The link need not be enlarged into a causal history of Göttingen method: it is already a direct textual connection between two books about representing heraldic colour without pigment.

Rather than treating colour as a metaphor for historical knowledge, the article follows concrete bibliographical states: colour named but not printed, encoded by letters, converted into hatching, supplied through a key, added by hand, lost with a leaf, retained across changes of format, or proposed for a historical table. The result is a short return from Göttingen to Nürnberg and back. The late-eighteenth-century discussion of coloured historical representation thus appears against a longer book history in which colour could be communicated even when no coloured pigment appeared on the page.

# I. Göttingen: Colouring Facts

In 1772 August Ludwig Schlözer compared the materials of universal history to the small coloured stones of a mosaic. Criticism recovered or tested facts from annals and monuments; the historian then distributed and combined them until a larger *Gemälde* appeared. Yet the same passage drew a limit around one kind of colouring. The writers who did not simply make their facts, Schlözer remarked, at least “coloured” them — *färben sie wenigstens*.[^1]

**[FIG. 1A]** Schlözer, *Vorstellung seiner Universal-Historie*, I (1772), pp. 44–45: mosaic / *Facta färben* / *Zusammenstellung*.

The sentence is useful here less as a metaphor than as one member of a short chronological cluster. In his reply to Herder, first issued in 1773, Schlözer refused to dismiss *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen* outright. The wording presently under exact textual control comes from the revised 1775 edition, p. 327. A painted sheet might place Solomon’s Temple, the Egyptian pyramids, and Homer together before the eye. Their co-presence would help fix their simultaneity — *Synchronismus* — through the *Association der Ideen*.[^2]

**[FIG. 1B]** Schlözer, reply to Herder, first issued 1773; revised 1775 ed., p. 327: *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen* / *Synchronismus* / *Association der Ideen*.

The same revised edition contained a further proposal. If five principal historical periods were placed on a table divided into five corresponding temporal compartments, their unequal *Länge und Dauer* could be made visible to a young viewer *durch Illumination der Tabelle*.[^3]

**[FIG. 1C]** Schlözer 1775: *Illumination der Tabelle* / *Länge und Dauer*. Exact primary page still to close.

These passages should not be collapsed into one philosophy of colour. Nor should they be collapsed into the separate *Species Facti* quarrel between Schlözer and Gatterer in 1773. That controversy concerned teaching, priority, and alleged borrowing between two Göttingen programmes of universal history; colour has not been shown to have been an explicit subject of the exchange.[^4] The narrower chronological point is enough: painted and illuminated historical pages were being discussed in the same scholarly environment in which plans for universal history were professionally consequential.

Gatterer’s *Synopsis historiae universalis* makes the book-historical problem more concrete. Its title describes six tables, two of them engraved in copper and *coloribusque illustratae*.[^5] André de Melo Araújo has already established the importance of these tables for the history of synchronistic and visual historiography. He has also shown that hand-applied pigment could sharpen boundaries and political changes while the engraved line structure carried much of the historical arrangement.[^6] That is prior art. The complication pursued here is materially simpler: the colour advertised by the title was not uniform across surviving books.

Araújo’s copy census makes the variation unusually specific. Coloured 1766 witnesses include the ULB Sachsen-Anhalt copy (`Ha 179 IZEA / Na 1338`), the Hannover copy (`F-A 10010`), and Tübingen `Fn 28.2`. Completely uncoloured 1766 witnesses include Leipzig `Allg.Gesch.73` and Gotha `SPB 2° 1270.00157`; several 1769 witnesses are likewise wholly uncoloured.[^7] Most useful for the present article, Tübingen `Fn 28.2` is internally mixed: the first table of the relevant pair is coloured while the second was not supplied with pigment.

**[FIG. 2A]** *Synopsis* title: *quarum duae in aes incisae coloribusque illustratae sunt*.  
**[FIG. 2B]** Tübingen `Fn 28.2`, relevant Table I, coloured.  
**[FIG. 2C]** Tübingen `Fn 28.2`, relevant Table II, uncoloured.

This one-copy comparison is stronger than an abstract statement that exemplars differ. A reader can encounter both states inside one surviving book. The edition-level promise of colour did not determine a uniform copy-level finish.

The question raised by these Göttingen books is therefore elementary: what did a book mean materially when it promised, proposed, carried, or lacked colour? To answer it, the discussion needs to leave Göttingen almost as soon as it begins.

# II. Nürnberg: An *Erklärung der Farben*

## Good copper, bad explanation

Genealogy-heraldry calendars were already substantial illustrated books before the long Weigel/Köhler series. Martin Schmeitzel’s *Einleitung zur Wappen-Lehre* of 1723 contains a useful contemporary judgment on an Augsburg genealogical-heraldic calendar of 1720. In a later exact transcription tied to p. 69 of the 1723 edition, Schmeitzel praised the arms as *sauber in Kupfer gestochen* but dismissed the attached explanation as *nichts werth*.[^8] The first-edition page still needs direct diplomatic inspection, but the distinction itself matters: good engraving and good explanation were separable qualities of the same book.

The target was not an isolated experiment. A surviving-copy description for the Augsburg *Genealogisch-Heraldischer Staats-Kalender* of 1723 lists an engraved frontispiece, seventy-four armorial copperplates, twelve text coppers, and folding genealogical trees.[^9] By the early 1720s, genealogy-heraldry calendars already brought together images, tables, and prose on a substantial scale.

Heraldic colour made the relation between image and explanation especially visible because the relevant colours did not have to appear materially on the printed page. Johann Siebmacher’s *New Wapenbuch* of 1605 supplied a *Declaration* of the proper colours so that a purchaser could have the arms *illuminiren und erhöhen lassen*.[^10] Colour could therefore exist as information in an uncoloured printed book and as pigment in a subsequently finished copy.

**[FIG. 3A]** Siebmacher 1605 *Declaration*: *gehörigen Farben* / *illuminiren und erhöhen*.

Another solution compressed colour information into the image. Modern scholarship reports lower-case letters in Siebmacher’s 1609 second part, although a direct plate should be inspected before it is reproduced here.[^11] More securely, Philipp Jakob Spener explained in 1680 that his copperplates used the French manner of dots and differently directed lines, *punctulis aut lineolis*, because Germans had hitherto indicated colours by initials, *literis initialibus colorum*. If the figures were too small for the lines to distinguish tinctures sufficiently, the reader had to return to the printed explanation: *ad explicationem typis expressam recurrere necesse erit*.[^12]

**[FIG. 3B]** Optional direct 1609 letter-coded shield.  
**[FIG. 3C]** Spener 1680: hatching / initial letters / prose fallback.

There is no need to write a linear invention history from prose to letters to hatching. The systems overlapped. The modest point is enough: by the eighteenth century, heraldic colour could be carried by words, initials, dots, lines, a separate key, or later hand colouring.

## The black page that explains colour

A page headed *Erklärung der Farben* might be expected to contain colours. The copy of the Weigel/Köhler *Geschichts-, Geschlechts- und Wappen-Calender* for 1744 preserved by the Universitäts- und Landesbibliothek Düsseldorf does not. Its colour explanation is a full-page copper engraving printed in black ink.[^13] Small shield-shaped fields contain dots, vertical lines, horizontal lines, diagonals, cross-hatching, and conventional fur patterns. French and German labels identify the corresponding tinctures: *d’Or / Gold*, *d’Argent / Silber*, *de Gueules / Roth*, *d’Azur / Blau*, *de Sable / Schwartz*, *de Sinople / Grün*, together with vair, ermine, purple, iron, and natural colour.

**[FIG. 4A — principal image]** 1744 *Erklärung der Farben*, ULB Düsseldorf, image 8331529.

The page supplies no sample of red, blue, green, gold, or silver. Instead it places a black graphic mark beside a named chromatic category. Dots are not gold and horizontal lines are not blue; they are read as those tinctures within the convention established by the page. The *Erklärung* is a monochrome key to colour.

The next image in the same copy confirms what the key is for. The armorial section begins with the *Kayserliches Wappen*, densely engraved with the same kinds of dots, lines, diagonals, and cross-hatchings displayed on the preceding key.[^14] The relation does not depend on identifying every minute field. A graphic vocabulary is presented and then immediately used in an actual heraldic image.

**[FIG. 4B]** *Kayserliches Wappen*, image 8331530, cropped to diagnostic hatch fields.

Later in the volume the colours are stated again in words. Printed p. 87 begins the *Kurtze und deutliche Beschreibung aller hierinnen vorkommenden Wappen / Nach dem Alphabet*.[^15] The blazons repeatedly name tinctures and metals — *Gold*, *roth*, *schwartz*, *blau*, *silbern*. The same chromatic information is therefore available through several routes inside one copy.

**[FIG. 4C]** p. 87, image 8331757, cropped around explicit tincture words.

The three panels belong together. The key explains marks; the armorial plate deploys them; the prose names the colours again. The same 1744 volume also contains a register of 125 genealogical tables.[^16] A monochrome book could thus contain extensive colour information without containing coloured printing.

The key recurred across the series. Detailed physical-copy descriptions report a full-page engraved *Erklärung der Farben* in annuals for 1728, 1732, 1737, 1738, 1744, 1751, 1755, and 1757; another copy description reports a colour-key copper in 1725.[^17] A 1745 description explicitly says that the colours were represented *durch Schraffierung*.[^18] These records establish repeated presence of a module; they do not establish one continuous copper matrix.

A defective 1729 copy exposes the key as a separable part of the book. Its collation lists among the missing material “1 gest. Tafel ‘Erklärung der Farben’,” together with two missing armorial engravings and other leaves.[^19] This is copy loss, not evidence that the edition was issued without the key. The distinction is valuable precisely because many encoded armorial images may survive after the internal decoder has disappeared.

A near-contemporary bibliographical source gives the explanatory layer its own history. Adam Friedrich Glafey’s 1747 catalogue of Eucharius Gottlieb Rinck’s library summarizes Johann David Köhler’s preface to the large Weigel armorial of 1734. According to the catalogue, Köhler regretted that the German explanation of the colours occurring in shields had been neglected in later editions after Siebmacher’s death, and the catalogue adds that the same explanatory layer was still lacking in the newest Weigel edition prepared with Köhler’s involvement.[^20] The literal 1734 page remains to be recovered, so this should be cited as Glafey’s report, not as Köhler’s wording. But it shows that graphic tincture coding and verbal explanation could have different edition histories.

The *Erklärung der Farben* is therefore not merely an example in a history of hatching. It is a bibliographical component: a leaf that can recur, disappear, move between hosts, and operate together with the plates and prose around it.

## The key inside a changing annual book

The *Wappenkalender* was not an armorial whose date simply changed from year to year. Its prefaces and terminal pages show a repeated cycle of revision. The 1728 annual states that its 108 genealogical tables had been reviewed anew and enlarged *nach den neuesten Nachrichten* so that they could be used with *zuverlässiger Gewißheit* and *guter Bequemlichkeit* in *täglichen Zeitungs-Lesen*.[^21] The same copy ends with a page headed *Unter währenden Druck eingelauffene Zusätze und Verbesserungen*. Births, marriages, and deaths received while the book was already in press are tied to specific tables or pages.[^22]

**[FIG. 5A]** 1728 *Geneigter Leser!*: newest reports / daily newspaper reading.  
**[FIG. 5B]** 1728 in-press additions, image 8331489.

The 1733 annual repeats the procedure. Its preface describes the series as serving the *alljährliche Vermehrung und Verbesserung der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldic*, names a *guter Freund* resident in Rome who had supplied information about Italian princely houses, and again emphasizes revision from the newest and most reliable reports.[^23] At the end appears *Unter währenden Druck geschehene Veränderungen*, recording dynastic changes that occurred during printing and pointing back to the affected tables.[^24]

**[FIG. 5C]** 1733 *Geneigter Leser!*: annual improvement / Rome correspondent.  
**[FIG. 5D]** 1733 in-press changes, image 8335352.

The colour key was one small recurring component inside this changing reference book. The persons, houses, arms, tables, and corrections could change while the chromatic convention remained usable. A central key allowed new armorial material to be read without re-explaining the entire code beside every shield.

A publisher’s retrospective of 1772 shows how such practical choices were remembered. The *Vorbericht* to *Der durchlauchtigen Welt vollständiges Wappenbuch* looked back at the large armorial tradition and listed several defects: too many arms forced figures into formats too small for adequate clarity; blazoning had not been sufficiently determinate; attempts to mark tinctures through letters caused *Unordnung und Mißverstand*; the number of images left too little room for description; and arms changed *von Zeit zu Zeit*.[^25]

**[FIG. 6A]** figures too small / unclear.  
**[FIG. 6B]** *Tincturen mit Buchstaben* / *Unordnung und Mißverstand*.  
**[FIG. 6C]** insufficient room for description / *von Zeit zu Zeit*.

The preface is retrospective and should not be attributed to Köhler in 1723. Its value is different. An eighteenth-century publisher himself placed colour notation beside figure size, descriptive prose, and the changing objects represented by the book. The practical problem does not need a modern vocabulary to become visible.

## A key survives the change of format

The *Erklärung der Farben* did not disappear with the last numbered Köhler/Weigel annual in 1757. A digitized copy of Johann Friedrich Seyfart’s *Der durchlauchtigen Welt vollständiges Wappenbuch* at Tübingen, catalogued under 1771 but bearing a title page dated 1772, opens in the sequence:

*Vorsatz → Erklärung der Farben → Titelblatt → Vorbericht*.[^26]

The colour key stands before the title page of the new cumulative work.

**[FIG. 7A]** Tübingen Fe 18-1 *Erklärung der Farben*.  
**[FIG. 7B]** following title page.

The publisher’s preface explicitly states that the new project took *die bisher bey dem Wappencalender gewesenen Wappen zum Grunde gelegt*: the arms previously used in the *Wappencalender* were made the basis of the cumulative *Wappenbuch*.[^27] Because additional arms could only be gathered gradually, the new divisions were to be issued *jährlich mit dem Wappencalender* and eventually accumulated into several octavo volumes.[^28]

**[FIG. 7C]** *Vorbericht*: older *Wappencalender* arms used as basis / annual delivery.

This is direct publisher-language for continuity between formats. It does not establish identity of every copperplate, or of the colour-key matrix. It does establish that the armorial corpus of the annual was consciously carried into a cumulative publication and that an *Erklärung der Farben* remained part of the book world in the same years in which Göttingen historians were discussing painted and illuminated historical tables.

That continuity is enough to turn the article back toward Gatterer without requiring an influence genealogy.

# III. Göttingen Again: Coloured Historical Books

## Gatterer in the Nürnberg publication world

Gatterer did not encounter the Nürnberg genealogy-heraldry trade only as a remote predecessor to his Göttingen work. In 1755 his *Historia genealogica dominorum Holzschuherorum* was produced in Nürnberg, with Martin Tyroff among the engravers associated with its visual apparatus.[^29] The Weigel-heirs *Wappenkalender* was still being issued, and Tyroff was active in extending inherited heraldic plate stock. These facts do not connect Gatterer personally to the design of the *Erklärung der Farben*, but they place his work within the same production world of genealogical books and heraldic copperplates.

After Johann David Köhler’s death in 1755, Gatterer contributed to the posthumous closure of Köhler’s *Wöchentliche Historische Münz-Belustigung*. Modern bibliographic work attributes six numismatic pieces and an unpaginated biographical preface on Köhler to Gatterer in the delayed final material; Johann Tobias Köhler also participated, so the volume should not be described as Gatterer’s single-handed continuation.[^30] The important point is simply that Gatterer was already publishing within the Köhler–Weigel ecology before he took over the genealogy-heraldry handbook.

Gatterer’s *Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik* appeared in 1759 under the imprint of Christoph Weigel the Elder’s heirs and described itself as *aufs neue ausgefertigt* by Gatterer.[^31] Formal bibliographic controls identify it as the continuation of the older *Wappenkalender*. Whether a specific 1759 copy retains an *Erklärung der Farben* remains an open material check, but it no longer bears structural weight: the Tübingen 1771/72 *Wappenbuch* independently proves later persistence of the key.

The handbook itself continued to change. The 1761 issue again used *aufs neue ausgefertigt*; the 1762 issue advertised that it had been *ganz neu ausgearbeitet* and enlarged with many *Stammtafeln* and arms; the 1763 title added an *Abriß der Heraldik oder Wappenkunde*; in 1764 the annual label returned as *Fortgesetzter Wappen-Calender ... oder jährliches Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik*.[^32] By 1766 Gatterer’s *Abriß der Heraldik* was advertised *zum Nutzen der studirenden Jugend* and *mit acht Kupfertafeln erläutert*.[^33]

The old reference-book form was therefore not discarded when a more explicit teaching component entered it. Current genealogy and armorial plates continued to coexist with a handbook intended to teach readers how heraldry worked.

## Gatterer reading colour signs

The expanded *Abriß der Heraldik* supplies a more explicit connection to the older colour-sign literature. On p. 12 of the second edition (Nürnberg: Raspe, 1774), Gatterer discusses two additional hatch signs attributed to Rink: one for *Naturfarbe* (fig. 19) and another for *Eisenfarbe* (fig. 20). He judged the first superfluous but the second useful because, he wrote, he had learned from old *Wappenbriefen* that heralds, at least in Germany, could use *Eisenfarbe* and *weise Farbe*, that is silver, as equivalent expressions.[^34]

The same paragraph then points readers backward: `Zur Erläuterung dieser Abhandlung vom Ursprunge der Schrafirungen dient das oben angeführte köhlerische Programma.` The programme can be identified as Johann David Köhler’s Göttingen *Programma De Inventoribus Incisurarum Gallice Les Hachures Dictarum Quibus Metalla Et Colores In Tesseris Gentilitiis Absque Pigmentis Indicantur* of 1736.[^35] Its subject is stated in its title: hachures by which metals and colours in coats of arms are indicated without pigments.

This is the strongest direct bridge in the article because it remains entirely on the level of books and colour signs. Köhler published a programme specifically about hachures for representing heraldic colours without pigment. Gatterer later discussed particular colour hatchings in his own printed textbook, tested one terminological distinction against old *Wappenbriefe*, and explicitly sent his reader back to Köhler’s programme. Nothing further has to be inferred about the transfer of a general historical method.

**[FIG. 8A]** 1759 *Handbuch* title/opening: Gatterer / Weigel heirs / *Aufs neue ausgefertigt*.  
**[FIG. 8B]** Gatterer, *Abriß der Heraldik*, 2nd ed. (1774), p. 12: *Schrafirungen* / *Naturfarbe* / *Eisenfarbe* / *Wappenbriefen* / *köhlerische Programma*.  
**[FIG. 8C]** Gatterer 1774, Tab. II figs. 19–20: Rink’s signs for *Naturfarbe* and *Eisenfarbe*.

The 1774 second edition is bibliographically secure; its p. 12 wording is currently controlled through repeated exact transcription with a fixed primary locator, while direct BSB/MDZ page capture remains pending. A reproduction of Tab. II figs. 19–20 is already independently identified in public image repositories, but the final article should use the institutional scan where possible.[^36]

Gatterer’s 1771 preface *Von der historischen Erziehung* offers a complementary but less necessary piece of evidence. The modern critical edition locates a passage proposing active exercises in genealogy, heraldry, and numismatics, including colouring coats of arms, drawing or colouring family trees, cataloguing coins, and making or using coin copies in materials such as plaster, tin, lead, or glass.[^37] It now belongs in prose or a note rather than carrying a figure. Its significance lies in its ordinariness: colouring a coat of arms could be recommended as something a learner actually did on paper.

## What kind of coloured book did the reader receive?

The *Synopsis* can now be read again at the level of one especially revealing copy. Tübingen `Fn 28.2` contains one coloured and one uncoloured table within the relevant pair.[^38] The title’s *coloribusque illustratae* therefore cannot be projected uniformly onto every table in every surviving copy. The phrase “coloured table” names neither a stable edition-wide finish nor a sufficient bibliographical description.

The Nürnberg books offer an inverse problem. Their *Erklärung der Farben* could be fully monochrome while still communicating heraldic colours. A missing key could change the internal apparatus of a copy without removing all the encoded arms. The later cumulative *Wappenbuch* could retain the key after the publication format changed. In one group of books colour information survived without pigment; in another, pigment could be absent while the underlying engraved table survived.

This does not make heraldic tinctures and synchronistic tables the same kind of representation. A hatch pattern in a coat of arms stands for a conventional heraldic colour category. Colour applied to a world-historical table may distinguish political or temporal relations whose referents are not literally red, green, yellow, or black. The relation pursued here is bibliographical: colour has to be located in the physical state of a book rather than inferred from its subject or title alone.

Schlözer’s three phrases also separate more sharply when placed beside those books. *Facta färben* is an accusation about historical content. *Gemahlte chronologische Tabellen* describes a proposed painted sheet. *Illumination der Tabelle* names an operation performed on an already tabulated chronology. They are not one theory of colour; they are different uses of colour language attached to different textual and material objects.

Herder’s 1772 attack on Schlözer can therefore remain outside the centre of the present article. His questions about *Maaß* and the grounds for comparing heterogeneous historical societies are important for the history of universal history, but they raise a prior problem of historical commensuration rather than the immediate book-historical question pursued here.[^39] A footnote is enough to acknowledge the boundary.

The return to Göttingen is deliberately narrow. Nürnberg has not been used to explain the origin of Gatterer’s synchronistic tables. The article has instead reconstructed a publication history close enough to Gatterer’s own work to make a more elementary distinction unavoidable: a historical book’s colour must be located materially before it is interpreted conceptually.

# Colour in Books

Recent histories of colour printing have substantially enlarged the field. *Printing Colour 1700–1830*, edited by Margaret Morgan Grasselli and Elizabeth Savage, surveys coloured inks, printing processes, book illustration, typography, labour, and variant impressions across the long eighteenth century.[^40] Histories of hand-colouring have established that coloured copies could be supervised, corrected, unevenly finished, and materially divergent.[^41] Giulia Simonini’s *Color Charts in 18th-century Europe* has integrated the history of natural, pigmentary, and trichromatic colour charts and their relations among samples, names, recipes, and systems.[^42] Heraldic scholarship has long reconstructed tricking and hatching conventions and has increasingly treated armorials as material and communicative objects.

The *Erklärung der Farben* belongs beside these histories but is not identical to their most familiar objects. It is not primarily a colour print made with coloured ink, and it is not a chart made from actual colour samples. It is a reproducible black-line decoder linking graphic signs to named chromatic categories. Its book history can be followed through recurrence, copy loss, and re-hosting.

The distinction can be put plainly. Histories of colour printing ask how colour was put onto paper. These books also show how colour could be carried when colour was not put onto paper.

The 1744 *Erklärung der Farben* makes that point visible without additional theory: a black page explains colour; the next plate uses the code; the prose later names the colours again. Other annuals show the key recurring over decades; a defective 1729 copy shows it missing; a cumulative 1771/72 *Wappenbuch* still places the key before its title page and explicitly declares its dependence on the old annual armorial corpus. Gatterer worked inside that publication environment and, in his own heraldic textbook, explicitly directed readers from a discussion of colour hatchings to Köhler’s earlier programme on representing heraldic metals and colours without pigments. He also published a *Synopsis* whose advertised colour survives inconsistently across copies.

The route from Göttingen to Nürnberg and back needs to be made only once. It is not a causal genealogy. Nürnberg does not explain the whole of Göttingen historical method, and Göttingen does not retroactively make heraldic hatching into universal history. The retrospective instead restores a material vocabulary to a set of late-eighteenth-century statements about coloured history. Colour could be a word, a letter, a hatch, a key, a hand-applied finish, a missing leaf, a classroom exercise, or an illuminated table.

The strongest contrast is also the simplest: **a black page could carry colour; a book advertised as coloured could survive without it.**
