# An Erklärung der Farben: Colour, Books, and Historical Method from Nürnberg to Göttingen

**Working draft v0 — 4 September 2026**  
**Target:** *Huntington Library Quarterly*  
**Status:** first complete article pass; image-driven; repository stuffing pass to follow  
**Core locked:** colour + book history first; Göttingen → Nürnberg → Göttingen; no general theory of knowledge production.

## Abstract

In 1772 August Ludwig Schlözer warned historians against making or at least “colouring” their facts. Within a year, however, he was prepared to defend *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen* as aids to historical synchronism, and in a later edition he proposed the *Illumination der Tabelle* as a way of making unequal historical durations visible to the eye. Johann Christoph Gatterer, Schlözer’s Göttingen rival, had meanwhile published a *Synopsis historiae universalis* whose title advertised two copper-engraved tables *coloribusque illustratae*. Surviving copies do not all preserve the same chromatic state. Some are coloured, some uncoloured, and at least one known witness is mixed.

This article approaches these Göttingen uses of colour through the book history of an older Nürnberg publishing tradition. From the 1720s through the 1750s, the Weigel/Köhler *Wappenkalender* repeatedly included an engraved *Erklärung der Farben*: a monochrome page that translated heraldic colours into dots and hatchings, immediately followed by armorial images using the same signs and, in a directly inspected 1744 copy, by prose descriptions that named the colours again. The colour key belonged to a serial publication in which genealogical and heraldic information was revised annually, corrected while the book was in press, and repackaged under new editorial and commercial regimes. Gatterer entered this Nürnberg print world before taking up his Göttingen chair, contributed to the posthumous completion of Köhler’s numismatic serial in 1756, and from 1759 revised the inherited genealogy-heraldry handbook before adding a student-oriented *Abriß der Heraldik* with explanatory copperplates.

Rather than treating colour as a metaphor for historical knowledge, the article follows its concrete states in books: named but not printed, encoded by letters, converted into hatching, supplied through a legend, added by hand, omitted from a copy, or assigned to a historical table. The result is a short book-historical return from Göttingen to Nürnberg and back. It suggests that the late-eighteenth-century debate over coloured historical representation becomes sharper when read against an older publishing practice in which colour had long been something a book could communicate even when no coloured ink or pigment appeared on the page.

## 1. Colouring Facts

In 1772 August Ludwig Schlözer described the materials of universal history with a chromatic image. Historical facts, he suggested, resembled the coloured stones of a mosaic. They were extracted from annals and monuments through criticism; the historian then distributed and combined them until a larger picture emerged. Yet in the same discussion Schlözer drew a line around what the historian was permitted to colour. The notorious writers who did not simply invent their facts, he remarked, at least “coloured” them — *färben sie wenigstens*. The objection was not to colour as such. It was to colouring at the level of the *Factum*.

[FIGURE 1A — Schlözer 1772, pp. 44–45: tight crop around *farbichte Steinchen*, *Facta färben*, and *Zusammenstellung*.]

That distinction becomes stranger when the next few years are read as a history of books rather than as a sequence of metaphors. In the second part of his *Vorstellung seiner Universal-Historie* in 1773, in the course of answering Johann Gottfried Herder, Schlözer defended at least the pedagogical possibility of *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen*. A painted sheet, he proposed, might place Solomon’s Temple, the Egyptian pyramids, and Homer together before the eye so that their simultaneity — *Synchronismus* — would remain fixed through the *Association der Ideen*. In the revised 1775 edition, a further note proposed the *Illumination der Tabelle* so that the unequal length and duration of five great periods of world history might become visible to the young eye.

[FIGURE 1B — Schlözer 1773 p. 327, crop around *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen*, *Synchronismus*, *Association der Ideen*. DIRECT PAGE IMAGE STILL TO CLOSE.]  
[FIGURE 1C — Schlözer 1775, crop around *Illumination der Tabelle* and unequal *Länge und Dauer*. EXACT PAGE STILL TO CLOSE.]

These passages should not be made to prove a philosophy of colour. They make a narrower point. Around the very moment when Göttingen universal history became the object of sharp professional and methodological quarrels, historians were also talking with surprising literalness about coloured historical pages. The words mattered because such pages existed.

Johann Christoph Gatterer’s *Synopsis historiae universalis*, first published in 1766 and revised in 1769, announced its material form in its title: six tables, two of them engraved in copper and *coloribusque illustratae*. The phrase did not describe colour in the represented objects. It described the tables. André de Melo Araújo has already shown how these tables used colour together with lines and spatial disposition to make historical simultaneity and political change visible. That historiographical point is established. What matters here is more elementary: the colour promised by the title was not materially stable across surviving books.

Known copies include coloured, uncoloured, and mixed states. In a Tübingen witness discussed in the existing copy census, one of the relevant tables is coloured while another is not. The engraved geometry survives whether or not pigment does. A reader of one copy could therefore encounter a visibly coloured historical instrument where the reader of another encountered much the same copper-engraved structure in monochrome.

[FIGURE 2A — title crop: *quarum duae in aes incisae coloribusque illustratae sunt*.]  
[FIGURE 2B — coloured *Synopsis* table, direct named witness preferred.]  
[FIGURE 2C — uncoloured or mixed comparable table; ideally same table/design, otherwise Tübingen mixed pair.]  

The problem raised by these books is not whether Gatterer “believed in visual history” or whether Schlözer did. Scholarship on Gatterer’s visual and material historiography has made such a claim too general to be useful. The simpler question is what eighteenth-century books meant materially when they promised, proposed, omitted, encoded, or applied colour. A coloured historical table belonged to a longer print history in which colour was frequently present as information without being present as pigment.

To see that history, it is useful to leave Göttingen almost as soon as the dispute has begun and return to Nürnberg.

## 2. Colour without Colour

A page headed *Erklärung der Farben* ought, at first glance, to contain colours. The copy of the Weigel/Köhler *Geschichts-, Geschlechts- und Wappen-Calender* for 1744 preserved and digitised by the Universitäts- und Landesbibliothek Düsseldorf does not. Its colour explanation is a full-page copper engraving printed in black ink. Small shield-shaped fields are filled instead with dots, vertical lines, horizontal lines, diagonal lines, cross-hatching, and conventional fur patterns. Beside them stand colour names in French and German: *d’Or / Gold*, *d’Argent / Silber*, *de Gueules / Roth*, *d’Azur / Blau*, *de Sable / Schwartz*, *de Sinople / Grün*, together with vair, ermine, purple, iron, and natural colour.

[FIGURE 4A — 1744 ULB Düsseldorf *Erklärung der Farben*, image 8331529. THIS SHOULD BE THE ARTICLE’S LARGE ANCHOR IMAGE.]

The page is a colour reference that contains no coloured sample. It asks the reader to move between a black graphic sign and a named chromatic category. Dots are not gold, and horizontal lines are not blue, yet in the heraldic grammar of the book they can carry those meanings. The page is therefore best understood in the first instance not as an example of colour printing but as a monochrome key to colour.

Its function is confirmed by its immediate neighbourhood. The next image in the same 1744 copy begins the armorial section with the *Kayserliches Wappen*. The imperial arms are densely engraved with the same kinds of dots, lines, diagonals, and cross-hatchings displayed on the preceding key. One does not have to decode every tiny field to establish the relation: the reader has just been taught a graphic vocabulary and immediately sees that vocabulary deployed inside an actual heraldic image.

[FIGURE 4B — detail from *Kayserliches Wappen*, image 8331530, selecting 4–5 easily legible hatch types corresponding to Fig. 4A.]

Later in the volume, the book says the colours again. At printed p. 87 begins the *Kurtze und deutliche Beschreibung aller hierinnen vorkommenden Wappen / Nach dem Alphabet*. Here the colours that the copperplates encode through line patterns reappear as words: *Gold*, *roth*, *schwartz*, *blau*, *silbern*, and related forms recur through the blazons. The relation between key, image, and prose is therefore directly visible within one surviving copy.

[FIGURE 4C — p. 87, image 8331757, crop showing several tincture words in prose blazon.]

The three panels should be read together. The colour information in this book is distributed. It can be accessed through a central engraved legend, through the hatching of the shield itself, or through prose description. The same volume also contains 125 genealogical tables, giving the armorial images a dynastic reference context. Calling the book “illustrated” does not adequately describe this structure. More importantly for the present argument, calling it “coloured” or “uncoloured” does not either. A monochrome copy can contain a substantial quantity of chromatic information.

The 1744 witness was not an isolated experiment. Detailed physical-copy descriptions record a full-page engraved *Erklärung der Farben* in annuals for 1728, 1732, 1737, 1738, 1744, 1751, 1755, and 1757. The number of armorial plates changes across these issues, but the colour-explanation module recurs. A 1745 copy description explicitly says that the colours were represented *durch Schraffierung*. The evidence supports continuity of a recurring book component, though not yet continuity of one physical copperplate. To claim that the same copper matrix survived for three decades would require direct comparison of lettering, outlines, dimensions, defects, and wear. The argument here needs only the more modest bibliographical fact: readers of successive annuals repeatedly encountered a monochrome colour key in a changing corpus of arms.

The strongest proof that this key was a distinct material component comes from failure. A surviving 1729 copy is catalogued as incomplete. Among the missing matter is “1 gest. Tafel ‘Erklärung der Farben’,” alongside two missing armorial engravings and other leaves. That description should not be mistaken for evidence that the 1729 edition was issued without the key. It is precisely the opposite: the copy is defective because an expected colour-explanation plate has disappeared.

The missing leaf makes a basic book-historical point visible. A copy could retain many armorial images while losing the easiest internal device for decoding their colour signs. The same encoded arms could therefore survive in two materially different reading environments: one with the legend present and one without it. The prose blazons or a reader’s prior heraldic knowledge might compensate, but the book no longer offers the same route through its own colour system.

[OPTIONAL FIGURE 4D or small inset — catalogue/collation detail documenting the missing 1729 *Erklärung der Farben*. This need not be a full formal figure if permissions/image quality are poor.]

This is one reason the history of colour in books cannot be reduced to a census of pigment. The page that most explicitly explains colour may itself be black and white, while a copy from the same publication tradition may become chromatically poorer simply by losing that page.

## 3. Before the *Erklärung*: Words, Letters, Lines, and Illumination

The Nürnberg colour key did not invent the problem it solved. Heraldic printing had long separated semantic colour from the physical colour of the printed page, and printers, authors, and readers had developed several overlapping ways of reconnecting them.

Johann Siebmacher’s *New Wapenbuch* of 1605 announced a *Declaration* of the proper colours so that the work could be completed and so that an individual purchaser might have the arms *illuminiren und erhöhen lassen*. The wording is important because it makes colouring an anticipated post-print operation. The printed book could provide the structural forms of the arms and the verbal information required to colour them without requiring every impression to leave the press already coloured.

[FIGURE 3A — Siebmacher 1605 title/Declaration crop around *gehörigen Farben* and *illuminiren und erhöhen*. Page image desirable; bibliographic wording currently controlled.]

A later solution compressed colour information into the image. Scholarly descriptions of the 1609 second part of Siebmacher’s armorial report lower-case letters such as *g*, *b*, and *w* to indicate Gold, Blau, and Weiss/Silber on the plates. That image-level claim still requires direct primary inspection before it should carry much weight in the finished article, but the broader practice of indicating heraldic tinctures by initials is independently and explicitly attested.

Philipp Jakob Spener’s *Historia insignium illustrium* of 1680 explains that his engraved tables followed the French practice of marking metals and colours by dots and differently directed lines. Germans, he wrote, had hitherto commonly used the initial letters of colours. More revealingly, Spener specified what happened when the graphic system reached its limit: if the figures were too small for the lines to distinguish the colours adequately, the reader had to return to the printed explanation — *ad explicationem typis expressam recurrere necesse erit*.

[FIGURE 3B — optional 1609 letter-coded arm detail if direct image can be secured.]  
[FIGURE 3C — Spener 1680 hatching example and/or tight preface crop around *literis initialibus colorum* and prose fallback.]

The sequence should not be turned into a technological progress narrative in which prose was replaced by letters and letters by superior hatching. These systems overlapped. Some books supplied verbal colour information so that copies could later be illuminated; some embedded abbreviated letters into the plate; some translated colour into hatching; some retained prose as a fallback when the image became too small. A single copy might combine more than one of these states.

What changes by the eighteenth-century *Wappenkalender* is less the invention of hatching than its installation in a serial book structure. The 1744 *Erklärung der Farben* concentrates the code in one engraved leaf, the armorial plates draw on that code repeatedly, and prose later repeats the chromatic information. A stable chromatic grammar could therefore accompany an annually changing set of dynasties and arms.

The longevity of the key also belonged to a publication in motion.

## 4. A Colour Key in an Annual Book

The *Wappenkalender* was not simply an armorial whose cover date changed each year. Its prefaces and terminal pages show an editorial rhythm in which information was repeatedly revised, printed, corrected, and revised again. The 1728 volume says that its 108 genealogical tables had been reviewed anew and enlarged according to the newest reports. The stated purpose was practical and current: they were to be useful with reliable certainty and convenience in *täglichen Zeitungs-Lesen*. Because space was limited, the tables privileged living rulers and retained the dead principally when they remained necessary for understanding the present.

The same volume then ends with a page headed *Unter währenden Druck eingelauffene Zusätze und Verbesserungen*: additions and improvements received while printing was underway. New marriages, deaths, births, and other changes are tied back to specific tables or pages. A fixed sheet was already outdated before the book was complete, and the solution was not to reset the whole book but to append a delta to the printed state.

[FIGURE 5A — 1728 *Geneigter Leser!* detail: *alljährigem Vermehrung*, *neuesten Nachrichten*, *täglichen Zeitungs-Lesen*.]  
[FIGURE 5B — 1728 terminal heading and one specific table/page correction, ULB image 8331489.]

The 1733 annual repeats the logic even more explicitly. It recalls that the series had been established for the *alljährliche Vermehrung und Verbesserung der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldic*. A friend in Rome had supplied information for several Italian princely houses. The tables had again been revised from the newest and most reliable reports. At the end of the volume appears another terminal patch, now headed *Unter währenden Druck geschehene Veränderungen*, recording changes that occurred while the book was in press and directing the reader back to the affected genealogical locations.

[FIGURE 5C — 1733 *Geneigter Leser!* crop around annual improvement / Rome correspondent / newest reports.]  
[FIGURE 5D — 1733 terminal *Unter währenden Druck geschehene Veränderungen*, image 8335352.]

The colour key was one small, recurring element inside this larger annual object. Its chromatic vocabulary could remain relatively stable while the identities and descriptions of arms, the genealogical tables, and the corrections attached to them changed. This matters because the history of the key is inseparable from the format that carried it. It was not a free-standing theory of colour. It was a tool in a serial reference book designed to be used again against new political and genealogical information.

A retrospective publisher’s preface from 1772 makes the practical stakes unusually explicit. Introducing the cumulative *Wappenbuch*, the publisher looked back at the large armorial tradition associated with Siebmacher and described a cluster of defects. Attempts to include too many arms had forced figures into formats too small for sufficient clarity. Older blazoning had not been sufficiently determinate. Attempts to indicate tinctures with letters had caused *Unordnung und Mißverstand*. A large number of images left inadequate room for description. And coats of arms themselves changed and acquired augmentations *von Zeit zu Zeit*.

[FIGURE 6A — 1772 *Vorbericht*, crop around small/unclear figures.]  
[FIGURE 6B — crop around *Tincturen mit Buchstaben* and *Unordnung und Mißverstand*.]  
[FIGURE 6C — crop around insufficient descriptive space and/or arms changing *von Zeit zu Zeit*.]

This is a retrospective account, not Johann David Köhler’s own statement from 1723, and it should be identified as such. But it is valuable precisely because a publisher inside the same long-lived Nürnberg ecology described earlier armorial formats in terms of their material limitations. Colour coding appears here beside legibility, page space, description, and updating. The preface then presents Köhler’s 1723 *Wappenkalender* as an attempt to remedy at least some of these inconveniences for princely arms.

The point is not that Nürnberg publishers discovered a modern concept of information design. The point is that they had an explicit history of what worked and failed when colour information had to travel through books. By 1772, letters could be remembered as a source of misunderstanding; by the late 1720s the annuals had long used a separate engraved hatching key; and the same publishing tradition had also learned to patch information that changed while pages were being printed.

It is at this point that the line back to Göttingen becomes materially short.

## 5. Gatterer in the Weigel–Köhler Print World

Johann Christoph Gatterer did not encounter the Nürnberg genealogy-heraldry trade only as a distant predecessor to his later Göttingen historical programme. His early career was embedded in it.

In 1755 his *Historia genealogica dominorum Holzschuherorum* was produced in Nürnberg, with Martin Tyroff among the engravers involved in its visual apparatus. The annual *Wappenkalender* was still being issued for Christoph Weigel’s heirs. Johann David Köhler died that year. In 1756 Gatterer then contributed substantially to the posthumous closure of the final part of Köhler’s *Wöchentliche Historische Münz-Belustigung*: six numismatic pieces and an unpaginated biographical preface on Köhler are attributed to him in the delayed final material. Johann Tobias Köhler also participated in the completion; this was not Gatterer’s single-handed continuation of the work. It was nevertheless a direct editorial and publication connection.

Two years later the genealogy-heraldry series changed hands in a way that makes the transition unusually concrete. Gatterer’s 1759 *Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik* was published *Im Verlag Christoph Weigels, des ältern, seel. Erben*. It presented itself as *aufs neue ausgefertigt* by Gatterer and retained the familiar pairing of armorial engravings and genealogical tables. The old *Wappenkalender* had not vanished into a bibliographical past; it was being revised under Gatterer’s name within the inherited publishing ecology.

[FIGURE 7A — 1759 *Handbuch* title/opening, showing Gatterer + Weigel heirs + *aufs neue ausgefertigt*.]  
[FIGURE 7B — PRIORITY CHECK: does the 1759 copy retain an *Erklärung der Farben*? If yes, reproduce whole key or a diagnostic detail beside 1744. If absent, document the absence carefully in the text rather than forcing a figure.]

The sequence after 1759 is revealing at the level of titles and contents. The 1761 *Handbuch* again used the language *aufs neue ausgefertigt*. The 1762 issue was *ganz neu ausgearbeitet* and advertised additional genealogical tables and arms. In 1763 the title added an *Abriß der Heraldik oder Wappenkunde*. In 1764 the annual label returned explicitly: *Fortgesetzter Wappen-Calender ... oder jährliches Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik*. By 1766 Gatterer’s *Abriß der Heraldik oder Wappenkunde* was explicitly directed *zum Nutzen der studirenden Jugend* and explained with eight copperplates; contemporary descriptions place the treatise and its plates in the same calendar/handbook environment.

[FIGURE 7C — 1766 *Abriß* title or explanatory plate, ideally a tincture/hatching plate rather than a generic heraldic image. Direct p. 12 / associated figures still high-value closure.]

This is the only bridge the article needs. It does not prove that Gatterer derived universal history from a heraldic colour key. It does not prove that one copperplate or one conceptual vocabulary travelled intact from Köhler to Göttingen. It establishes something simpler: Gatterer participated in the posthumous Köhler serial, revised an inherited Nürnberg genealogy-heraldry publication, and gradually attached explicit heraldic instruction and explanatory copperplates to that book environment before and during the formation of his Göttingen historical programme.

If direct inspection shows that the 1759 *Handbuch* retained the earlier *Erklärung der Farben*, the bridge becomes even more concrete. If it did not, the argument remains intact. The transition can be written as one from a recurrent colour decoder embedded in a commercial annual to a Gatterer handbook that retained and enlarged the larger armorial/genealogical apparatus while increasingly teaching readers how to interpret heraldry. The material connection is strong enough without a claim of intellectual inheritance.

## 6. Back to Göttingen: What Kind of Coloured Book?

Returning to the Göttingen controversy after Nürnberg changes the scale of the question. The temptation is to ask what colour “meant” for Gatterer or Schlözer. The books suggest a more literal question: what kind of coloured historical object did a reader actually receive?

The title of Gatterer’s *Synopsis* promised that two copper-engraved tables were *coloribusque illustratae*. Yet the pigment was hand-applied, and surviving witnesses differ. The material category “coloured table” therefore cannot simply be inferred from the edition title and projected uniformly across all surviving copies. A copy may be fully coloured, uncoloured, or mixed; the line work and placement remain even where the pigment does not.

This distinction is familiar in the history of hand-coloured scientific and illustrated books. Research on natural-history illustration has already shown that hand-colouring raises problems of supervision, recipes, standardisation, correction, and copy variation. The point here is not to rediscover those problems. It is to notice that a historical table can belong to the same material world while using colour for a very different object. In a coat of arms, hatching allows the reader to recover the semantic tincture of a represented shield. In the *Synopsis*, pigment can mark a distinction assigned to the historical table itself. The physical operation — adding colour to paper — may be comparable; what the colour refers to is not.

Nürnberg also makes Schlözer’s language look less paradoxical without requiring a theory of “epistemic jurisdiction.” The same eighteenth-century book culture could distinguish several mundane acts that all involved colour. One could specify a colour in prose. One could replace it with a letter. One could convert it into hatching. One could provide a central legend. One could ask a purchaser or colourist to illuminate a printed impression. One could colour a synchronistic table. And one could accuse a historian of colouring a fact.

The proximity of the words should not erase the differences among the acts. Schlözer’s 1772 warning against *Facta färben* concerned historical statements. His 1773 *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen* concerned a proposed didactic sheet in which historical objects became co-present and memorable. The 1775 *Illumination der Tabelle* concerned the visible proportion of historical periods. These were not three uses of one stable technical term. But once placed beside actual coloured and colour-coded books, they no longer need to be treated as purely metaphorical language either.

This is also where the famous Göttingen quarrels belong in the article — briefly. Gatterer and Schlözer competed over students, fees, teaching, and priority in universal history. Their programmes were close enough that each could accuse the other of appropriation. Gatterer complained of the theft of his *Plan*, *Denkungsart*, *Methode*, and discoveries; Schlözer issued a *Species Facti*, and Gatterer answered it in print in 1773. The dispute itself has not been shown to turn explicitly on colour. It need not.

What the dispute supplies is a historical setting in which the form of universal history had become professionally consequential. Tables, outlines, synchronistic arrangements, and teaching aids belonged to that setting, but colour should not be smuggled into the polemic where the sources do not place it. The cleaner claim is that the quarrel surrounded historians who were simultaneously publishing, proposing, and discussing historical books in which colour had specific material forms.

The Göttingen return can therefore remain at the level of books. Gatterer’s *Synopsis* was an edition whose chromatic promise was realized unevenly in surviving copies. Schlözer’s texts could condemn colouring facts while recommending painted or illuminated tables for particular didactic purposes. Gatterer’s heraldic handbooks emerged from an inherited Nürnberg publication world where colours had long been encoded without colour and where a central colour key formed one recurring part of the book.

No line of influence needs to be perfect for these objects to belong in the same article. Their historical proximity is sufficient to make the comparison meaningful, while their material differences keep it from becoming a genealogy.

## 7. Colour in Books

Histories of colour printing have recently expanded dramatically. The field now reconstructs coloured inks, multi-plate processes, intaglio colour, colour typography, hand-coloured illustration, pigments, recipes, labour, and copy variation across the long eighteenth century. The historiography of colour charts has likewise shown how physical samples, names, recipes, and numerical systems made colours comparable; studies of hand-colouring have established that coloured impressions were produced, supervised, corrected, and varied in ways that mattered scientifically and commercially. Heraldic scholarship has its own long history of tricking and hatching conventions, including the seventeenth-century debates over letters, dots, and lines.

This article does not propose another history of the invention of hatching, and it does not claim that colour in eighteenth-century books has been neglected. Its object sits between these literatures. The Nürnberg *Erklärung der Farben* belongs to a history of colour precisely because it is a colour instrument without coloured ink. It encodes chromatic categories through black marks, sits beside images that require those marks to be read chromatically, and coexists with prose that names the colours again. In a serial book it could recur for decades; in an individual copy it could disappear.

The distinction can be put plainly. Histories of colour printing ask how colour was put onto paper. The books examined here also ask how colour could be carried when colour was not put onto paper.

That problem does not end with monochrome hatching. Gatterer’s *Synopsis* reverses it. There the copper-engraved structure can survive without the hand-applied colour advertised by the title. Nürnberg begins with a black page that still communicates colour; Göttingen supplies a historical book whose colour may vanish while much of its engraved historical structure remains. Between those poles lie colour words, letters, hatchings, legends, prose blazons, colouring instructions, hand-finished impressions, and proposed painted tables.

This book history also explains why images should carry much of the argument. The 1744 *Erklärung der Farben* is not evidence for a theory that can be paraphrased away. Its point is visible in the relation between the legend, the following arms, and the later prose. The mixed or uncoloured state of a *Synopsis* witness is not simply a textual fact. It is a difference between books. The 1728 and 1733 correction pages make the annual character of the *Wappenkalender* visible in its own typography. The 1772 publisher’s complaint about letters belongs on the same page as the older systems it remembers.

For that reason, the article’s movement is deliberately short. It begins with a Göttingen dispute in which “colouring” could be both a charge and a proposed technique. It turns back to an older Nürnberg print world in which colour was routinely translated between words, signs, hatchings, legends, prose, and occasionally pigment. It then returns to Göttingen, not to prove that Nürnberg caused a historiographical method, but to recover the material specificity behind the language of coloured history.

A monochrome *Erklärung der Farben* is a useful object with which to end. It makes no claim to reproduce gold, silver, red, blue, black, or green perceptually. It gives the reader instead a set of conventional marks by which those colours can be recovered from a black printed page. That modest operation persisted inside books that were revised, corrected, rebound, incompletely preserved, and eventually taken up under new authors and publishers. By the time Göttingen historians were debating what could be coloured in universal history, German historical and auxiliary-historical books had already spent generations separating colour from pigment and putting the two back together in different ways.
