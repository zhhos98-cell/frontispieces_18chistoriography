# An *Erklärung der Farben*: Colour, Books, and Historical Method from Nürnberg to Göttingen

**Target:** *Huntington Library Quarterly*

**Movement:** Göttingen → Nürnberg → Göttingen

## Abstract

In 1772 August Ludwig Schlözer warned historians against making or at least “colouring” their facts. Within a year, however, he was prepared to defend *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen* as aids to historical synchronism, and in a revised edition he proposed the *Illumination der Tabelle* as a way of making unequal historical durations visible to the eye. Johann Christoph Gatterer, Schlözer’s Göttingen rival, had meanwhile published a *Synopsis historiae universalis* whose title advertised two copper-engraved tables *coloribusque illustratae*. Surviving copies do not preserve a uniform chromatic state: coloured, uncoloured, and mixed witnesses are known.

This article approaches those Göttingen books through the history of an older Nürnberg practice of communicating colour in print. The Weigel/Köhler *Geschichts-, Geschlechts- und Wappen-Calender* repeatedly included an engraved *Erklärung der Farben*: a monochrome page in which dots and hatchings stood for named heraldic colours. In a directly inspected 1744 copy the key is immediately followed by armorial plates using the same marks, while a later prose section names the tinctures again. Other copies show that the key was a separable bibliographical component: a defective 1729 copy is explicitly described as lacking it. The annuals in which it appeared were themselves revised from year to year and sometimes patched with additions and changes that arrived while printing was underway. A cumulative *Wappenbuch* preserved at Tübingen, catalogued under 1771 but bearing a 1772 title state, still places an *Erklärung der Farben* before its title page; its publisher explicitly states that arms formerly used in the *Wappencalender* supplied the basis of the new work.

Gatterer’s relation to this publication world was material as well as chronological. He worked with Nürnberg engravers in 1755, contributed to the posthumous closure of Johann David Köhler’s numismatic serial in 1756, and in 1759 issued the *Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik* under the Weigel-heirs imprint. During the 1760s the handbook was repeatedly revised and enlarged and came to include Gatterer’s *Abriß der Heraldik* with explanatory copperplates. In 1771 Gatterer’s programme for historical education included colouring coats of arms and family trees among practical exercises.

Rather than treating colour as a metaphor for historical knowledge, the article follows concrete bibliographical states: colour named but not printed, encoded by letters, converted into hatching, supplied through a key, added by hand, lost with a leaf, retained across changes of format, or proposed for a historical table. The Göttingen discussion of coloured historical representation thus appears against a longer book history in which colour could be communicated even when no coloured pigment appeared on the page.

## 1. Colouring Facts

In 1772 August Ludwig Schlözer described the materials of universal history with a chromatic image. Historical facts were like the small coloured stones of a mosaic. Criticism recovered or tested those facts from annals and monuments; the historian then distributed and combined them until a larger *Gemälde* appeared. Yet the same passage drew a limit around one kind of colouring. The writers who did not simply make their facts, Schlözer remarked, at least “coloured” them — *färben sie wenigstens*.[^1] The remark has been noticed before, and there is no need to turn it into a general philosophy of colour. What matters here is its proximity to a cluster of much more literal proposals about coloured historical pages.

**[FIG. 1A]** Schlözer, *Vorstellung seiner Universal-Historie*, I (1772), pp. 44–45: tight crop including the coloured mosaic, *Facta färben*, and *Zusammenstellung*.

In the second part of the *Vorstellung*, published in 1773 with Schlözer’s answer to Johann Gottfried Herder, he defended at least the pedagogical possibility of *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen*. A painted sheet might place Solomon’s Temple, the Egyptian pyramids, and Homer together before the eye. Their co-presence would help fix their simultaneity — *Synchronismus* — through the *Association der Ideen*.[^2] In the revised 1775 edition a further note proposed the *Illumination der Tabelle* so that the unequal *Länge und Dauer* of five main periods of world history could be made visible to a young viewer.[^3]

**[FIG. 1B]** Schlözer 1773, p. 327: *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen*, *Synchronismus*, *Association der Ideen*.  
**[FIG. 1C]** Schlözer 1775: *Illumination der Tabelle*, *Länge und Dauer*. Exact page to be closed before submission.

These passages belong to different textual settings and should remain distinct. The 1773 discussion is part of Schlözer’s answer to Herder; the separate 1773 *Species Facti* controversy with Gatterer concerned teaching, priority, and alleged borrowing between two Göttingen programmes of universal history.[^4] Colour has not been shown to have been an explicit subject of the *Species Facti* exchange. The quarrels matter here only because they locate these books and proposals in a setting where plans for representing universal history were professionally consequential.

Gatterer’s *Synopsis historiae universalis*, first published in 1766 and revised in 1769, makes the material problem more immediate. The title describes six tables, two of them engraved in copper and *coloribusque illustratae*.[^5] André de Melo Araújo has already shown that the *Synopsis* belongs to the history of visual and synchronistic historiography, and that hand-applied pigment could sharpen boundaries and major political changes while the engraved line geometry carried much of the historical structure.[^6] That is established prior art. The bibliographical complication is simpler: the colour advertised by the title is not materially uniform across surviving books.

Araújo’s copy census records coloured, uncoloured, and mixed witnesses, including a Tübingen copy in which one of the relevant tables is coloured and another is not.[^7] The exact pair selected for reproduction in this article should be directly inspected before publication, but the copy-level fact does not depend on reconstructing a new theory of the table. A reader might receive a conspicuously coloured historical instrument; another might receive substantially the same engraved structure in monochrome.

**[FIG. 2A]** *Synopsis* title: *sex tabulis comprehensa, quarum duae in aes incisae coloribusque illustratae sunt*.  
**[FIG. 2B]** Named coloured witness.  
**[FIG. 2C]** Named uncoloured witness, or preferably the Tübingen mixed copy with one coloured and one uncoloured table.

This is the point at which the article leaves Göttingen. The question is not whether either historian was “visual.” It is more elementary: what could an eighteenth-century book mean materially when it promised, proposed, encoded, omitted, or applied colour? A coloured historical table belonged to a print culture in which chromatic information could also be transmitted without pigment. To see that more clearly, we need to go backward to the books of genealogy and heraldry printed in Nürnberg.

## 2. Before Köhler: Good Copper, Bad Explanation

The genealogy-heraldry calendar was already a substantial illustrated genre before the long Weigel/Köhler series. Martin Schmeitzel’s *Einleitung zur Wappen-Lehre* of 1723 contains a useful contemporary judgment on an Augsburg genealogical-heraldic calendar of 1720. In a later exact transcription tied to p. 69 of the 1723 edition, Schmeitzel praised the arms as *sauber in Kupfer gestochen* but dismissed the attached explanation as *nichts werth*.[^8] The first-edition page still requires direct diplomatic inspection before the sentence is quoted in final form, but the distinction it records is already stable enough to matter: clean copper engraving and explanatory value were not the same thing.

The target was not an isolated curiosity. A surviving-copy description for the Augsburg *Genealogisch-Heraldischer Staats-Kalender* of 1723 lists an engraved frontispiece, seventy-four armorial copperplates, twelve text coppers, and folding genealogical trees.[^9] By the early 1720s, therefore, genealogy-heraldry calendars already combined multiple engraved and tabular components. The Weigel/Köhler publication entered an existing field in which readers and writers could judge the quality of an image separately from the quality of the explanation attached to it.

Heraldic colour intensified this separation because the relevant colours did not have to appear materially on the printed page. Johann Siebmacher’s *New Wapenbuch* of 1605 supplied a *Declaration* of the proper colours so that a purchaser could have the arms *illuminiren und erhöhen lassen*.[^10] The printed state and the final coloured state could therefore be different objects. Colour information might be supplied in words and subsequently turned into pigment by a colourist.

**[FIG. 3A]** Siebmacher 1605 *Declaration*: tight crop around *gehörigen Farben* and *illuminiren und erhöhen*.

Another solution put abbreviated colour information directly into the image. Modern heraldic scholarship reports lower-case letters in Siebmacher’s 1609 second part — *g*, *b*, and *w* for Gold, Blau, and Weiss/Silber — although a direct 1609 plate should be inspected before that specific image is reproduced here.[^11] More securely, Philipp Jakob Spener explained in 1680 that his copperplates used the French manner of dots and differently directed lines, *punctulis aut lineolis*, for metals and colours because Germans had hitherto been accustomed to initials, *literis initialibus colorum*.[^12]

Spener’s next sentence is as useful as the genealogy of the code. If the figures were too small for the lines to distinguish tinctures sufficiently, readers had to return to the printed explanation: *ad explicationem typis expressam recurrere necesse erit*.[^13] Hatching did not make prose obsolete. Image and explanation remained complementary because the graphic system had limits of scale.

**[FIG. 3B]** Optional 1609 letter-coded shield, only if directly controlled.  
**[FIG. 3C]** Spener 1680: hatching example and/or preface crop containing *literis initialibus colorum* and the printed-explanation fallback.

There is no need to make these materials into a teleology from words to letters to hatching. The systems overlapped, and hand colouring remained possible. The modest point is enough: by the time the eighteenth-century Nürnberg calendars were being issued, a reader could encounter heraldic colour as prose, initials, dots, lines, a separate legend, or applied pigment. The book did not have to be printed in colour to communicate colours.

## 3. A Monochrome *Erklärung der Farben*

A page headed *Erklärung der Farben* might be expected to contain colours. The copy of the Weigel/Köhler *Geschichts-, Geschlechts- und Wappen-Calender* for 1744 preserved by the Universitäts- und Landesbibliothek Düsseldorf does not. Its colour explanation is a full-page copper engraving printed in black ink.[^14] Small shield-shaped fields display dots, vertical lines, horizontal lines, diagonals, cross-hatching, and conventional fur patterns. French and German labels identify the tinctures: *d’Or / Gold*, *d’Argent / Silber*, *de Gueules / Roth*, *d’Azur / Blau*, *de Sable / Schwartz*, *de Sinople / Grün*, together with vair, ermine, purple, iron, and natural colour.

**[FIG. 4A — principal full-size image]** 1744 *Erklärung der Farben*, ULB Düsseldorf digital image 8331529.

The page is not a colour sample chart in the ordinary sense. It offers no visible sample of red, blue, green, gold, or silver. Instead it places a black graphic sign beside a named chromatic category. Dots are not gold and horizontal lines are not blue; they become legible as those tinctures within the heraldic convention supplied by the page. The *Erklärung* is therefore a monochrome colour key in the most literal sense.

The next image in the same copy confirms what the key is for. The armorial section begins with the *Kayserliches Wappen*, densely engraved with the same kinds of dots, lines, diagonals, and cross-hatchings displayed on the preceding page.[^15] It is unnecessary to decode every minute field of the imperial arms to establish the relation. The reader has just encountered a graphic vocabulary and then sees that vocabulary deployed in an actual armorial image.

**[FIG. 4B]** *Kayserliches Wappen*, image 8331530, cropped to several diagnostic hatch fields corresponding to Fig. 4A.

Later in the same volume the book states the colours again in words. Printed p. 87 begins the *Kurtze und deutliche Beschreibung aller hierinnen vorkommenden Wappen / Nach dem Alphabet*.[^16] The blazons repeatedly name tinctures and metals — *Gold*, *roth*, *schwartz*, *blau*, *silbern*, and related forms. The graphic code is therefore not the only way in which the 1744 book carries colour information.

**[FIG. 4C]** p. 87, ULB image 8331757, cropped around several explicit colour terms.

The three panels need to be read together. The key explains signs; the plate deploys them; the prose names the colours again. The same volume also contains a register of 125 genealogical tables.[^17] The armorial imagery belongs inside a larger genealogical reference object. “Illustrated” is too weak a description of the book, but “coloured” and “uncoloured” are also insufficient. A monochrome copy could carry extensive chromatic information because its black marks, labels, and prose were organized to do so.

The 1744 key was not unique to that year. Detailed physical-copy descriptions record a full-page engraved *Erklärung der Farben* in annuals for 1728, 1732, 1737, 1738, 1744, 1751, 1755, and 1757.[^18] A separate copy description also reports a colour-key copper in 1725, although the page itself has not yet been inspected.[^19] A 1745 copy description explicitly says that the colours are represented *durch Schraffierung*.[^20] These records establish recurrence of a module across the series; they do not establish that one physical copperplate survived unchanged throughout. That would require direct comparison of lettering, shield outlines, spacing, scratches, wear, and retouching.

A defective 1729 copy exposes the separateness of the key particularly well. Its collation lists among the missing material “1 gest. Tafel ‘Erklärung der Farben’,” along with two missing armorial engravings and other leaves.[^21] The description does not show that the 1729 edition was issued without the key. It records the history of one damaged or incomplete copy. Many encoded arms may remain materially present while the most convenient internal route for reading their colour signs has vanished.

This distinction between image and explanation also appears in a near-contemporary bibliographical witness to another Weigel armorial. Adam Friedrich Glafey’s 1747 catalogue of Eucharius Gottlieb Rinck’s library summarizes Johann David Köhler’s preface to the large Weigel *Wappen-Buch* of 1734. According to the catalogue, Köhler regretted that the German explanation of colours occurring in shields had been neglected in later editions after Siebmacher’s death. More strikingly, Glafey says that the same explanatory layer was still missing from the newest Weigel edition prepared with Köhler’s involvement.[^22]

The literal 1734 Köhler page remains to be recovered, so the 1747 catalogue should be cited as a near-contemporary synopsis, not as Köhler’s wording. But the book-historical point is already secure enough to sharpen the chronology. Graphic tincture coding and explanatory apparatus could have different edition histories. A large armorial might preserve ways of marking colours while losing a German explanation. The annual *Wappenkalender*, by contrast, repeatedly externalized tincture decoding into a separate engraved key.

Histories of heraldic hatching usually track conventions, inventors, or systems. Those histories are indispensable background, but the objects here are slightly different: the legend leaf, the armorial plate it decodes, the prose description that repeats colour names, and the individual copy in which one of those components may be missing. The *Erklärung der Farben* belongs as much to bibliography as to heraldry.

## 4. The Colour Key in an Annual Book

The *Wappenkalender* was not merely an armorial whose date changed each year. Its prefaces and terminal pages show a publication rhythm in which genealogical and heraldic material was repeatedly revised, printed, corrected, and revised again. The 1728 annual states that its 108 genealogical tables had been reviewed anew and enlarged according to the newest reports — *nach den neuesten Nachrichten vermehret*.[^23] Their announced use was current and practical: *mit zuverlässiger Gewißheit* and *guter Bequemlichkeit* in *täglichen Zeitungs-Lesen*. Because the available space was limited, the tables concentrated on living high-ranking persons and retained deceased predecessors principally where they remained necessary for understanding the present.

The same copy ends with a page headed *Unter währenden Druck eingelauffene Zusätze und Verbesserungen*.[^24] The additions and improvements had arrived while the book was in press. Marriages, births, and deaths are attached to specified table or page locations. A genealogical table could therefore become outdated before the whole volume had been printed, and the terminal page served as a patch to an already fixed portion of the book.

**[FIG. 5A]** 1728 *Geneigter Leser!*: *alljährigem Vermehrung*, *neuesten Nachrichten*, *täglichen Zeitungs-Lesen*.  
**[FIG. 5B]** 1728 terminal additions, image 8331489, including one concrete table/page correction.

The 1733 annual repeats this procedure and makes its information network more visible. Its preface recalls that the series existed for the *alljährliche Vermehrung und Verbesserung der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldic*. It reports that a *guter Freund* resident in Rome had supplied information about several Italian princely houses and states that the tables were revised from the newest and most reliable reports.[^25] At the end of the volume a page headed *Unter währenden Druck geschehene Veränderungen* records dynastic changes that occurred while printing was underway and points the reader back to the affected tables.[^26]

**[FIG. 5C]** 1733 *Geneigter Leser!*: annual revision, Rome correspondent, newest reports.  
**[FIG. 5D]** 1733 terminal *Veränderungen*, image 8335352.

The colour key was one small recurring component inside this changing annual book. The number and identity of represented persons, houses, arms, and tables could change; the reader could nevertheless continue to rely on a stable-looking convention for reading tinctures. This is a practical reason to centralize a code. New armorial material need not carry a fresh prose explanation of the entire chromatic vocabulary beside every shield.

A publisher’s retrospective of 1772 shows that eighteenth-century participants themselves remembered the history of those representational choices. The *Vorbericht* to *Der durchlauchtigen Welt vollständiges Wappenbuch* looked back at the large armorial tradition associated with Siebmacher and listed several defects.[^27] Large corpora forced figures into a format too small for adequate clarity. Earlier blazoning had not been sufficiently determinate. Attempts to mark tinctures through letters caused *Unordnung und Mißverstand*. The number of arms left too little room for description. Coats of arms themselves could also change and receive augmentations *von Zeit zu Zeit*.

**[FIG. 6A]** 1772 *Vorbericht*: figures too small / insufficient clarity.  
**[FIG. 6B]** *Tincturen mit Buchstaben* / *Unordnung und Mißverstand*.  
**[FIG. 6C]** insufficient descriptive room and/or *von Zeit zu Zeit*.

The *Vorbericht* was written roughly half a century after the beginning of Köhler’s calendar and must not be quoted as his original programme. It is valuable for another reason: it gives a publisher-side history of armorial books in which colour notation, figure size, explanatory prose, and temporal updating belonged to the same practical field. The preface then presented Köhler’s 1723 *Wappencalender* as an attempt to remedy these inconveniences, at least for princely arms.[^28]

The point need not be made more abstract than the source itself. Nürnberg publishers had experience with what readers could and could not recover from heraldic books. A letter code could be remembered as confusing. A hatching key could be supplied on a separate leaf. Prose could compensate for what the image did not communicate clearly. New information could arrive after the relevant table had been printed. Colour was part of that ordinary history of making a reference book usable.

## 5. From Annual Key to Cumulative *Wappenbuch*

The *Erklärung der Farben* did not disappear with the last numbered Köhler/Weigel annual in 1757. A fully digitized copy of Johann Friedrich Seyfart’s *Der durchlauchtigen Welt vollständiges Wappenbuch* at Tübingen, catalogued under 1771 but bearing a scanned title page dated 1772, preserves an unusually revealing opening sequence:[^29]

*Einband → Spiegel → Vorsatz → Erklärung der Farben → Titelblatt → Vorbericht*.

The colour key stands before the title page. Even as the armorial material was reorganized into a cumulative work, a monochrome decoder remained at the threshold of the book.

**[FIG. 7A]** Tübingen Fe 18-1, opening *Erklärung der Farben*.  
**[FIG. 7B]** Following title page, showing the new cumulative *Wappenbuch* host.

The publisher’s preface makes the relation to the older annual explicit. The new project took *die bisher bey dem Wappencalender gewesenen Wappen zum Grunde gelegt*: the arms previously used in the *Wappencalender* were made the basis of the cumulative work.[^30] Since additional arms could only be gathered gradually, the divisions were to be issued *jährlich mit dem Wappencalender* and ultimately accumulated into several octavo volumes.[^31]

**[FIG. 7C]** *Vorbericht*: crop containing *die bisher bey dem Wappencalender gewesenen Wappen zum Grunde gelegt* and, if legible at publication scale, *jährlich mit dem Wappencalender ausgegeben werden*.

This is direct actor-language for continuity between publication formats. It does not prove that every later image came from the same copperplate used in an earlier annual, nor does it establish the physical matrix history of the colour key. It does prove that the later publisher consciously built the cumulative project on the armorial corpus of the calendar and continued to use the calendar’s annual rhythm for distribution.

The Tübingen copy therefore matters for the article’s chronology in two ways. First, the *Erklärung der Farben* was not merely a mid-century feature that vanished before the Göttingen debates of the 1770s. A colour-key leaf remained part of the same broader armorial book world while Gatterer and Schlözer were publishing and arguing about historical tables. Second, the key survived a substantial change of host. The annual reference book and the cumulative *Wappenbuch* were different publication forms, but both could place the same kind of monochrome colour decoder in front of their armorial content.

This afterlife also reduces the burden placed on any single “bridge” between Nürnberg and Göttingen. The article need not prove that Gatterer personally carried one colour key northward or derived a method from it. The relevant book practices were still materially present in the publishing environment in which his own genealogy and heraldry books circulated.

## 6. Gatterer in the Weigel–Köhler Print World

Johann Christoph Gatterer did not encounter the Nürnberg genealogy-heraldry trade only as a historical predecessor to his later Göttingen work. His early publications were embedded in it.

In 1755 Gatterer’s *Historia genealogica dominorum Holzschuherorum* was produced in Nürnberg, with Martin Tyroff among the engravers associated with its visual apparatus.[^32] The annual *Wappenkalender* was still being issued for Christoph Weigel’s heirs, and Tyroff had been extending inherited heraldic plate stock during the same period.[^33] These facts do not show that Gatterer designed the calendar’s colour system. They place him in a working environment in which genealogical books, armorial copperplates, and publisher-held visual stock were already ordinary objects.

Johann David Köhler died in 1755. In the following year Gatterer contributed to the posthumous closure of Köhler’s *Wöchentliche Historische Münz-Belustigung*. Modern bibliographic work attributes six numismatic pieces and an unpaginated biographical preface on Köhler to Gatterer in the delayed final material.[^34] Johann Tobias Köhler also participated in the completion, so the volume should not be described as Gatterer’s single-handed continuation. The narrower point is sufficient: before Gatterer took over the genealogy-heraldry handbook, he was already publishing within the broader Köhler–Weigel serial ecology and helping frame Köhler’s work after his death.

Gatterer’s first *Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik* appeared in 1759 under the literal imprint *Im Verlag Christoph Weigels, des ältern, seel. Erben*. Its title presented the work as *aufs neue ausgefertigt* by Gatterer.[^35] Bibliographic controls identify the *Handbuch* as the continuation of the older genealogy-heraldry calendar. The exact status of the colour key in the 1759 copy remains the single most valuable unresolved material check: if an *Erklärung der Farben* survives there, its design should be compared directly with the earlier annuals and the later cumulative *Wappenbuch*; if it does not, the absence is equally useful and should be stated plainly.

**[FIG. 8A]** 1759 *Handbuch* title/opening: Gatterer, Weigel heirs, *aufs neue ausgefertigt*.  
**[FIG. 8B]** 1759 colour-key check, only if the leaf is directly present in the inspected copy.

The title-state sequence after 1759 shows the reference object continuing to change. The 1761 *Handbuch* again used *aufs neue ausgefertigt*. The 1762 issue advertised that it had been *ganz neu ausgearbeitet* and enlarged with many genealogical tables and coats of arms. In 1763 the title added an *Abriß der Heraldik oder Wappenkunde*. In 1764 the annual label returned explicitly: *Fortgesetzter Wappen-Calender ... oder jährliches Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik*.[^36] By 1766 Gatterer’s *Abriß der Heraldik oder Wappenkunde* was advertised *zum Nutzen der studirenden Jugend* and *mit acht Kupfertafeln erläutert*; contemporary descriptions place the treatise and its explanatory plates in the same calendar/handbook environment.[^37]

This sequence matters because the old reference book did not simply become a university treatise. Current genealogical tables and armorial material continued to be revised while an explicit heraldic teaching component was added to the publication. The relation between reference book and instructional book was additive rather than substitutive.

The internal order of the *Abriß* reinforces that modest reading. The 1766 contents place *Quellen der Wappenkunde* near the beginning before the extended technical treatment of heraldic forms and tinctures.[^38] A secondary transcription of the enlarged 1774 edition further attributes to Gatterer a discussion of Rink’s signs for *Naturfarbe* and *Eisenfarbe*, with old *Wappenbriefe* invoked in evaluating at least one distinction.[^39] The exact p. 12 passage and associated figures should be directly checked before use in the final article. It is not needed to make the main argument, but it may provide a compact final panel showing Gatterer himself discussing heraldic colour signs.

**[FIG. 8C]** Preferred if closed: Gatterer, *Abriß der Heraldik* 1774, p. 12 + relevant tincture/hatching figures. Otherwise use the 1766 title/plate evidence only.

The continuity established here is deliberately bibliographical and editorial. It is enough to show that Gatterer’s early and mid-career work overlapped directly with the Nürnberg publication ecology that had long carried colour keys, armorial plates, genealogical tables, and revised annual information. Nothing in this sequence requires the stronger claim that Nürnberg heraldry “caused” Gatterer’s later universal-history graphics.

## 7. Colouring as Historical Exercise

One Gatterer text makes the return to Göttingen particularly concrete. In the 1771 preface *Von der historischen Erziehung*, Gatterer proposed active exercises for learning genealogy, heraldry, numismatics, and related historical subjects. The modern critical edition and subsequent reconstruction locate a passage that includes colouring coats of arms, drawing or colouring family trees, cataloguing coins, and making or using copies of coins in materials such as plaster, tin, lead, or glass.[^40] The first-edition passage in the eight-page preface still needs direct page capture, but its content and location are secure enough to determine its role in the article.

**[FIG. 8D, or substitute for 8C]** Gatterer, *Von der historischen Erziehung* (1771): crop around colouring coats of arms / family trees once the first-edition page is captured.

This material should remain literal. Gatterer was recommending that learners colour an armorial representation or genealogical tree as part of historical education. The activity sat beside other acts of handling, copying, cataloguing, and reproducing historical objects. It did not require him to formulate a single abstract doctrine of colour.

The date is useful. In the same year Gatterer published his *Einleitung in die synchronistische Universalhistorie*. In 1772 Schlözer warned against colouring *Facta*. In 1773 he could defend painted chronological tables as pedagogical aids to synchronism. In 1775 he proposed illumination of a table to make unequal durations visible. These statements do not use colour in the same way, but they show that colouring and coloured pages were not remote metaphors imported by modern interpretation. They were ordinary enough operations for eighteenth-century historians to discuss in relation to books and teaching.

This also changes the role of the Gatterer–Schlözer controversy in the article. The *Species Facti* dispute remains relevant as evidence that plans for universal history could become objects of priority and professional conflict, but it need not carry the colour argument. The book-historical sequence is already sufficient: Gatterer’s genealogy-heraldry publications overlap with an older colour-decoding tradition; his historical pedagogy includes colouring exercises; his *Synopsis* advertises coloured tables; Schlözer discusses both illicitly coloured facts and legitimate painted or illuminated tables.

The fold back to Göttingen can therefore remain short.

## 8. Back to Göttingen: What Kind of Coloured Historical Book?

Returning to the Göttingen materials after Nürnberg changes the scale of the question. Instead of asking what colour “meant” for Gatterer or Schlözer in the abstract, we can ask what kind of coloured historical object a reader actually received.

The *Synopsis* advertised two copper-engraved tables *coloribusque illustratae*, but the pigment was hand-applied and surviving copies differ.[^41] The bibliographical category “coloured table” cannot simply be inferred from the title and projected uniformly across an edition. A copy may be coloured, uncoloured, or mixed. The engraved line work remains when pigment is absent. That fact does not make colour irrelevant; it makes its presence a property that must be controlled at copy level.

The Nürnberg books present the inverse case. Their *Erklärung der Farben* could be fully monochrome while still communicating heraldic colours. A missing key could alter a copy’s internal decoding apparatus without removing all of its armorial plates. A cumulative *Wappenbuch* could carry the key into a new host before its title page. In one set of books, colour information remains available when pigment is absent; in another, pigment can disappear while the engraved historical structure remains.

This comparison does not make heraldic tinctures and synchronistic tables the same kind of representation. The referents are different. A hatch pattern in a coat of arms points to a conventional heraldic colour category. Hand-applied pigment in a world-historical table may distinguish political or temporal relations whose objects are not literally red, green, yellow, or black. The point of the comparison is bibliographical rather than semantic. In both cases, “colour” has to be located in a particular material state of the book.

Schlözer’s wording becomes sharper in that setting. To “colour” a *Factum* was an accusation about the content of a historical claim. A *gemahlte chronologische Tabelle* was a proposed pedagogical sheet. *Illumination der Tabelle* described an operation performed on a table to make durations visible. Those phrases should not be collapsed into one theory. They name different things done to different paper objects.

Herder’s attack on Schlözer can remain at the edge of this argument. Herder questioned the *Maaß* and *Data zum Maaße* by which heterogeneous times and peoples could be brought into a universal historical plan.[^42] That is an important history of tabulation and commensuration, but it is not necessary to make colour carry it. For this article it is enough to note that colouring occurred after the table, the periodization, or the selected historical units already existed on the page. The colour-book history remains one step lower and more concrete.

The resulting picture of Göttingen is therefore less about a contest between text and image than about several kinds of historical books existing at once: prose programmes, painted-sheet proposals, synchronistic tables, coloured and uncoloured copies, heraldic handbooks, and practical exercises in colouring arms and family trees. The dispute over historical method happened in a world in which those objects were available to be printed, bought, bound, coloured, left uncoloured, or lost in incomplete copies.

## 9. Colour in Books

Recent histories of colour printing have made it impossible to claim that colour in eighteenth-century print culture has simply been neglected. Work on long-eighteenth-century colour printing reconstructs coloured inks, intaglio processes, multi-plate techniques, typography, labour, illustration, and variant impressions.[^43] Histories of hand-colouring have shown that coloured copies could be supervised, corrected, unevenly finished, and materially divergent.[^44] Giulia Simonini’s recent study of eighteenth-century colour charts has integrated natural, pigmentary, and trichromatic systems and the relation among samples, names, recipes, and ordered comparison.[^45] Heraldic scholarship, meanwhile, has a much older history of tricking and hatching conventions and a newer interest in armorial objects, makers, media, and material use.

The *Erklärung der Farben* belongs next to those histories but is not identical to their usual objects. It is not, in the first instance, a page on which coloured ink has been printed. Nor is it a chart made from actual colour samples. It is a reproducible black-line decoder that links a graphic convention to named chromatic categories. Its bibliographical history can be followed through recurrence, loss, reuse, and change of host.

That distinction can be stated plainly: histories of colour printing ask how colour was put onto paper. The books examined here also show how colour could be carried when colour was not put onto paper.

The argument need go no further. The 1744 *Erklärung der Farben* shows a black page explaining colour; the following armorial shows the code in use; the prose blazon names the colours again. Other annuals show the key recurring over decades; a defective copy shows it missing; a later cumulative *Wappenbuch* shows it still present after the publication format changed. The Weigel/Köhler succession places Gatterer directly within that book world. His later educational writing includes the colouring of arms and genealogical trees. His *Synopsis* then supplies a different copy problem: a work advertised as containing coloured tables survives in materially different chromatic states.

This is why the route from Göttingen to Nürnberg and back needs to be made only once. It is not an influence genealogy. The Nürnberg books do not explain the whole of Göttingen historical method, and the Göttingen texts do not retroactively turn heraldic hatching into universal history. The retrospective is useful because it restores the ordinary material vocabulary behind a set of late-eighteenth-century statements about coloured history. Colour could be a word, a letter, a hatch, a key, a hand-applied finish, a missing leaf, a classroom exercise, or an illuminated table. Before it became an interpretive problem for historians, it was already a problem that books had practical ways of carrying.
