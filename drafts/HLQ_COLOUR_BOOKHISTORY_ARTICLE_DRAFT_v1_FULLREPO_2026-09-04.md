## Abstract

In 1772 August Ludwig Schlözer warned historians against making or at least “colouring” their facts. Within a year, however, he was prepared to defend *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen* as aids to historical synchronism, and in a later edition he proposed the *Illumination der Tabelle* as a way of making unequal historical durations visible to the eye. Johann Christoph Gatterer, Schlözer’s Göttingen rival, had meanwhile published a *Synopsis historiae universalis* whose title advertised two copper-engraved tables *coloribusque illustratae*. Surviving copies do not all preserve the same chromatic state. Some are coloured, some uncoloured, and at least one known witness is mixed.

This article approaches these Göttingen uses of colour through the book history of an older Nürnberg publishing tradition. From at least the 1720s through the 1750s, the Weigel/Köhler *Wappenkalender* repeatedly included an engraved *Erklärung der Farben*: a monochrome page that translated heraldic colours into dots and hatchings, immediately followed by armorial images using the same signs and, in a directly inspected 1744 copy, by prose descriptions that named the colours again. The key was not a single isolated leaf in an otherwise stable armorial. It belonged to a serial publication in which genealogical and heraldic information was revised annually, patched while the book was in press, and repeatedly repackaged. A 1729 copy is described as defective because its colour-explanation plate is missing. A 1771 cumulative *Wappenbuch*, explicitly built on the arms formerly used in the *Wappenkalender*, still opens with an *Erklärung der Farben* before its title page.

Gatterer entered this Nürnberg print world before taking up his Göttingen chair. He worked with Nürnberg engravers in his 1755 *Historia genealogica*, contributed six numismatic pieces and a biographical preface to the posthumous completion of Johann David Köhler’s numismatic serial in 1756, and in 1759 revised the inherited genealogy-heraldry handbook under the Weigel-heirs imprint. During the 1760s that publication was repeatedly reworked and eventually supplemented with Gatterer’s student-oriented *Abriß der Heraldik* and explanatory copperplates. In 1771 Gatterer’s programme for historical education even included the colouring of coats of arms and family trees among practical exercises.

Rather than treating colour as a metaphor for historical knowledge, the article follows its concrete states in books: named but not printed, encoded by letters, converted into hatching, supplied through a legend, added by hand, omitted from a copy, or assigned to a historical table. The result is a short book-historical return from Göttingen to Nürnberg and back. It suggests that the late-eighteenth-century discussion of coloured historical representation becomes materially sharper when read against an older publishing practice in which colour had long been something a book could communicate even when no coloured ink or pigment appeared on the page.

## 1. Colouring Facts

In 1772 August Ludwig Schlözer described the materials of universal history with a chromatic image. Historical facts, he suggested, resembled the coloured stones of a mosaic. They were extracted from annals and monuments through criticism; the historian then distributed and combined them until a larger *Gemälde* appeared. Yet in the same discussion Schlözer drew a line around what the historian was permitted to colour. The writers who did not simply make their facts, he remarked, at least “coloured” them — *färben sie wenigstens*. The objection was not to colour in every form. It was to colouring the factual material itself.

[FIGURE 1A — Schlözer, *Vorstellung seiner Universal-Historie*, I (1772), pp. 44–45: tight crop around coloured mosaic stones, *Facta färben*, and *Zusammenstellung*. DIRECT PRIMARY CONTROL CLOSED.]

That distinction becomes more concrete when the next few years are read as a history of books rather than as a succession of metaphors. In the second part of the *Vorstellung* in 1773, in the course of answering Johann Gottfried Herder, Schlözer defended at least the pedagogical possibility of *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen*. A painted sheet, he proposed, might place Solomon’s Temple, the Egyptian pyramids, and Homer together before the eye. Their co-presence would make the objects vivid and help fix their simultaneity — *Synchronismus* — through the *Association der Ideen*. In the revised 1775 edition, a further note proposed the *Illumination der Tabelle* so that the unequal *Länge und Dauer* of five main periods of world history could be made visible to the young eye.

[FIGURE 1B — Schlözer 1773, p. 327: *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen*, *Synchronismus*, *Association der Ideen*. PAGE LOCUS SECURE; DIRECT IMAGE STILL TO CLOSE.]  
[FIGURE 1C — Schlözer 1775: *Illumination der Tabelle*, unequal *Länge und Dauer*. EDITION SECURE; EXACT PAGE STILL TO CLOSE.]

These passages should not be made to prove a philosophy of colour. They establish a narrower chronological fact. At the moment when Göttingen universal history was becoming the subject of highly visible professional and methodological quarrels, historians were also writing with surprising literalness about painted and illuminated historical pages.

Johann Christoph Gatterer’s *Synopsis historiae universalis*, first published in 1766 and revised in 1769, announced its material form in its title: six tables, two of them engraved in copper and *coloribusque illustratae*. André de Melo Araújo has already shown that these tables addressed the problem of representing historical simultaneity and that pigment could sharpen boundaries and major political changes while the engraved line geometry continued to carry substantial historical structure. That intellectual-historical interpretation is prior art. The book-historical complication is simpler: the colour promised by the title was not materially stable across surviving books.

Known witnesses include coloured, uncoloured, and mixed states. In a Tübingen copy recorded in the existing census, one of the relevant tables is coloured while another is not. The engraved geometry survives whether or not pigment does. One reader could therefore encounter a conspicuously coloured historical instrument where another reader encountered much the same copper-engraved structure in monochrome.

[FIGURE 2A — title crop: *Synopsis historiae universalis sex tabulis comprehensa, quarum duae in aes incisae coloribusque illustratae sunt*.]  
[FIGURE 2B — coloured table, named institutional witness.]  
[FIGURE 2C — uncoloured comparable table or, preferably, the Tübingen mixed state demonstrating one coloured and one uncoloured table in the same copy.]

The problem raised by these books is not whether Gatterer or Schlözer “believed in visual history.” Scholarship on Gatterer’s visual and material historiography is now too substantial for such a claim to do much work. The more elementary question is what an eighteenth-century book meant materially when it promised, proposed, encoded, omitted, or applied colour. A coloured historical table belonged to a longer print history in which chromatic information could be present even where pigment was absent.

To recover that history, it is useful to leave Göttingen almost as soon as the dispute has begun and return to Nürnberg.

## 2. Before Köhler: Good Copper, Bad Explanation

The genealogy-heraldry calendar was already a substantial illustrated genre before the long Weigel/Köhler run. A contemporary judgment by Martin Schmeitzel is useful because it separates two things modern discussions too easily collapse. In his *Einleitung zur Wappen-Lehre* of 1723, commenting on an Augsburg genealogical-heraldic calendar of 1720, Schmeitzel reportedly praised the arms as *sauber in Kupfer gestochen* while dismissing the attached explanation as *nichts werth*. The exact first-edition spelling and punctuation still await direct inspection of p. 69, but the notice is precisely located and stable in later heraldic transcription.

The contrast is worth retaining in its ordinary eighteenth-century terms. A heraldic book could contain cleanly engraved arms and poor explanation at the same time. Graphic execution and explanatory value were separable properties of one printed object. The genre Schmeitzel criticized was not ephemeral. A surviving-copy description for an Augsburg *Genealogisch-Heraldischer Staats-Kalender* of 1723 lists an engraved frontispiece, seventy-four armorial copperplates, twelve text coppers, and folding genealogical trees. By the time the Weigel series emerged, illustrated genealogy and heraldry already had a dense book architecture and competing publishers.

This context matters for colour because heraldic colour was one of the things such books had to explain without necessarily printing in colour. Johann Siebmacher’s *New Wapenbuch* of 1605 supplied a *Declaration* of proper colours so that the work might be completed and so that an individual purchaser could have the arms *illuminiren und erhöhen lassen*. The printed image and the finished coloured copy were thus not necessarily the same material state. Colour information could be supplied verbally and turned into pigment after printing.

[FIGURE 3A — Siebmacher 1605 declaration, crop around *gehörigen Farben* and *illuminiren und erhöhen*.]

A later solution compressed colour information into the image itself. Scholarship on the 1609 second part of Siebmacher reports lower-case letters such as *g*, *b*, and *w* for Gold, Blau, and Weiss/Silber. That specific plate-level claim should remain subordinate until a direct image is inspected, but the larger practice of using initials is secured by a stronger source. In 1680 Philipp Jakob Spener explained that his engraved tables used the French method of *punctulis aut lineolis*, dots and differently directed lines, because Germans had hitherto commonly indicated colours through their initial letters — *literis initialibus colorum*.

Spener also explained what happened when the graphic system failed. If figures were too small for the lines to distinguish colours sufficiently, readers had to return to the explanation in type: *ad explicationem typis expressam recurrere necesse erit*. Hatching therefore did not make text obsolete. Graphic code and printed explanation were complementary channels whose usefulness depended partly on scale.

[FIGURE 3B — optional direct 1609 letter-coded shield if secured.]  
[FIGURE 3C — Spener 1680 hatching example and/or preface crop around *literis initialibus colorum* and textual fallback.]

There is no need to turn this sequence into a story of technical progress from words to letters to lines. The systems overlapped, and later users could continue to colour impressions by hand. What matters for the eighteenth-century calendar is that a mature repertoire already existed for carrying heraldic colour through a monochrome printed book: prose, initials, dots, lines, legends, and subsequent illumination.

## 3. A Monochrome *Erklärung der Farben*

A page headed *Erklärung der Farben* ought, at first glance, to contain colours. The copy of the Weigel/Köhler *Geschichts-, Geschlechts- und Wappen-Calender* for 1744 preserved by the Universitäts- und Landesbibliothek Düsseldorf does not. Its colour explanation is a full-page copper engraving printed in black ink. Small shield-shaped fields are filled instead with dots, vertical lines, horizontal lines, diagonal lines, cross-hatching, and conventional fur patterns. Beside them stand colour names in French and German: *d’Or / Gold*, *d’Argent / Silber*, *de Gueules / Roth*, *d’Azur / Blau*, *de Sable / Schwartz*, *de Sinople / Grün*, together with vair, ermine, purple, iron, and natural colour.

[FIGURE 4A — 1744 ULB Düsseldorf *Erklärung der Farben*, digital image 8331529. LARGE ANCHOR IMAGE.]

This page is not a colour sample chart in the ordinary sense. It provides no visible sample of red, blue, green, gold, or silver. It asks the reader to move between a black graphic sign and a named chromatic category. Dots are not gold, and horizontal lines are not blue, yet within the heraldic grammar of the book they can carry those meanings. The page is therefore, at the most literal level, a monochrome key to colour.

Its function is confirmed by its immediate neighbourhood. The next image in the same 1744 copy begins the armorial section with the *Kayserliches Wappen*. The imperial arms are densely engraved with the same kinds of dots, lines, diagonals, and cross-hatchings displayed on the preceding key. One does not have to decode every tiny field to establish the relation. The reader has just been given a graphic vocabulary and immediately sees that vocabulary deployed in an actual heraldic image.

[FIGURE 4B — *Kayserliches Wappen*, image 8331530, crop 4–5 clear hatch patterns matching Fig. 4A.]

Later in the volume the book says the colours again. Printed p. 87 begins the *Kurtze und deutliche Beschreibung aller hierinnen vorkommenden Wappen / Nach dem Alphabet*. The colours carried by line patterns on the copperplates reappear as words: *Gold*, *roth*, *schwartz*, *blau*, *silbern*, and related forms recur through the blazons.

[FIGURE 4C — p. 87, image 8331757, crop several tincture words.]

The three panels belong together because no single one contains the whole chromatic apparatus. The key explains marks. The armorial plate deploys them. The prose verbalizes the colours again. The same volume contains a register of 125 genealogical tables, placing the armorial images inside a dynastic reference book. Calling the volume “illustrated” misses this structure. Calling it simply coloured or uncoloured misses it even more completely. A monochrome copy could still carry a large quantity of chromatic information.

The key was not peculiar to 1744. Detailed physical-copy descriptions record a full-page engraved *Erklärung der Farben* in annuals for 1728, 1732, 1737, 1738, 1744, 1751, 1755, and 1757. A separate project control reports such a colour-explanation copper in a physical-copy description for 1725 as well, though that earlier boundary still requires direct image inspection before fine claims are made. A 1745 copy description explicitly identifies the colours as being represented *durch Schraffierung*. This establishes recurrence of the module across the series without proving that one physical copperplate survived unchanged throughout.

A defective 1729 copy makes the colour key’s separateness unusually visible. Its collation lists among the missing material “1 gest. Tafel ‘Erklärung der Farben’,” alongside two missing armorial engravings and other leaves. That is not evidence that the 1729 edition was published without the key. It is a record of copy loss. The difference matters. Many encoded armorial images can survive while the easiest internal route for decoding them has disappeared.

[OPTIONAL FIGURE 4D — copy-collation detail for the missing 1729 *Erklärung der Farben*, if image quality/permissions justify it. Otherwise footnote only.]

The same distinction between image and explanation appears in a near-contemporary bibliographical source concerning a different Weigel publication. Adam Friedrich Glafey’s 1747 catalogue of Eucharius Gottlieb Rinck’s library summarizes Johann David Köhler’s preface to the large Weigel armorial of 1734. According to the catalogue, Köhler regretted that the German explanation of the colours appearing in shields had been neglected in later editions after Siebmacher’s death. More strikingly, the catalogue says that the same explanatory layer was still lacking in the newest Weigel edition prepared with Köhler’s involvement.

The literal 1734 Köhler page has not yet been recovered, so the 1747 catalogue remains a near-contemporary witness to his complaint rather than a substitute for it. But the bibliographical fact is already useful. Within overlapping Nürnberg publication lines, encoded arms and explanatory apparatus could have different histories. A large armorial could retain ways of marking tinctures while losing a German explanation; the annual *Wappenkalender* could externalize colour decoding into a recurrent separate copperplate.

This is precisely the kind of difference that a history of “heraldic hatching” by itself does not capture. The relevant book-historical units are not only the armorial image and its code but also the legend, the prose explanation, and the state of the surviving copy.

## 4. The Colour Key in an Annual Book

The *Wappenkalender* was not simply an armorial whose cover date changed each year. Its prefaces and terminal pages show a publication rhythm in which information was repeatedly revised, printed, corrected, and revised again. The 1728 volume states that its 108 genealogical tables had been reviewed anew and enlarged according to the newest reports — *nach den neuesten Nachrichten vermehret*. Their purpose was practical and current: they were to be used with *zuverlässiger Gewißheit* and *guter Bequemlichkeit* in *täglichen Zeitungs-Lesen*. Because space was limited, the tables concentrated on living high-ranking persons and retained deceased predecessors principally where they remained necessary for understanding the present.

The same volume ends with a separate page headed *Unter währenden Druck eingelauffene Zusätze und Verbesserungen*. The additions and improvements had arrived while printing was underway. Marriages, births, and deaths are attached to specific table or page locations. A fixed table could therefore be outdated before the book was fully produced, and the solution was to append a correction layer rather than remake the entire volume.

[FIGURE 5A — 1728 *Geneigter Leser!*: *alljährigem Vermehrung*, *neuesten Nachrichten*, *täglichen Zeitungs-Lesen*.]  
[FIGURE 5B — 1728 terminal *Unter währenden Druck eingelauffene Zusätze und Verbesserungen*, image 8331489, with one table/page update.]

The 1733 annual repeats the logic even more explicitly. It recalls that the series had been established for the *alljährliche Vermehrung und Verbesserung der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldic*. A *guter Freund* resident in Rome had supplied information about several Italian princely houses. The tables were again revised from the newest and most reliable reports. At the end appears *Unter währenden Druck geschehene Veränderungen*, recording dynastic changes that occurred while printing was underway and directing the reader back to the affected tables.

[FIGURE 5C — 1733 *Geneigter Leser!*: annual improvement / Rome correspondent / newest reports.]  
[FIGURE 5D — 1733 terminal *Unter währenden Druck geschehene Veränderungen*, image 8335352.]

The colour key was a small recurring component inside this changing annual object. Its vocabulary could remain stable while the identities and descriptions of arms, the genealogical tables, and the corrections attached to them changed. This was one practical advantage of a centralized key: new armorial material did not require the entire chromatic code to be explained afresh beside every image.

A retrospective publisher’s preface from 1772 makes the material stakes explicit. Introducing the cumulative *Wappenbuch*, the publisher looked back at the large armorial tradition associated with Siebmacher and described a cluster of defects. Attempts to include too many arms had forced figures into formats too small for sufficient clarity. Older blazoning had not been sufficiently determinate. Attempts to indicate tinctures with letters had caused *Unordnung und Mißverstand*. A large number of images left inadequate room for description. Coats of arms also changed and received augmentations *von Zeit zu Zeit*.

[FIGURE 6A — 1772 *Vorbericht*, crop small/unclear figures.]  
[FIGURE 6B — *Tincturen mit Buchstaben* / *Unordnung und Mißverstand*.]  
[FIGURE 6C — insufficient descriptive space +/or *von Zeit zu Zeit*.]

The *Vorbericht* is retrospective to 1723 and should never be quoted as Köhler’s own original programme. But it gives a publisher-side history of older armorial books in which colour notation belonged to the same practical field as scale, legibility, explanatory prose, and updating. The preface then represented Köhler’s 1723 calendar as an attempt to remedy these inconveniences, at least for princely arms.

The point is not that Nürnberg publishers discovered a modern theory of information. They had a concrete history of what readers could and could not recover from printed heraldic pages. By 1772 letter-coded tinctures could be remembered as a source of misunderstanding. By the late 1720s, and probably earlier, the annuals repeatedly supplied a separate engraved hatching key. The same publications had also learned to patch facts that changed while the book was being printed.

## 5. From Annual Key to Cumulative *Wappenbuch*

The afterlife of the colour key did not end with the last numbered Köhler/Weigel annual in 1757. A directly digitized copy of Johann Friedrich Seyfart’s *Der durchlauchtigen Welt vollständiges Wappenbuch* at Tübingen, catalogued under 1771 with a title-state dated 1772, preserves an especially revealing opening sequence:

*Einband → Spiegel → Vorsatz → Erklärung der Farben → Titelblatt → Vorbericht*.

The colour key stands before the title page. Even as the publication was reorganized into a cumulative *Wappenbuch*, a monochrome decoder remained at the threshold of the armorial apparatus.

[FIGURE 7A — Tübingen Fe 18-1 opening *Erklärung der Farben*, whole page or diagnostic detail.]  
[FIGURE 7B — title page immediately following, showing new cumulative *Wappenbuch* host.]  

The publisher’s preface makes the material genealogy explicit. The new project took *die bisher bey dem Wappencalender gewesenen Wappen zum Grunde gelegt*: the arms previously used in the *Wappenkalender* were made the basis of the cumulative work. Because additional arms could only be gathered gradually, the new divisions would be issued *jährlich mit dem Wappencalender* and eventually accumulated into several octavo volumes.

[FIGURE 7C — *Vorbericht* crop: *die bisher bey dem Wappencalender gewesenen Wappen zum Grunde gelegt* and/or *jährlich mit dem Wappencalender ausgegeben werden*.]

This is direct actor-language for continuity between two publication formats. It does not prove that every plate in the cumulative work was the identical copper formerly used in an annual, and it does not settle the physical matrix history of the colour-key leaf itself. It does prove that the later *Wappenbuch* was deliberately assembled from the existing armorial corpus and distributed through the calendar’s annual rhythm.

The 1771/72 opening therefore provides an important control for the article’s chronology. The *Erklärung der Farben* was not merely a feature of a mid-century calendar that disappeared before Göttingen universal history. A colour-key leaf remained part of the armorial book world in the very years when Gatterer and Schlözer were publishing and arguing about historical tables.

## 6. Gatterer in the Weigel–Köhler Print World

Johann Christoph Gatterer did not encounter the Nürnberg genealogy-heraldry trade only as a distant predecessor to his later Göttingen work. His early career was embedded in it.

In 1755 Gatterer’s *Historia genealogica dominorum Holzschuherorum* was produced in Nürnberg, with Martin Tyroff among the engravers associated with its visual apparatus. The annual *Wappenkalender* was still issued for Christoph Weigel’s heirs. Tyroff himself had been extending inherited heraldic plate stock during the same period. These coincidences should not be turned into a claim that Gatterer designed the calendar’s colour system, but they place him within the same working world of heraldic copperplates and genealogical publication.

Johann David Köhler died in 1755. In the following year Gatterer contributed materially to the posthumous closure of Köhler’s *Wöchentliche Historische Münz-Belustigung*. Modern bibliographic work attributes six numismatic pieces and an unpaginated biographical preface on Köhler to Gatterer in the delayed final material. Johann Tobias Köhler also participated in completion, and the volume should not be described as Gatterer’s single-handed continuation. The point is narrower: before Gatterer took over the genealogy-heraldry handbook, he was already publishing inside the broader Köhler–Weigel serial ecology and framing Köhler’s life and work for print.

Gatterer’s first *Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik* appeared in 1759 under the literal imprint *Im Verlag Christoph Weigels, des ältern, seel. Erben*. It presented itself as *aufs neue ausgefertigt* by Gatterer and retained the familiar pairing of armorial engravings and genealogical tables. The bibliographical succession from the older *Wappenkalender* into this *Handbuch* is secure.

[FIGURE 8A — 1759 *Handbuch* title/opening: Gatterer + Weigel heirs + *aufs neue ausgefertigt*.]  
[FIGURE 8B — HIGH-PRIORITY OPEN CHECK: first leaves/apparatus for *Erklärung der Farben*. If present, compare directly with 1744/1771. If absent, do not fabricate continuity.]

The title-state sequence after 1759 adds further book-historical density. The 1761 *Handbuch* again used *aufs neue ausgefertigt*. The 1762 issue advertised that it had been *ganz neu ausgearbeitet* and enlarged with many genealogical tables and arms. In 1763 the title added an *Abriß der Heraldik oder Wappenkunde*. In 1764 the annual label returned explicitly: *Fortgesetzter Wappen-Calender ... oder jährliches Handbuch der neuesten Genealogie und Heraldik*. By 1766 Gatterer’s *Abriß der Heraldik oder Wappenkunde* was explicitly designed *zum Nutzen der studirenden Jugend* and explained with eight copperplates; contemporary descriptions place the treatise and its plates in the same calendar/handbook environment.

The internal order of Gatterer’s heraldic textbook also matters at a modest level. The 1766 *Abriß* places *Quellen der Wappenkunde* near its beginning, before the long technical sequence on divisions, figures, tinctures, and terminology. In the expanded 1774 edition, a currently secondary transcription attributes to Gatterer a discussion of Rink’s additional signs for *Naturfarbe* and *Eisenfarbe*, evaluating at least part of the notation against old *Wappenbriefe*. That p. 12 passage and its plate figures remain a bounded direct-primary check; until then it should not bear a major claim. But its existence as a targeted source is useful because it places the question of colour signs inside Gatterer’s own heraldic books rather than only in the earlier Weigel apparatus.

[FIGURE 8C — 1766/1774 *Abriß* title or tincture/hatching plate; ideally direct p. 12 + figures 19–20 once closed.]

The bridge is therefore substantial even if the 1759 colour-key check remains unresolved. It consists of people, publishers, titles, plates, and serial continuities. It does not require a perfect line of intellectual influence from Nürnberg heraldry to Göttingen universal history.

## 7. Colouring as Historical Exercise

One further Gatterer text makes the return to Göttingen especially concrete. In the 1771 preface *Von der historischen Erziehung*, Gatterer proposed active exercises for learning genealogy, heraldry, numismatics, and related historical subjects. The surviving modern reconstruction of the passage includes colouring coats of arms, drawing or colouring family trees, cataloguing coins, and making or using coin copies in materials such as plaster, tin, lead, or glass. Direct inspection of the eight-page 1771 first-edition preface remains a bounded task, but the content is securely located in the modern critical edition.

[OPTIONAL FIGURE 8D or FIGURE 9A — 1771 *Von der historischen Erziehung*, direct page once captured, crop around colouring arms/family trees. This may be more useful than a generic *Abriß* plate if figure count must stay at 7–8.]

The passage is important precisely because it should not be made abstract. Gatterer was recommending that learners do things to historical and auxiliary-historical representations: colour a coat of arms, work on a family tree, handle or reproduce a coin. In the same year he also published his *Einleitung in die synchronistische Universalhistorie*. The coexistence is enough. There is no need to claim a unified Gattererian theory of colour. His books and teaching projects placed colour among ordinary practices of historical learning at both heraldic and tabular scales.

This also makes the chronology around Schlözer less isolated. In 1771 Gatterer could recommend colouring arms and genealogical trees as exercises. In 1772 Schlözer warned against colouring *Facta*. In 1773 he could nevertheless defend painted chronological tables for teaching synchronism. In 1775 he proposed illumination of a table to make unequal historical durations visible. These statements do not use colour identically, but all belonged to a scholarly environment in which colour could be a concrete operation on paper.

## 8. Back to Göttingen: What Kind of Coloured Historical Book?

Returning to the Göttingen dispute after Nürnberg changes the scale of the question. Instead of asking what colour “meant” for Gatterer or Schlözer, we can ask what kind of coloured historical object a reader actually received.

The title of Gatterer’s *Synopsis* promised two copper-engraved tables *coloribusque illustratae*. Yet pigment was hand-applied and surviving witnesses differ. The bibliographical category “coloured table” cannot simply be inferred from the edition title and projected uniformly across all copies. A copy may be coloured, uncoloured, or mixed; the line work and layout remain present even where pigment does not.

This sort of copy variation is not itself novel. Histories of hand-coloured scientific and illustrated books have long shown that colouring depended on labour, supervision, recipes, correction, and copy state. The relevant contrast here is between kinds of book-colour. In an armorial, dots and hatchings allow a reader to recover the semantic tincture of a represented shield without pigment. In the *Synopsis*, pigment is applied to a copper-engraved historical table whose referents are not themselves coloured objects. The physical fact of colour on paper is similar; its relation to the represented material is different.

Nürnberg also makes Schlözer’s language less paradoxical without turning it into a theory. The same print culture could distinguish several ordinary acts that all involved colour. One could specify colour in prose. One could embed a letter in a shield. One could convert a tincture into hatching. One could supply a central *Erklärung der Farben*. One could ask a purchaser or colourist to illuminate an impression. One could colour a genealogical tree as an exercise. One could illuminate a historical table. And one could accuse a historian of colouring a fact.

The proximity of these acts should not erase their differences. Schlözer’s 1772 warning concerned historical statements. His 1773 *gemahlte chronologische Tabellen* concerned a proposed didactic sheet in which historical objects became co-present and memorable. His 1775 *Illumination der Tabelle* concerned the visual proportion of historical periods. Gatterer’s *Synopsis* was an actual published table whose surviving copies differ in pigment. Gatterer’s 1771 educational programme treated colouring arms and family trees as practical exercises. The Nürnberg key communicated colour while remaining monochrome.

The Gatterer–Schlözer quarrel belongs here as context, but only in proportion. Schlözer began teaching Universalgeschichte alongside Gatterer. Their programmes were close enough that each could accuse the other of appropriation. Schlözer issued a *Species Facti*; Gatterer replied in a separate forty-eight-page *Antwort* in 1773. Teaching audiences and student fees were involved alongside questions of priority and method. Gatterer’s polemical vocabulary included his *Plan*, *Denkungsart*, *Methode*, and discoveries. None of this proves that colour itself was at issue in the plagiarism quarrel, and the article should not imply that it was.

What the dispute supplies is a setting in which the arrangement and presentation of universal history had become professionally consequential. The article’s book-historical evidence sits beside that dispute rather than secretly replacing it. The historian who worried about the colouring of facts and the historian whose *Synopsis* was sold as *coloribusque illustratae* were working within a world of printed historical aids, tables, armorials, genealogies, and teaching books whose colours had concrete production histories.

The return to Göttingen can therefore remain materially modest. The Nürnberg evidence does not explain the content of Gatterer’s colour scheme or cause Schlözer’s vocabulary. It makes their books stranger in a productive way. A “coloured” historical work no longer looks like an obvious category. Colour might be printed, hand-applied, encoded, named, absent, proposed, or lost from a copy. What survives materially in a particular book has to be established before its chromatic features are made to carry a larger historiographical argument.

---

## 9. Colour in Books

Recent histories of colour printing have substantially expanded the field. Work on eighteenth-century colour printing now reconstructs coloured inks, multi-plate processes, intaglio colour, typography, illustration, labour, and variant impressions. Histories of hand-colouring have established that coloured copies could be supervised, corrected, unevenly finished, or materially divergent. Research on eighteenth-century colour charts has shown how samples, names, recipes, and ordered systems made chromatic comparison possible. Heraldic scholarship, meanwhile, has a much older literature on tricking and hatching conventions and a newer material history of armorial production and use.

This article therefore does not claim that colour in eighteenth-century books has been neglected, that hand-coloured copies vary, or that hatching was an eighteenth-century invention. Its object lies at the overlap of those histories. The Nürnberg *Erklärung der Farben* belongs to a history of colour precisely because it is a colour instrument without coloured ink. It encodes chromatic categories through black marks, sits beside images that require those marks to be read chromatically, and coexists with prose that names the colours again. In a serial book it could recur for decades; in an individual copy it could disappear; in a later cumulative book it could migrate to a new host before the title page.

The distinction can be put plainly: histories of colour printing ask how colour was put onto paper. The books examined here also ask how colour could be carried when colour was not put onto paper.

That question does not end with monochrome hatching. Gatterer’s *Synopsis* reverses it. There the engraved structure can survive even where the hand-applied colour advertised by the title is absent. Nürnberg begins with a black page that still communicates colour; Göttingen supplies a historical book whose colour can disappear while much of its engraved historical structure remains. Between those poles lie colour words, initials, hatchings, legends, prose blazons, colouring instructions, hand-finished impressions, coloured genealogical exercises, and painted historical tables.

The image programme should therefore carry much of the article’s argument. The 1744 *Erklärung der Farben* cannot be paraphrased away because its function is established by visual adjacency to the following armorial plate and by comparison with the later prose description. The mixed or uncoloured state of a *Synopsis* witness is a difference between books, not merely an assertion about them. The 1728 and 1733 correction pages make the annual temporality of the *Wappenkalender* visible in its own typography. The 1771 cumulative *Wappenbuch* physically places the colour key before a new title and then explains in its preface that its arms were taken from the calendar tradition.

For that reason the article’s movement is deliberately short. It begins with a Göttingen dispute in which “colouring” could be both a charge and a proposed technique. It turns back to an older Nürnberg print world in which colour was routinely translated between words, signs, hatchings, legends, prose, and sometimes pigment. It follows the key through annual and cumulative book formats and Gatterer through the publishing ecology that connected them. It then returns to Göttingen, not to prove that Nürnberg caused a historical method, but to restore the material specificity behind the language of coloured history.

A monochrome *Erklärung der Farben* is an apt object with which to end. It does not reproduce gold, silver, red, blue, black, or green perceptually. It provides conventional marks by which those colours can be recovered from a black printed page. That modest device persisted in books that were revised, corrected, incompletely preserved, repackaged, and reused. By the time Göttingen historians were debating what might be coloured in universal history, German historical and auxiliary-historical books had already spent generations separating colour from pigment and putting them back together in different material forms.
